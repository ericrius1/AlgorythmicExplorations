Solar Stack · Part 1

The Tandem Tree

How a perovskite film half a micron thick, laid on top of a silicon cell, gets past silicon's own efficiency limit, and what has to go right before it can sit on a roof for 25 years. The cross-section above is sorting sunlight live.

Scroll down to read ↓

The picture at the top of this page is a solar cell in cross-section, with sunlight falling into it. Each streak is one photon, drawn in its own colour, with its energy picked at random from the sun's spectrum, and each one stops at a depth picked at random from how strongly the layer it's in absorbs that colour. The thin layer on top is a lead-halide perovskite, half a micron thick. It stops most of the blue and green photons. The thick layer under it is an ordinary silicon cell, 160 µm thick and drawn on a log scale so its first few microns aren't crushed, and it catches the red and near-infrared light that gets through. The streaks that fall out the bottom are infrared photons with too little energy for either layer.

Where a photon stops it frees an electron (cyan, rising toward the top contact) and a hole (pink, sinking toward the bottom one). The orange sparks are the photon's energy above the layer's gap, lost as heat in the first picosecond. Every figure on this page works this way: it simulates the particles on your GPU and counts what they do, and the readouts are those counts. The numbers in the text are what the counts settle to.

That stack is a perovskite-on-silicon tandem, and it's the most promising way anyone has found to push solar panels past the limit silicon is stuck under. Lab cells passed 35% in 2026. The best silicon cells are around 27%, and silicon by itself can't go much above 29%. This page works through why the tandem works, using the same physics models that run the figures, and then through the longer list of things that decide whether it becomes a product.

It took a lot of separate work to get here: a century of photovoltaics, sixty years of making silicon surfaces behave, and a crystal family that was first used in a solar cell in 2009. Here's how those branches join up. Click a card to read about it.

Step 1 · One thresholdWhat a single bandgap throws away

A semiconductor has a bandgap: the energy it takes to lift an electron out of its bond and leave it free to move. A photon with less energy than the gap goes straight through. A photon with more energy frees one electron (and leaves behind a hole, the missing electron, which moves too). Whatever energy the photon had above the gap is lost within about a picosecond, as the electron bumps around the lattice and settles to the bottom of the band. That excess becomes heat.

So one bandgap is one threshold, and sunlight is spread over a wide range of energies. A low gap catches lots of photons but keeps only a little of each one. A high gap keeps more of each photon but lets most of them pass.

William Shockley and Hans Queisser turned this into a hard limit in 1961. Their argument is called detailed balance, and the core of it fits in three lines. It's what the next figure computes for every gap you pick: 

jsc = q × (sunlight photons above Eg)            // one electron per absorbed photon
j0  = q × (300 K thermal glow above Eg)          // what a perfect cell must emit back
V   = (kT/q) × ln((jsc − J) / j0 + 1)            // its voltage when it delivers current J

The second line is the subtle one. Anything that absorbs light at some energy also emits light at that energy when it's warm, so even a perfect cell glows faintly from the electrons and holes that find each other and recombine. That glow sets a floor on the dark current j0, and with it a ceiling on the voltage. Multiply current by voltage, find the maximum, and you have the most electrical power a single gap can deliver.

Below is one absorber with a gap you choose, under the same sunlight. Photons below the gap fall straight through it. Photons above it stop, and each one throws off its excess energy as a burst of orange sparks: a blue photon makes a big burst, one just above the gap almost none. The histogram in the corner adds up every photon's energy by what became of it, against the sun's spectrum (dashed). The counts give what passes through, what becomes heat and how much current the absorber makes; the diode equation above then splits the rest between the cell's voltage loss and the part that comes out as electrical power.

Silicon's gap is 1.12 eV. Counted, it keeps 30.3% of the sunlight (the count settles within a few tenths of a percent of the model's answer). 32.4% turns into heat, 22.9% passes through without being absorbed, and the remaining 14.4% is the price of the cell's voltage sitting below its gap. The best single gap, near 1.26 eV here, does only half a point better. The curve is flat on top, and silicon is already near the top of it.

Aside · Which sun

The figures on this page use the simplest sun there is: a 5,778 K blackbody, scaled to 1,000 W/m². Real sunlight at the ground has dips where water, oxygen and carbon dioxide absorb, and the standard test spectrum (called AM1.5G) puts the single-junction limit at 33.7%, near 1.34 eV. The blackbody numbers come out two or three points lower, but the shapes and the trade-offs are the same.

No real silicon cell gets to 30%. Silicon's gap is indirect, so it absorbs weakly, and electrons and holes also recombine by colliding three at a time (Auger recombination). With those included the practical ceiling for silicon is about 29.4%. Research cells have reached the high 27s, and factories ship cells around 25%. That's close to the wall, and the losses left are mostly the two big ones in the figure: heat and light that passes through. Neither can be fixed with a better silicon cell, because both come from having only one gap.

Step 2 · Two thresholdsSplitting the spectrum

The fix has been known since the 1950s: use two gaps. Put a wide-gap absorber on top. It takes the high-energy photons and keeps more of each one, because its higher gap means less excess energy turns into heat. Everything below its gap goes straight through to a narrow-gap cell underneath. Space satellites have flown stacks of three or more gallium arsenide-family cells for decades. Those cells are grown one atomic layer at a time and cost far too much for a roof.

For a tandem on silicon, the question is what gap the top cell should have. Below, two ideal absorbers are stacked: a top cell with the gap you choose, which takes every photon above it, and silicon underneath, which gets what's left above 1.12 eV. The bars are each cell's current, counted photon by photon, and the efficiency comes from those two currents. The curve in the corner is what the pair can do at every top gap.

There are two ways to wire the pair. In a two-terminal stack the cells are in series, so the same current runs through both and the weaker one sets it. That's the solid curve, and it peaks sharply where the two currents are equal: 41.6% at a top gap of 1.76 eV in this model. With AM1.5 sunlight the match comes a little lower, around 1.7 eV, and the limit is about 45%. In a four-terminal stack each cell gets its own wires and runs at its own best point. That's the dashed curve: flatter, and barely higher at its peak.

At 1.68 eV, the gap I used for the hero and for the rest of this page, the perovskite takes 21.9 mA/cm² and the silicon gets 18.1. In series the stack runs at 18.1, so the top cell's extra current is wasted (the dashed part of its bar). Real cells use gaps around 1.68 eV anyway, for a reason the next section gets to: materials with wider gaps tend not to stay stable under light.

Most of the industry is betting on two terminals. A two-terminal tandem has the same two wires as an ordinary cell, so it drops into a normal module and a normal inverter. A four-terminal design needs a second set of transparent electrodes, twice the wiring, and electronics that handle two cells separately, which costs more than the efficiency it gains back. The catch with two terminals is that the current match has to hold all day, as the sun's spectrum shifts toward the red in the morning and evening.

Step 3 · The bottom cellSixty years of silicon surfaces

Bell Labs showed the first practical silicon solar cell in 1954, at about 6%. For two decades it was a space technology, because a satellite would pay almost any price for power. Cheap terrestrial silicon came later, from many small improvements that added up: bigger and purer crystals, thinner wafers, better saws, screen-printed contacts and very large factories.

Aside · Vanguard 1

The first solar-powered satellite, Vanguard 1, went up in March 1958 with six small silicon cells. Its radio ran until 1964. The satellite is still in orbit, and it's the oldest human-made object up there.

Inside the cell, most of the progress since the 1980s has been about surfaces. The middle of a silicon wafer is a nearly perfect crystal, and an electron can wander in it for a millisecond or more before it recombines. The surface is where the crystal ends, and every silicon atom there has a broken bond. Those broken bonds give electrons and holes a place to meet and recombine, and the worst surface of all is where metal touches silicon. Passivation is the name for everything that stops this: films that tie up the broken bonds, charged layers that push one carrier type away, and contacts that let current through without the metal touching the wafer.

Each generation of factory cell is mostly a better answer to that one problem. The figure is a piece of wafer in real units: 160 µm thick, a little over a millimetre across, with two silver fingers on top. Each blue dot is a carrier freed by sunlight, at a depth drawn from silicon's absorption, and it random-walks with silicon's diffusion constant until it recombines. In the bulk that happens at the wafer's own rate. At a surface, each bounce off the face has a small chance of being the last, set by that stretch of surface's recombination velocity: green for passivated, red for bare or metal. A red flash is a carrier lost at a surface, violet one lost in the bulk.

Nothing is collected here, which is how a cell sits at its open-circuit voltage. Carriers pile up until they recombine as fast as light makes them, and the figure measures how long one lasts on average. That lifetime is what sets the voltage: the longer carriers last, the more of them there are, and the voltage is (kT/q)·ln(Δn·(N + Δn)/ni²), with Δn the extra carriers and N the doping. Pick each generation in turn and the counted voltages come out 0.64, 0.68, 0.71 and 0.74 V. Aluminium back-surface-field cells alloyed the whole rear with aluminium. PERC covered the rear with a thin dielectric and let the metal through small openings. TOPCon covered the rear with a 1.5 nm oxide and a layer of doped polysilicon, which passivates the whole surface and still conducts. Heterojunction (HJT) cells cover both faces with a few nanometres of amorphous silicon, so the metal never touches the wafer at all. Typical open-circuit voltages went from about 0.64 V to 0.74 V along the way, the same climb as in the figure, and almost all of it is less recombination at the surfaces. By HJT, carriers last about a millisecond, and half of the ones lost are lost inside the wafer, partly to Auger recombination, which no surface can fix.

Most record tandems sit on heterojunction bottom cells. Their high voltage adds straight to the stack's, and the transparent conductor already on top of an HJT cell can double as the layer that joins it to the perovskite. The cost is temperature. Amorphous silicon starts to lose its passivation above roughly 200 °C, so everything built on top of it has to be made cooler than that. That rules out most of the high-temperature processes semiconductor factories normally use, and it's one reason the top cell has to be a material you can make at low temperature.

Step 4 · The top cellA crystal you can tune

"Perovskite" is a crystal structure, not one material. The solar ones have the formula ABX₃. B is lead, X is a halide (iodide, bromide or a mix), and A is a small cation sitting in the gaps: methylammonium, formamidinium, caesium, or a blend.

Aside · 1839, twice

The mineral perovskite, calcium titanate, was found in the Ural Mountains in 1839 and named after the Russian mineralogist Lev Perovski. That's the same year Edmond Becquerel first saw light make a current, the first card on the tree above.

The lead-halide versions entered photovoltaics through dye-sensitized cells, which use a liquid electrolyte. In 2009 Akihiro Kojima, Tsutomu Miyasaka and colleagues used methylammonium lead iodide as the dye and got 3.8%, and the electrolyte dissolved the perovskite quickly. In 2012 two groups replaced the liquid with a solid hole conductor and reached about 10%. One of them found the perovskite carried electrons well enough by itself that it didn't need the porous titanium dioxide the dye cells used to carry them. It was a thin-film semiconductor in its own right, and single perovskite cells climbed past 25% within a decade.

Three properties make it a good top cell. It absorbs strongly, so half a micron of it takes nearly all the light above its gap, where silicon would need hundreds of microns. It can be made from solution or vapour at around 100 °C, well under the bottom cell's limit. And its gap can be tuned by swapping iodide for bromide: about 1.55 eV with no bromide, around 1.68 eV with a quarter of the iodide replaced, and about 2.3 eV with all bromide. The exact numbers depend on the cation mix. The readout on the last figure converts each gap to a rough bromide fraction.

Aside · When light un-mixes the crystal

In 2015 Eric Hoke and colleagues at Stanford found that light pulls mixed iodide–bromide perovskites apart. Under a lamp the film separates into iodide-rich and bromide-rich patches within minutes, and the iodide-rich patches have a lower gap that pulls the cell's voltage down with them. In the dark the halides mix again. More bromide makes it worse, which is why tandem top cells stay near 1.68 eV instead of the gap the current match would like, and why so much of the chemistry since then (caesium, mixed cations, additives) is about holding the mix together.

A working top cell is a stack of its own, made of layers mostly thinner than the wavelength of the light going through them. This is roughly the structure of recent record cells, with light coming in from the top. Pick a layer to see what it does and what it costs.

Twelve layers, and everything except the wafer adds up to about a micron. Every layer above the perovskite absorbs a little of the light meant for the cells, and every interface between layers is a place where carriers can recombine. Most of the last decade of tandem research has gone into the contact layers on either side of the perovskite.

Step 5 · ContactsGetting carriers out

Absorbing a photon only makes an electron and a hole. They then have to leave through opposite sides: electrons through the C₆₀ layer above the perovskite, holes through the monolayer below it. Each contact has to let one kind of carrier through and block the other, and its energy level has to line up with the perovskite's band.

Lining up matters in both directions. If the hole contact's level sits above the perovskite's valence band, holes drop into it and the energy they drop by is lost from the cell's voltage. If it sits below, holes have to climb a step to get out. Only a share of them, falling off as e−step/kT, make it over, and the rest pile up and recombine.

The figure is that band diagram in 3D, with height as a hole's energy. Every hole the light frees walks across the perovskite's band toward the contact. It takes a step up only with the Boltzmann chance of having that much thermal energy at that moment (the same rule the ion channels follow in How Neurons Signal), and a drop it always falls down, giving up the energy. Holes that wait recombine, and more of them do where they crowd. The curve in the corner is the simple formula for the same thing; the dots are what the holes did at each level you've tried.

A drop costs voltage one for one: at +0.20 eV the top cell's open-circuit voltage falls from 1.25 V to 1.05 V. A step costs almost nothing until it's a few kT high (kT is 26 meV at room temperature), then cuts the current off fast. By −0.2 eV the top cell keeps less than a third of its power.

That's why a single layer of molecules made such a difference. In 2020 a team at Helmholtz-Zentrum Berlin used a self-assembled monolayer called Me-4PACz as the hole contact. Its molecules bond to the transparent conductor at one end, and the dipole along each molecule shifts the contact level to line up with the perovskite. Being one molecule thick, it absorbs almost nothing, and it leaves very few defects at the interface. Their tandem reached 29.15%, a record at the time, and monolayer contacts are now in nearly every record cell. The electron side is the bigger loss today. In many record cells the interface between the perovskite and C₆₀ is the largest single source of lost voltage, which is what the nanometre of LiF in the layer figure is there to reduce.

Step 6 · SeriesTwo cells, one current

Now put real losses into the two-terminal stack. Each subcell gets a diode equation with the same detailed-balance j0 as Step 1, multiplied up to account for non-radiative recombination, which lowers the voltage by a fixed amount. The bottom cell loses 130 mV, about what a good heterojunction cell does. The stack's curve is then one sum: 

V_stack(J) = V_top(J) + V_bottom(J)      // same current, voltages add
P = max over J of  J × V_stack(J)

If one cell is asked for more current than it makes, it gets driven into reverse bias, and only its shunt resistance lets the extra through. That's why the stack's curve (white) ends at the smaller of the two subcell currents. The perovskite's thickness sets how the light is split: a thicker film takes more of the photons near its gap and leaves less for the silicon.

In the figure the split is counted: photons fall into a real perovskite film over a light-trapping silicon wafer, and each cell's current is the share it catches. Then watch the junction between the cells. Every hole coming down out of the perovskite has to meet an electron coming up out of the silicon there, and they recombine in pairs; that is how one current passes through both. When one cell makes more, some of its carriers find no partner, wait at the junction and recombine. That's the flickering pink band, and the readout counts what share of carriers it takes.

At the default 500 nm the perovskite catches about 20.9 mA/cm² and the silicon only 18.8, so the stack runs at the silicon's current: 1.97 V open-circuit, 32.6%, and about one carrier in twenty waits at the junction for a partner that never comes. Thin the film to about 330 nm and the currents nearly match (20.1 and 19.7), the pink band all but empties, and the stack gets to 33.5%. A mismatch of 2 mA/cm² costs less than a point here, because the cell with spare current runs closer to its open-circuit voltage and makes up some of the difference. Voltage losses are worse. Every 100 mV the top cell loses comes straight off the stack, and raising the series resistance from 2 to 8 Ω·cm² takes the fill factor from 88% to 82%.

That lands within a couple of points of the real records, which passed 35% in 2026 under the standard spectrum. The model leaves out light that never reaches either absorber: reflection, the silver grid's shadow, and parasitic absorption in the contact layers. In a real cell those cost a few mA/cm² between them.

Step 7 · TextureCoating a field of pyramids

Silicon cells aren't flat. Etching a wafer in hot alkaline solution exposes the crystal's {111} planes and leaves the surface covered in random pyramids a few microns tall, with faces at 54.7° to the wafer. Light that hits a face either goes in or bounces off sideways into the next pyramid for a second try, and the light that gets in travels through the wafer at an angle, which lengthens its path. On bare silicon, texture cuts reflection from about a third of the light to about a tenth.

The perovskite, though, is half a micron thick, and the pyramids are several times that. The early tandems were made by spin coating, where a drop of solution spreads across the wafer and dries. A liquid fills the valleys and levels out, so on a textured wafer the film ends up thick in the valleys and missing from the tips. Wherever a tip pokes through, the top contact lands right on the silicon, and that spot becomes a short circuit (a shunt) between the two cells.

The figure is a patch of textured wafer 20 µm across, under sunlight. Each photon meets a facet with silicon's real reflectance, and one that bounces off flies on to the next pyramid for another try, so the readout counts how many get away: about 34% from a flat wafer (the textbook figure is 35%) and about 11% from pyramids of any size. The film is modelled as what a liquid does, filling the valleys up to the height that holds its volume. With 2 µm pyramids and a 600 nm solution film it settles about 1.3 µm deep, and the tallest tips poke through: about 8% of the surface is left bare, and every bare tip is a shunt. At 4 µm, nearly a third is. Making the film thicker helps slowly, because it mostly fills the valleys. The first tandems avoided the problem by polishing the front of the wafer flat, and lost the optical benefit. In 2018 a group at EPFL and CSEM evaporated the inorganic part of the perovskite first, which coats every facet evenly, and then converted it with solution. That's the "conformal" method in the figure. Their fully textured tandem reached 25.2%. Many record cells since then have used textured wafers too, usually with smaller pyramids than standard silicon cells.

Step 8 · ScaleFrom one square centimetre to a wafer

Record efficiencies are usually measured on cells about one square centimetre in size. A factory cell is a whole wafer: 18.2 cm on a side in the current M10 format, 331 cm². That isn't only a harder coating job. It changes the statistics of defects.

Suppose the coating leaves killer defects (pinholes, particles, spots where the film didn't form) scattered at random, D of them per square centimetre on average. A cell of area A is good only if it has none, and for randomly placed defects the chance of that is 

yield = exp(−D × A)      // Poisson: the chance of zero defects in area A

The figure cuts one M10 wafer into cells of whatever size you choose and scatters defects on it from a fixed set of random positions.

At 0.02 killer defects per square centimetre, 98% of 1 cm² cells come out fine. On a full wafer, 0.1% do. A process that looks excellent in the lab can be useless at full size without any change in the chemistry. For a factory to get 95% of its full-wafer cells clean, D has to be below about 0.00015 per cm²: one killer defect in roughly twenty wafers.

Large cells lose efficiency for other reasons too: the film is never perfectly even across 18 cm, current has to travel further through the transparent conductor, and the edges take up a smaller share of a small cell. The best large-area results show the size of the gap. In June 2025 LONGi reported a certified 33% on a 260.9 cm² tandem cell, a few points below its small-cell records. Modules lose more again, to the gaps between cells, the wiring, and the glass. Oxford PV's 60-cell module reached 26.9% in 2024, and the modules it started shipping that year were rated at 24.5%.

Step 9 · LifetimeEfficiency has to last

Silicon modules come with 25- or 30-year warranties, and field studies find median losses of around half a percent of their power per year. Perovskites are much more fragile. Moisture breaks them down, heat drives the organic cations out, and light and electric fields move halide ions through the crystal and into the contacts. Sealing the module in glass with tight edge seals keeps the water out, but the heat, light and ions are part of normal operation.

The standard qualification tests (IEC 61215) were written for silicon. The best known is damp heat: 1,000 hours at 85 °C and 85% relative humidity. Perovskite modules can now pass it, but the tests don't cover everything that matters for them, such as long periods under light at the maximum power point, which is where ion motion does its damage. Outdoor data is only starting to build up.

The figure compares a tandem module against a 22% silicon module losing 0.5% of its starting power per year, both declining in a straight line the way warranties are written. The total energy over 25 years is the area under each line.

A 26% tandem that loses 1.5% per year falls behind the silicon module in year 14 and makes only 2.4% more energy over 25 years, despite starting 18% ahead. At the same degradation rate as silicon it makes 18% more. Every tenth of a percent per year matters, and it takes years in the field to show what the real rate is.

Lead is part of the reliability question too. A 500 nm perovskite film holds roughly 0.7 grams of lead per square metre, a little over a gram per panel. That's a small amount, but a cracked module in the rain can leak some of it, so encapsulation and recycling plans have to account for it.

Step 10 · CostWhere the extra watts pay

Silicon modules are very cheap now, and getting cheaper. A tandem has to pay for its extra layers out of the extra watts it makes from the same area. It also saves money elsewhere: racking, land, cables and installation labour are paid per square metre, so a module that makes more power per square metre needs less of all of them for each watt.

The figure does that arithmetic. The silicon module is fixed at 22% and $0.20 per watt, about $44 per square metre. You choose the tandem's efficiency, the extra cost of its top cell per square metre, and the area-related cost of the system. Costs paid per watt, like inverters, are the same for both and are left out. The defaults are only assumptions. Real prices move a lot by year and by market.

With $70 per square metre of area cost, a 26% tandem breaks even if its top cell adds no more than $21 per square metre; at $30 it loses. Push the area cost up to $150, more like a rooftop, and the same tandem wins at $30. The lower chart shows the whole trade: for each area cost, the most the top cell can add before the tandem stops paying for itself. The case is easiest where area is expensive, on roofs and crowded sites. Oxford PV's first shipment did go to a utility-scale project in the US, but there, with cheap land, the top cell has to be cheap too.

EpilogueWhat carries over

The tandem is specific to solar cells, but a few lessons from it travel:

  • Know which limit you're under. Silicon cells got better for sixty years by fixing losses inside the cell. The two biggest losses left come from having one bandgap, and only a second gap removes them.
  • Series connections punish imbalance. In a two-terminal stack the weaker cell sets the current, so the best design is the matched pair, not the best single top cell.
  • Interfaces are most of the work. From silicon's passivated contacts to the perovskite's monolayer, most of the gains came from what happens where two materials meet.
  • Scale changes the problem. Defect yield falls off exponentially with area, and lifetime only shows up after years in the field. Neither appears in a record on a one-centimetre cell.

Things I'd try next: swap the blackbody for the tabulated AM1.5G spectrum, so the figures' numbers match the published limits; add an optical model of the stack (thin-film interference and parasitic absorption) to explain the gap between the J–V figure and the records; and compute the current match over a whole day of shifting sunlight, which is the real test for a two-terminal stack.

That's the end of Solar Stack for now. The tandem at the top of the page is still sorting photons: roughly three in ten stop in the perovskite, a quarter reach the silicon, and the rest are infrared that neither layer can use.
