Solar Stack · Part 1

The Tandem Tree

How a perovskite film half a micron thick, stacked on top of a silicon wafer, gets past silicon's own efficiency ceiling, and everything that still stands between a lab record and a 25-year roof.

Scroll down to read ↓

Ordinary silicon solar panels are getting close to the ceiling physics allows. The best research silicon cells sit around 27% efficiency, and even an idealized silicon wafer runs into a hard wall near 29.4%. Yet in 2026, lab cells blew past 35% by laying a translucent crystal film just half a micron thick directly on top of a regular silicon cell.

That two-layer device is a perovskite-on-silicon tandem, and the cross-section overhead is sorting sunlight through one right now. Every streak falling in is a simulated photon drawn at random from the solar spectrum and colored by its wavelength; how deep it penetrates before stopping comes straight from Beer–Lambert absorption in each material. The thin top layer of lead-halide perovskite grabs most of the blue and green light. The 160 µm silicon wafer underneath (drawn on a log depth scale so the top few microns don't disappear into a hairline) catches the red and near-infrared photons that slip through, while the lowest-energy infrared streaks fall straight out the bottom.

Wherever a photon gets absorbed, it frees an electron (cyan, drifting up toward the top contact) and a hole (pink, heading downward). The orange burst at the impact point is the photon's leftover energy above the material's bandgap, dumped into the crystal lattice as heat within a picosecond. All the GPU figures down this page actually simulate these photons and carriers and tally what happens to them, so the readouts come from live particle counts checked against the analytical equations.

Getting a real tandem working required stitching together three separate histories: a century of photovoltaic physics, sixty years of obsessing over silicon's surface defects, and a crystal family nobody tried in a solar cell until 2009. You can pan around the timeline below and click any milestone to see how those threads finally collided.

Step 1 · Single junctionWhat one bandgap throws away

Every semiconductor has a bandgap: the minimum jolt of energy needed to kick an electron loose from its atomic bond so it can carry current (leaving behind a positively charged hole that moves too). If an incoming photon carries less energy than that gap, the crystal is effectively transparent to it and the photon sails right through. If a photon carries more energy than the gap, it still frees just one electron, and all the spare energy above the threshold shakes out into the crystal as heat in roughly a picosecond as the electron relaxes down to the band edge.

That creates an awkward compromise the moment you put a single semiconductor under sunlight, because sunlight spans a broad spread of photon energies. Pick a narrow bandgap and you catch almost every photon, wasting huge chunks of the blue ones as heat. Pick a wide bandgap and you preserve more voltage from every photon you catch, while letting half the spectrum pass untouched.

In 1961 William Shockley and Hans Queisser turned that compromise into a famous efficiency ceiling using an argument called detailed balance. Stripped down to its essentials, the math fits in three lines, and it's what runs inside the figure below for whatever bandgap you dial in: 

jsc = q × (sunlight photons above Eg)            // one electron per absorbed photon
j0  = q × (300 K thermal glow above Eg)          // what an ideal cell must radiate back
V   = (kT/q) × ln((jsc − J) / j0 + 1)            // output voltage at current J

That middle line catches people off guard the first time they see it. By thermodynamics, any material that absorbs light at a given energy must also emit light at that energy when warm. Even a flawless solar cell at room temperature glows faintly in the infrared as electrons and holes bump into each other and recombine radiatively. That unavoidable glow sets a floor on the dark saturation current j0, which caps how high the cell's voltage can climb. Multiply current by voltage, find the peak of that curve, and you have the maximum electrical power any single-gap absorber can possibly extract.

Slide the bandgap below to watch that trade-off happen photon by photon. Photons below the threshold pass straight out the bottom; photons above it stop and kick off bursts of orange heat proportional to how far above the gap they sit (a blue photon throws off a big flash, while one right near the threshold barely sparks). The histogram in the bottom right sorts the incoming solar power by fate against the dashed solar spectrum, tallying transmission, heat, and current directly from the particles, while the diode equation splits the remaining voltage loss from usable electrical power.

At silicon's 1.12 eV bandgap, the particle tally settles right around 30.3% electrical power (within a few tenths of a percent of the analytical model). Almost a third of the sunlight (32.4%) goes straight to heat, 22.9% passes completely unabsorbed, and the last 14.4% disappears to the fundamental gap between photon energy and operating voltage. Moving to the mathematical sweet spot near 1.26 eV barely buys half a percentage point. The peak is broad and flat, and silicon already landed right near the top of it.

Aside · Which sun is in these figures?

To keep the photon sampling clean, these figures model the sun as a 5,778 K blackbody scaled to 1,000 W/m². Real sunlight reaching the ground has bite marks chewed out of it by atmospheric water vapor, oxygen, and CO₂; under that standard terrestrial test spectrum (AM1.5G), the single-junction limit lands a bit higher, at 33.7% near 1.34 eV. Using a smooth blackbody shifts the raw numbers down by two or three points while keeping all the physics and trade-offs intact.

Even 30% is out of reach for real silicon. Because silicon has an indirect bandgap, it absorbs light relatively weakly, and at high carrier densities electrons and holes start recombining in three-body collisions (Auger recombination). Factor those in and silicon's practical ceiling drops to about 29.4%. Today's best lab silicon cells are already pushing the high 27s and mass-produced factory panels hover around 25%. Almost all the remaining room for improvement sits inside the two giant orange and slate chunks of the histogram (heat and unabsorbed light), and you can't engineer your way out of either as long as you only have one bandgap.

Step 2 · Two bandgapsSplitting the spectrum

People noticed the obvious way around Shockley–Queisser back in the 1950s: stack two absorbers with different bandgaps. Put a wide-gap material on top to intercept high-energy blue and green photons and harvest them at a higher voltage (throwing away far less as heat), while letting lower-energy red and infrared photons pass untouched to a narrow-gap cell underneath. Satellites have flown triple-junction gallium arsenide stacks like this for decades, grown one atomic layer at a time in slow epitaxial reactors. That works fine for space, and is wildly too expensive for a suburban roof.

If the bottom cell is fixed as silicon (1.12 eV), the first design question is what bandgap to aim for on top. Below, an adjustable ideal absorber sits directly over silicon. The two bars at top left tally each layer's current straight from the simulated photons, and the inset curve at bottom right maps out total efficiency across every top bandgap.

Notice there are two curves in that corner plot, corresponding to two ways of wiring the stack. In a two-terminal tandem (solid curve), the two cells are stacked electrically in series like batteries in a flashlight: the same current has to flow through both, so whichever cell generates less current bottlenecks the pair. Efficiency climbs to a sharp tent peak right where the currents match: 41.6% at a 1.76 eV top gap under the blackbody sun (or roughly 45% near 1.70 eV under AM1.5G). In a four-terminal stack (dashed curve), each cell has its own independent pair of wires and runs at its own maximum power point, which flattens out the penalty for mismatch while barely raising the peak.

Right at 1.68 eV (the gap used in the hero and the rest of this page), the top layer produces 21.9 mA/cm² while silicon below gets 18.1 mA/cm². Wired in series, the whole stack is throttled to 18.1 mA/cm², leaving the dashed chunk at the end of the green bar stranded. Real tandems still cluster near 1.68 eV anyway because pushing halide perovskites much wider makes their crystal chemistry start to separate under bright light, as Step 4 shows.

Even with that tighter current-matching constraint, almost the entire industry is chasing two-terminal tandems. A two-terminal cell plugs into existing module assembly lines, junction boxes, and inverters just like regular silicon. Four-terminal stacks require extra transparent conducting layers, twice the wiring, and separate power electronics, and that extra hardware usually costs more than the extra efficiency pays back. The catch is that a two-terminal stack needs its currents balanced not just under a noon lab lamp, but across redder morning and evening skies too.

Step 3 · The bottom cellSixty years of silicon surfaces

Bell Labs demonstrated the first usable silicon solar cell in 1954 at around 6% efficiency. For the next twenty years silicon mostly flew on spacecraft, where watts per kilogram mattered and dollars per watt barely registered. Bringing the price down for Earth took decades of unglamorous engineering: purer ingots, thinner diamond-wire slicing, screen-printed silver fingers, and gigawatt-scale factories.

Aside · Vanguard 1

Vanguard 1 launched in March 1958 carrying six tiny silicon solar cells, the first satellite ever powered by sunlight. Its radio kept beeping until 1964, and the little aluminum sphere is still circling Earth today, the oldest human-made object left in orbit.

Inside the wafer itself, most of the efficiency gains since the 1980s have come down to fixing surfaces. The interior of a modern silicon wafer is an astonishingly clean crystal where a free electron can wander around for a full millisecond before recombining. At the top and bottom faces, though, the crystal lattice abruptly ends, leaving rows of unsatisfied dangling bonds. Those surface defects act like traps where electrons and holes cancel out, and slapping bare metal straight onto silicon makes the trap density vastly worse. Passivation is the catch-all term for taming those faces: thin insulating films that cap dangling bonds, built-in electric fields that repel one type of carrier, and clever contacts that extract current without letting metal touch bare silicon.

Every major generation of commercial silicon cell was basically a better strategy for keeping carriers away from lethal surfaces. Below is a slice of wafer to scale (160 µm thick and a bit over a millimeter wide, with two silver contact fingers across the top). Each blue dot is a photogenerated carrier diffusing randomly through silicon until it dies, either inside the bulk (violet flash) or on a bounce off a surface (red flash), with green regions well-passivated and red regions acting as recombination sinks.

With no external load connected, the wafer sits at its open-circuit voltage: carriers accumulate until recombination balances sunlight generation, and the simulation measures the average carrier lifetime directly from the death rate. Longer lifetimes pack more carriers into the wafer, pushing the voltage up according to (kT/q)·ln(Δn·(N + Δn)/ni²). Click through the four generations and watch the counted open-circuit voltage climb from 0.64 V to 0.68 V, 0.71 V, and 0.74 V, closely mirroring real factory history.

Old aluminum back-surface-field (Al-BSF) cells simply baked aluminum across the entire rear face: cheap and rugged, with carriers dying rapidly on the back metal. PERC coated the rear in passivating aluminum oxide and poked tiny laser openings for the metal contacts. TOPCon went further, sandwiching a 1.5 nm tunnel oxide and doped polysilicon across the whole rear so electrons can tunnel out without bare metal ever seeing the wafer. Heterojunction (HJT) cells blanket both front and back with nanometers of amorphous silicon, keeping metal off the crystal everywhere; by HJT, carriers survive roughly a millisecond and half of the remaining deaths happen deep inside the bulk (including unavoidable Auger recombination), leaving little more for surface passivation to save.

That high voltage is why most record tandems sit on heterojunction silicon bottom cells. Every millivolt the bottom cell adds goes straight into the tandem's total, and the transparent conducting oxide already coating an HJT cell doubles cleanly as the electrical bridge to the top cell. The catch is heat. Above roughly 200 °C, hydrogen starts baking out of those thin amorphous silicon layers and ruins their passivation, which means whatever top cell you build on top of silicon has to be processed cool.

Step 4 · The top cellA crystal you can tune

"Perovskite" names a crystal lattice geometry rather than one specific compound. The solar versions share the formula ABX₃: B is lead, X is a halide (iodide, bromide, or a mixture), and A is a small positively charged ion tucked inside the lattice cages (usually methylammonium, formamidinium, cesium, or a carefully balanced recipe of all three).

Aside · Two discoveries in 1839

The original perovskite mineral, calcium titanate, was dug up in the Ural Mountains in 1839 and named after Russian mineralogist Lev Perovski. By pure coincidence, 1839 was the exact same year nineteen-year-old Edmond Becquerel first observed light generating an electric current, the very first card on the tree at the top of this page.

Lead-halide perovskites stumbled into solar research through the side door of dye-sensitized cells. In 2009 Akihiro Kojima, Tsutomu Miyasaka, and collaborators tried methylammonium lead iodide as a light-absorbing dye in a liquid electrolyte cell and hit 3.8% efficiency before the liquid quickly dissolved the crystal. Three years later, two groups swapped the liquid out for a solid hole-transport layer and jumped straight to ~10%, discovering along the way that the perovskite didn't even need the porous TiO₂ scaffold to move electrons; it was a surprisingly capable thin-film semiconductor all by itself. Within a single decade, standalone perovskite cells rocketed past 25%.

Three traits make lead-halide perovskites almost uncannily suited to sitting on top of silicon. First, they have a direct bandgap and absorb light fiercely, so a film just 500 nanometers thick soaks up nearly everything above its threshold (where silicon needs a hundred microns or more). Second, you can deposit and crystallize them from liquid ink or vapor around 100 °C, comfortably below HJT silicon's 200 °C ceiling. Third, you can dial their bandgap smoothly by trading iodide for bromide: pure iodide sits near 1.55 eV, replacing roughly a quarter of the iodide with bromide lifts it to 1.68 eV, and pure bromide pushes all the way to ~2.3 eV.

Aside · When sunlight un-mixes the crystal

In 2015 Eric Hoke and colleagues at Stanford noticed a nasty quirk in mixed iodide–bromide perovskites: shine steady light on the film and within minutes the halides migrate apart into iodide-rich and bromide-rich islands. Because the iodide-rich pockets have a narrower bandgap, carriers funnel into them and drag the entire cell's voltage down. Put the film back in the dark and the ions slowly remix. The effect worsens sharply as you add more bromide, which is why tandem top cells generally park near 1.68 eV rather than the 1.76 eV ideal, and why huge stretches of perovskite chemistry (cesium alloying, triple cations, passivation additives) are spent locking those halides in place.

Of course, a working tandem contains far more than just perovskite and silicon. The whole device is a sandwich of ultra-thin functional films, most of them thinner than a single wavelength of visible light. Click or slide through the twelve layers of a modern record-style cell below to see what each sliver does and what trouble it introduces.

Put the whole stack on a linear ruler (the bar at bottom right) and everything besides the silicon wafer collapses into a 0.7% sliver, barely one micron total on top of 160 microns of silicon. Yet every layer above the perovskite steals a small fraction of incoming photons before they can generate power, and every boundary between layers is a fresh opportunity for electrons and holes to recombine. Over the past decade, most of the real leaps in tandem efficiency have happened right at the contact layers flanking the perovskite.

Step 5 · Energy alignmentGetting carriers out

Once a photon creates an electron–hole pair inside the perovskite, you have to escort them out opposite doors before they find each other again: electrons upward into the C₆₀ fullerene layer, holes downward through the monolayer toward the bottom cell. Each contact layer has to be selective (welcoming one carrier while turning the other away) and its energy level has to match the perovskite's band edge cleanly.

Miss that alignment in either direction and you pay immediately. If the hole contact sits too high in energy relative to the perovskite's valence band, holes waterfall straight down into it and surrender the energy difference directly out of your cell's voltage. If the contact sits too low, holes hit an uphill cliff; only the thermal tail with enough kinetic energy (falling off exponentially as e−step/kT) hops over, while the rest jam up at the boundary and recombine.

The 3D simulation below turns that textbook band diagram into a live carrier-transport game, where height represents a hole's energy. Photogenerated holes drift across the perovskite sheet toward the monolayer contact. At a barrier step, each hole crosses with the exact Boltzmann probability of carrying enough thermal energy (the same physics governing ion crossings in How Neurons Signal); at a downward drop, every hole falls right over and loses that voltage. Holes stuck waiting in traffic recombine faster the denser the crowd gets, and the corner plot compares the counted power kept at each level you test against the analytical curve.

Drag the contact level both ways to feel how lopsided the penalty is. On the drop side (+0.20 eV), voltage bleeds off one-for-one: open-circuit voltage falls straight from 1.25 V to 1.05 V. On the barrier side, thermal energy (kT ≈ 26 meV at room temperature) lets holes shrug off a tiny step, and then the cliff hits like a brick wall: by −0.20 eV, the top cell loses over two-thirds of its power.

This sensitivity explains why switching to a single-molecule contact layer in 2020 made such a big difference. A group at Helmholtz-Zentrum Berlin anchored a self-assembled monolayer (SAM) called Me-4PACz onto the transparent conducting oxide underneath the perovskite. Each molecule has an internal electric dipole that nudges the contact's energy level straight into alignment with the perovskite valence band; being literally one molecule (~1.5 nm) thick, it steals virtually zero light and leaves an unusually clean interface. That cell hit 29.15%, and self-assembled monolayers quickly became standard equipment across almost every record tandem. Today the bigger voltage leak has shifted to the opposite side (the perovskite/C₆₀ electron interface), which is why record stacks often slip a 1 nm buffer of LiF right between them.

Step 6 · Series matchingTwo cells, one current

Next come realistic voltage losses and resistance in a two-terminal stack. Each subcell obeys the same detailed-balance diode equation from Step 1, except its dark current j0 is scaled upward for non-radiative recombination (which trims a fixed chunk off its voltage: here 130 mV for a good HJT silicon bottom cell, and an adjustable loss for the perovskite). Because the two subcells share one current in series, the combined J–V curve is just the vertical sum of their voltages at each current: 

V_stack(J) = V_top(J) + V_bottom(J)      // shared current, voltages add
P = max over J of  J × V_stack(J)

Try pulling more current than the weaker cell produces and that subcell gets pushed into reverse bias, capping the stack's white J–V curve at the smaller subcell's short-circuit current. In practice, engineers fine-tune the split by adjusting the perovskite's thickness: a thicker film absorbs more photons right near its band edge, starving the silicon slightly, while a thinner film lets more red light leak down to boost the bottom cell.

Below, photons drop into a real finite-thickness perovskite film over a light-trapping silicon wafer so both currents come directly from counted absorptions. Watch the recombination junction separating the two layers: to pass one continuous current through a monolithic tandem, every hole sinking out of the perovskite has to meet and annihilate an electron rising out of the silicon in exact one-to-one pairs. Whenever one layer produces excess carriers, the leftovers have no partner to meet, so they pile up in that glowing pink band along the junction and go to waste.

At the starting 500 nm thickness, the perovskite harvests roughly 20.9 mA/cm² while silicon only gets 18.8 mA/cm², throttling the stack to 32.7% (1.97 V open-circuit) with around 5–6% of carriers stranded partnerless at the junction. Thin the perovskite down near 330 nm and the two currents draw nearly even (20.1 and 19.7 mA/cm²): the glowing backlog at the junction clears out and efficiency bumps up to ~33.5%. Notice that a modest 2 mA/cm² mismatch actually costs less than a full percentage point, because the overproducing cell simply rides slightly higher up its own J–V knee toward open-circuit voltage, recouping part of the loss. Voltage loss and resistance hurt much harder: every 100 mV lost in the top cell comes straight off the tandem's output, and pushing series resistance from 2 to 8 Ω·cm² pulls fill factor down from 88% to 82%.

Even with its simplified blackbody sun, this model lands within a couple of points of the 35%+ world records set in 2026 under AM1.5G. What it leaves out are the purely optical losses before photons ever reach the absorbers: front-glass reflection, shadowing from silver grid fingers, and parasitic absorption in the transparent conducting oxides and C₆₀, which together shave a few mA/cm² off real cells.

Step 7 · Surface textureCoating a field of pyramids

If you zoom in on a commercial silicon solar wafer under an electron microscope, it isn't smooth at all. Factories dip the wafer in a hot alkaline etch that eats faster along certain crystal directions, exposing the silicon's {111} planes as a jagged carpet of microscopic pyramids a few microns tall with 54.7° slopes. That roughness is optical gold: a photon reflecting off one pyramid face bounces sideways straight into a neighboring face for a second chance to enter, and once inside it refracts at an oblique angle that stretches its path through the wafer. On bare silicon, texturing alone slashes reflection from ~35% down to around 11%.

Now try laying a 500 nm perovskite film onto pyramids that are four to eight times taller than the film itself. Early lab tandems used spin coating, squirting liquid precursor onto a spinning wafer and letting it dry. Gravity and surface tension level the liquid flat across the valleys, leaving thick puddles in the troughs while the sharp pyramid peaks poke straight out through the film. Any bare peak lets the top contact touch the bottom cell directly, creating an electrical short (a shunt) that drains power across the whole cell.

Above is a 20 µm patch of etched silicon pyramids catching simulated sunlight. Every photon reflects according to silicon's real Fresnel reflectance and gets tracked across secondary bounces into adjacent facets: drag pyramid height down to zero and reflection leaps to ~34%; raise it back up and reflection drops to ~11% regardless of how big the pyramids are. With the default 2 µm pyramids and a 600 nm solution-coated film, the liquid pools ~1.3 µm deep in the valleys yet still leaves roughly 8% of the pyramid tips poking out bare; at 4 µm pyramids, nearly a third of the surface is exposed. Dumping thicker ink on top barely helps because the wide valleys swallow most of the volume.

Early tandem groups dodged the headache by polishing the front of the silicon wafer mirror-flat, giving up its light trapping. In 2018 a team at EPFL and CSEM broke that deadlock with a hybrid two-step process: thermally evaporate the inorganic lead-halide skeleton first so it drapes uniformly over every 54.7° facet, then convert it into perovskite with an organic solution. Click the coating button to flip from solution (spin / slot-die) to conformal (evaporated) and every bare tip vanishes. That textured approach reached 25.2% in 2018, and most record tandems since have paired conformal or hybrid coatings with gentler, slightly smaller pyramids than standard silicon lines use.

Step 8 · Factory yieldFrom one square centimeter to a whole wafer

Most headline lab records are measured on postage-stamp cells roughly 1 cm² across. A factory silicon cell is an entire wafer, 18.2 cm on a side in the standard M10 format, or 331 cm². Going 330× larger doesn't just make uniform coating harder; it runs head-on into Poisson statistics.

Imagine your coating line leaves random killer defects (pinholes, dust specks, bare patches that short out the cell) at an average density of D defects per square centimeter. For a cell of area A to survive, it has to dodge every single defect, which happens with probability: 

yield = exp(−D × A)      // Poisson probability of zero defects in area A

Below, one M10 wafer gets diced into cells of whatever size you pick while killer defects land at fixed random spots across the silicon.

At 0.02 killer defects per cm², 98% of 1 cm² research cells come off the bench spotless. Take the exact same chemistry and coat a full 331 cm² wafer, and yield craters to 0.1%—virtually every wafer catches a short somewhere. To keep 95% of full-wafer cells clean in mass production, killer defect density has to drop below ~0.00015 per cm², meaning fewer than one fatal flaw across twenty entire wafers.

Bigger cells also surrender efficiency to everyday geometry: coating thickness drifts across an 18 cm span, current has farther to travel through resistive transparent oxides, and perimeter effects change. You can watch that gap in certified numbers. In June 2025 LONGi reported 33% on a full commercial-scale 260.9 cm² tandem cell, impressive, and still a couple of points under its 1 cm² champions. Assemble cells into a full glass module and cell spacing, ribbons, and front glass shave off a few more points: Oxford PV's 60-cell tandem module hit 26.9% in 2024, with its first commercial shipments rated at 24.5%.

Step 9 · DurabilityEfficiency has to survive the weather

A regular silicon panel sits on a roof for 25 or 30 years under warranty, typically losing only around 0.5% of its initial power each year. Perovskites are a soft ionic crystal by comparison: water vapor chews through them, summer rooftop heat can drive volatile organic cations out of the lattice, and sunlight combined with electric bias nudges halide ions slowly into the contacts. Double-glass encapsulation with tight butyl edge seals can keep rain and humidity out, but heat, sunlight, and operating voltage are literally the job description.

Standard solar qualification protocols (IEC 61215) were invented around silicon's failure modes. The headline torture test is damp heat: 1,000 hours baked at 85 °C and 85% relative humidity. Good encapsulated perovskite tandems can pass that hurdle now, yet damp heat in the dark misses ion migration under continuous illumination and load, the exact condition that ages perovskites fastest. Real multi-year outdoor rooftop datasets are still young.

The chart below pits a tandem module against a standard 22% silicon module losing 0.5% of its starting power per year, modeling both as straight-line warranty curves where total 25-year electricity equals the area under the line.

Watch how unforgiving compound time is. Start a tandem at 26% (a full 18% relative head start over 22% silicon), let it degrade at 1.5% per year instead of 0.5%, and silicon overtakes it in year 14. Over a 25-year life, that big starting lead shrinks to just 2.4% more total energy. Match silicon's 0.5%/year fade rate, on the other hand, and the tandem delivers the full 18% lifetime bonus. Every tenth of a percent per year moves the economics dramatically, and there is no shortcut for proving a 25-year fade rate short of putting panels outside and waiting.

Lead content sits alongside durability too. A 500 nm perovskite film contains around 0.7 grams of lead per square meter (roughly a gram or so per panel, far less than a car battery), though still water-soluble if hail shatters the glass in a storm. Credible commercial deployments have to pair tight encapsulation (sometimes with lead-capturing films inside the laminate) with end-of-life recycling.

Step 10 · EconomicsWhere the extra watts pay for themselves

Silicon panels have gotten almost absurdly cheap, which sets a brutal bar for any newcomer. A tandem has to earn back the cost of its extra deposition tools, materials, and yield loss from the extra watts it squeezes out of each square meter. Where it gets help is the rest of the installation: aluminum racking, land lease, wiring runs, and crew labor mostly scale with panel area, so packing more watts into every square meter cuts balance-of-system costs per watt.

The calculator below runs that break-even math. The baseline silicon module is pinned at 22% efficiency and $0.20/W (roughly $44/m²), while per-watt items like inverters cancel out between the two. Adjust the tandem's efficiency, how much extra its top cell adds per square meter, and the site's area-related installation cost (these defaults are ballpark assumptions; real hardware prices swing fast by region and year).

At $70/m² of area-related cost (typical of an open utility field), a 26% tandem breaks even only if the perovskite top cell adds $21/m² or less; at $30/m² extra, plain silicon still wins on dollars per watt. Drag area-related cost up to $150/m² (much closer to residential and commercial rooftops, where mounting labor and limited roof space dominate) and that exact same $30/m² top cell comes out ahead. That explains the natural adoption ladder: tandems win earliest wherever space is expensive (tight roofs, carports, high-labor markets). Even though Oxford PV's very first commercial shipment went into a US utility-scale project, winning broad utility markets long-term means driving top-cell manufacturing cost down into the teens per square meter.

Stepping backWhy the whole tree matters

Tracing a perovskite-on-silicon tandem from Shockley and Queisser's 1961 paper down to a factory yield curve leaves a few lessons that stick. For sixty years silicon engineers wrung astonishing gains out of bulk purity and surface passivation, yet the two biggest losses left on the table—blue-photon heat and wasted infrared—belong strictly to having one bandgap. Adding a second absorber fixes the optical ceiling, and immediately hands you a new set of constraints: matching currents in series so neither layer starves the other, aligning contact energy levels within tens of millivolts at interfaces only one molecule thick, and draping half a micron of crystal evenly over micron-tall pyramids.

Then factory scale changes the game all over again. Defect densities that look spotless on a 1 cm² hero cell wipe out full-wafer yields exponentially, and a 1% annual degradation penalty quietly erases a four-point efficiency lead over two decades on a roof. None of those bottlenecks show up on a lab efficiency chart, which is why turning a 35% record into an ordinary rooftop panel is so much harder—and more interesting—than chasing the next decimal place.

Any future expansion of this page has three upgrades queued up: swapping the smooth blackbody for the real tabulated AM1.5G spectrum so every figure reproduces published limits to the decimal, adding a thin-film optical interference model to capture reflection and parasitic contact absorption, and integrating two-terminal current matching across a full day of shifting solar angles and redder sunset skies.

That's Solar Stack for now. Up at the top of the page, the simulated tandem is still sorting photons live—about three in ten caught by the perovskite, a quarter absorbed by the silicon underneath, and the leftover infrared sailing straight out the back.
