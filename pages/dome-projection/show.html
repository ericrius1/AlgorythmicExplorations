Dome Projection · Part 4

The Dome Show

Several projectors share one dome. Each needs a calibrated view, a warp to the real surface, and a blend where it overlaps its neighbours.

Scroll down to read ↓

Part two gave every point on the dome a source direction, and part three two ways of storing the image. A real room adds a physical question: which projector puts each point on the dome, and how does its image line up with the one beside it? Domes much bigger than a few metres usually use several projectors, each covering part of the surface, with overlaps between them.

In the hero, three projectors sit on the rim of the dome, each throwing its image across to cover the far third, with a band of overlap into its neighbours. For a few seconds every projector just adds its whole picture, tinted in its own colour, and the overlaps come out brighter where two images pile up. Then the blend fades in and the seams disappear into one sky.

Step 1 · Projector camerasModel each projector as a camera

A projector camera is a model of one physical projector: where it's mounted, which way it points, and the shape of its lens. Run it backwards and it tells you, for every pixel the projector can show, which ray leaves the lens. Follow that ray to the dome, take the direction from the dome centre, and you know which part of the master that pixel should show. That's the chain from part two, once per projector. 

projector pose  → where it is and which way it faces
projector lens  → which ray each of its pixels sends out
dome surface    → where each ray lands
coverage        → which projectors reach each point of the dome

Modelling the projector first keeps the rest grounded. The warp and the blend that follow are corrections to a specific physical view, not effects floating over the dome.

Step 2 · WarpCalibration meshes

A model is never exact. Mounts sag, lenses distort, and the dome isn't a perfect hemisphere. So measurement corrects the model, through a warp mesh laid over each projector's image. Each vertex stores where in the master that point of the projector's image should sample, and moving a vertex bends the image until the test grid lands where it belongs on the real dome. Between vertices the GPU interpolates. 

projector output pixel
  → the warp-mesh triangle it falls in
  → interpolated master uv
  → sample the fisheye or cubemap master

The measuring is usually done with a camera: show patterns, photograph the dome, solve for each projector's mesh. Ramesh Raskar, Michael Brown and colleagues at UNC showed camera-based registration of overlapping projectors in 1999, and automatic dome calibration systems work on the same principle. Get the geometry right before touching the blend. A soft blend can't hide two copies of a line that should be one, as the figure in the next step shows.

Step 3 · BlendOverlaps, gamma and black

Neighbouring projectors overlap on purpose, because no physical edge lines up perfectly. Across the overlap projector A fades down while B fades up, and the total should look like one projector. Two details decide whether it does.

The first is gamma. A projector doesn't turn pixel values into light in a straight line: the light it puts out is roughly the value raised to the power 2.2. So if each projector ramps its pixel values down linearly, halfway across the overlap each one sends 0.5, which comes out as 0.52.2 ≈ 0.22 of its full light, and the two together make 44%, a dark band. The ramp has to happen in light, which means sending ramp1/2.2 instead. 

light = value ^ 2.2                     // roughly what a projector does

ramp the values:  0.5^2.2 + 0.5^2.2             = 0.44   // dark band
ramp the light:   (0.5^(1/2.2))^2.2 * 2         = 1.0    // seamless

The second is black. A projector can't send less light than its own black level, and where two overlap, their blacks add up, so a dark scene shows a grey band right where the blend is. Turning the overlap down can't fix that, because it's already sending zero. The fix is to raise the black of everything outside the overlap to match, which costs a little contrast everywhere.

The strip at the top is what the audience sees: a bright-to-dark ramp with grid lines on it above a band of pure black, lit by projector A from the left and B from the right. Under it, the blue and orange curves are the light each projector puts out for white content, and the white curve is their total. With no blend the overlap is twice as bright. Ramping the pixel values sags to 44% in the middle, and ramping the light stays flat at 100%. Then raise the black level and watch the black band, and add some misalignment to watch the grid lines double inside the overlap, where both projectors draw them.

Aside · 2.2 is an approximation

Real projectors don't follow a single power law, and two units of the same model rarely match each other. Good blending measures each projector's actual response with a camera or a meter and builds its ramp from that curve. 2.2 is the sRGB-ish starting point, and it's close enough to show why the band appears.

Step 4 · MatchingColour, focus and test patterns

Lining up the geometry is only the first thing the eye catches. Two projectors can agree on where every line goes and still disagree on brightness, black level, focus or white point. That's why calibration uses dull images before pretty ones: 

rings and meridians   → geometry and seams
grey ramps            → gamma, brightness and black level
solid colour fields   → white point, projector to projector
small text            → focus

Calibrate in the order the eye notices mistakes: line positions first, then brightness in the overlaps, then colour. A soft blend can't hide a bad mesh, and colour correction can't hide a bright stripe. If a flat grey field shows a band in the overlap, it's the blend or the black level, and no amount of work on the artwork will fix it.

EpilogueThe whole loop

A working dome show runs the same loop every frame:

render or load one seamless dome image
for each projector:
  sample the image through its calibrated warp mesh
  apply its blend, black-level and colour correction
display, measure, adjust, repeat
  • Model the projector, then correct the model. The camera gets you close, and the measured warp mesh gets you the rest of the way.
  • Blend in light, not in pixel values. Otherwise every overlap is a dark band.
  • Black adds up. Overlaps lift the floor, and the only fix is to lift it everywhere else to match.
