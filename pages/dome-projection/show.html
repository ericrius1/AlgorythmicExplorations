Dome Projection · Part 4

The Dome Show

How multiple projectors split up a single planetarium ceiling without leaving double lines, dark overlap bands, or glowing seams in black skies.

Scroll down to read ↓

Once a dome gets larger than a few meters across, a single projector runs out of both brightness and pixel density. Real planetariums solve that by ringing the cove with several projectors, each throwing across the room to illuminate one wedge of the hemisphere with a deliberate strip of overlap into its neighbors. In part two and part three, the series built clean ways to store a whole sky; now that sky has to be split across physical hardware so nobody in the audience can spot where one projector stops and the next begins.

Watch the hero banner cycle between raw and blended output. Three projectors sit around the rim, each casting a tinted image across to the opposite third of the dome. While unblended, every overlap zone glows noticeably brighter where two beams stack on top of each other. Once the edge blend fades in, those bright bands melt away and the starfield and polar grid run uninterrupted overhead.

Step 1 · Projector camerasTreating each projector like a camera in reverse

Optically, a projector is just a camera running light backward. Start by modeling each physical unit as a camera: where it sits on the rim, which direction it aims, and the field of view and shift of its lens. Trace a ray outward through each pixel of the projector's chip until it hits the dome hemisphere, compute that point's direction from the dome center, and sample the fisheye or cubemap master there. That is the exact inverse chain from part two, evaluated once per projector: 

projector pose  → where it is mounted and which way it aims
projector lens  → which ray each chip pixel sends out
dome surface    → where that ray hits the ceiling
coverage        → which projectors reach each region of the dome

Building this physical camera model first gives you a clean baseline for every channel before you touch a single calibration handle, tying every downstream warp and mask to real room geometry.

Step 2 · WarpMeasured calibration meshes

No physical theater matches its architectural blueprints down to the millimeter. Ceiling trusses sag a hair, mounts drift over months, lenses introduce barrel or pincushion distortion, and the dome itself is rarely an exact mathematical sphere. To close that gap, each projector runs its output through a fine 2D warp mesh. Each vertex in the mesh holds the exact coordinate in the dome master that ought to land at that projector pixel, and the GPU interpolates smoothly across every triangle between control points: 

projector output pixel
  → the warp-mesh triangle it falls in
  → interpolated master uv
  → sample the fisheye or cubemap master

Dragging mesh vertices by eye works for tiny setups, though modern planetariums almost always automate the measurement with wide-angle cameras. Each projector flashes structured test patterns onto the ceiling, cameras photograph where every dot actually lands, and a solver computes each channel's warp mesh automatically. Ramesh Raskar, Michael Brown, and their colleagues at UNC pioneered camera-based multi-projector registration back in 1999, and commercial dome autocalibration rigs still follow that blueprint. Either way, geometry has to lock down first: as you can test in the next figure, no amount of feathering can disguise a grid line drawn in two slightly different places at once.

Step 3 · BlendWhy overlaps turn dark, and why black turns gray

Projectors overlap intentionally because trying to butt two physical light cones edge-to-edge leaves either a razor-sharp dark crack or a bright sliver the moment a mount shifts by a fraction of a degree. Across the shared overlap band, projector A ramps smoothly down to zero while projector B ramps smoothly up to full output so their combined light stays flat. Getting that transition genuinely invisible runs into two physical realities of projector optics.

First is gamma. Projectors respond nonlinearly to encoded pixel values: the light leaving the lens scales roughly as the pixel value raised to the power 2.2. If you ramp the raw pixel values linearly across the overlap, both projectors send 0.5 at the midpoint. Each beam emits only 0.52.2 ≈ 0.22 of full white, so the sum hits just 44%, carving an obvious dark bruise right down the seam. To make the light ramp linearly, you have to apply the inverse gamma curve and feed each projector ramp^(1/2.2) instead: 

light = value ^ 2.2                     // rough projector electro-optical curve

ramp the values:  0.5^2.2 + 0.5^2.2             = 0.44   // dark seam band
ramp the light:   (0.5^(1/2.2))^2.2 * 2         = 1.0    // flat brightness

Second is projector black level. Even when you send pure RGB (0, 0, 0), a projector lamp still leaks a faint glow through the imaging chip onto the dome. Inside an overlap zone, two projectors leak onto the same patch of ceiling, doubling the dark floor and painting a pale gray stripe straight across night skies. You cannot dim the overlap any further because both channels are already commanding zero; the only way to erase the stripe is black-level uplift, raising the darkest black everywhere outside the overlap to match the double floor, trading away a small bit of contrast in exchange for a uniform dark sky.

Aside · 2.2 is only a starting point

Real projection hardware rarely follows a clean single-exponent power law, and even two units of the same model fresh out of the box drift slightly apart as lamps or lasers age. Serious calibration setups measure each projector's actual luminance curve with a camera or photometer and build per-channel lookup ramps from those readings; 2.2 is just the standard sRGB ballpark that explains why naive linear blending fails so dramatically.

Step 4 · MatchingColor, focus, and calibration order

Once the lines align and the blend stays flat across gray ramps, your eye moves on to subtler mismatches: one projector leaning slightly warmer green while its neighbor tilts cool blue, or slightly softer focus toward one corner of the lens. Technicians work through plain diagnostic images in a strict order: 

rings and meridians   → geometry and warp registration
gray ramps            → gamma, brightness, and black level
solid color fields    → white point and color matching across channels
small text            → optical focus across each lens

Fixing them in that exact sequence saves endless chasing of symptoms. Feathering a blend never heals double lines from a loose warp mesh, and tweaking color matrices never removes a mid-tone dip from bad gamma. Whenever a uniform gray dome shows a visible band inside the overlap, the culprit is always the blend curve or black floor rather than the show content.

EpilogueClosing the projection loop

Every frame of a finished dome playback system repeats the same compact pipeline:

render or decode one seamless dome image
for each projector:
  sample the image through its calibrated warp mesh
  apply its light-linear blend, black-level uplift, and color correction
display, measure, adjust, repeat

Getting a multi-projector dome to disappear around an audience takes a physical camera model refined by measured warp meshes, edge ramps shaped in real light rather than encoded pixels, and raised black levels so dark scenes stay seamless. A great next experiment for Figure 1 would be adding a virtual calibration camera aimed back at the strip, snapping distorted grids and solving the warp automatically from detected corners.

Everything above assumes a single finished dome master already exists before the projector passes run. When the visuals are generated live every frame in Three.js or WebGPU, though, you face one more architectural choice: render a shared master first, or point a separate 3D camera down each projector's throat directly.
