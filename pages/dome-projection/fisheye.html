Dome Projection · Part 2

The Fisheye Map

How a circular domemaster packs an entire hemisphere of directions into a flat square, and how four classic lens laws divide up its radius.

Scroll down to read ↓

Open a fulldome movie file on an ordinary laptop and it looks strangely warped: the whole planetarium sky sits squeezed inside a circle touching the four edges of a square frame, with horizons bent into rings and constellations curving around the rim. That layout makes complete sense the moment you treat the image as a lookup table of 3D directions. The exact center of the disk corresponds to straight overhead (the zenith), the circular rim corresponds to the horizon all 360 degrees around the room, and the angle around the disk gives the compass bearing.

Watch the hero at the top of the page for a few seconds to see the connection directly. It starts from a seat inside the dome looking up at the stars, then smoothly pulls every pixel into the position it occupies inside a 180-degree fisheye master before sliding back into the room. Those faint blue rings sit every 10 degrees down from the zenith, and once the image flattens into a disk, every ring lands at an identical radial spacing.

In part one the work stayed strictly in two dimensions, turning pixels inside a flat disk into (r, θ). Moving up to a hemisphere adds one crucial design choice: how far from the center of the image should a given tilt angle land?

Step 1 · Direction firstFrom sky angles to a unit vector

Any point on the hemisphere can be pinned down by two angles: the azimuth φ turning around the room, and the zenith angle θ tilting downward from straight overhead. For a full 180-degree hemisphere, θ = 0° sits at the top of the dome and θ = 90° rings the horizon. 

// direction from dome angles, z up
dir.x = sin(θ) * cos(φ)
dir.y = sin(θ) * sin(φ)
dir.z = cos(θ)

Every rendering or projection pipeline revolves around that unit vector. A fisheye image is simply a tidy way to pack all of those vectors onto a square texture: keep the azimuth φ as the angle around the image center, and map the zenith angle θ onto a normalized radius ρ between 0 and 1. Different optical lenses do that radial mapping with different curves.

The circular master on the left renders backward pixel by pixel, just like the disk in part one: each pixel's radius ρ runs backward through the chosen lens formula to recover θ, which gives a 3D direction into the test pattern. On the upper right, the dome appears sliced side-on with rays every 15 degrees, and below it sits a plot of ρ against θ for all four classic curves. Click any law in the plot legend (or cycle the law button) while watching the 15-degree rings on the left. Equidistant spaces the rings evenly from center to rim, equisolid and orthographic pack them tighter toward the horizon, and stereographic stretches the horizon outward while compressing the zenith.

Step 2 · LensesFour ways to spend the radius

equidistant    ρ = θ / θmax
equisolid      ρ = sin(θ/2) / sin(θmax/2)
orthographic   ρ = sin(θ) / sin(θmax)
stereographic  ρ = tan(θ/2) / tan(θmax/2)

Equidistant hands every degree of elevation the exact same slice of pixels. That linearity makes shaders trivial to debug, which is why fulldome video settled on the 180-degree equidistant fisheye as its standard delivery format, usually called a domemaster. Equisolid preserves solid angle so equal patches of sky occupy equal pixel areas, a property many photographic fisheye lenses aim for. Orthographic is what a hemisphere looks like viewed from infinitely far above, crushing the horizon almost flat: the final 15 degrees above the rim squeeze into roughly 3% of the radius. Stereographic goes the other way, preserving local angles so small shapes keep their proportions near the rim even as they grow in pixel size.

Dragging the field of view slider narrows θmax below 90 degrees. At 180 degrees of total field of view, the circle captures the entire hemisphere; tighten the lens and red arcs appear along the lower dome in the side profile to mark the blind band it can no longer reach. Real glass lenses rarely obey any of these four textbook curves to the sub-pixel, so in production you always document the target formula, check it against a measured ring grid, and let the calibration stage soak up whatever residual lens distortion remains.

Aside · Where "fisheye" comes from

Physicist Robert W. Wood coined the phrase in 1906 after building a pinhole camera filled with water to photograph how a fish sees the world above a pond. Refraction at the air-water boundary compresses the entire 180-degree sky into a cone roughly 97 degrees wide, known as Snell's window. Two decades later, meteorologist Robin Hill designed an all-sky glass lens to photograph cloud cover across the whole hemisphere in a single exposure, establishing the optical family dome projection still relies on.

Step 3 · Inverse ray chainFollowing a pixel back from the projector

In an actual theater, the projector almost never sits neatly at the dome's center, the ceiling itself has slight structural bumps, and the projection lens introduces its own warp. Even so, the shader follows the exact same backward lookup per projector pixel, just with a couple of geometric steps spliced in: 

projector pixel  → ray out of the lens
ray              → point where it hits the dome
dome point       → direction from the dome center
direction        → fisheye uv
fisheye uv       → color

Analytic math gives a clean first approximation of those arrows, and physical calibration turns them into warp meshes that match the real room. Part four unpacks how multiple projectors measure and blend that chain.

Step 4 · DebuggingReading a calibration grid

Before loading nebulae or starfields onto a dome, engineers always project a plain grid marked with known azimuth spokes, elevation rings, a zenith dot, and an obvious seam, much like the test pattern in Figure 1. When something goes sideways in the pipeline, the grid tells you immediately which stage broke: 

seam opens or doubles            → azimuth wrap, or the image is mirrored
rings bunched or stretched       → wrong projection law or field of view
a spoke or pinch at the center   → something depends on φ at the zenith
labels drift between projectors  → calibration error, rather than content
Another way to store the same directions

A cubemap packs the exact same sphere of directions into six square images, one per face of a cube. Real-time 3D engines frequently render a cubemap first because each face uses a standard 90-degree perspective camera, then resample it into a fisheye or read the cube straight inside each projector pass. Either way, the underlying 3D direction vector stays identical. Part three digs into how those two representations trade off.

EpilogueKeeping the direction separate from the container

Once you start thinking in unit directions instead of raw texture pixels, fisheye formats stop feeling mysterious. The unit vector is the actual scene data; the lens law ρ(θ) is just the encoding that lays those vectors onto a flat circle, and every downstream pass works backward from an output pixel through that encoding. A further extension of Figure 1 would plot a fifth curve fitted to measured dots from a physical projector lens and overlay its error against an ideal equidistant curve, since that residual curve is precisely what venue calibration ends up correcting.

A circular fisheye is ideal when you need to ship a finished movie file to another planetarium, yet standard GPU rasterizers struggle to draw curved 180-degree images directly. Next comes the representation real-time engines love instead: five flat square cameras looking up and around the room.
