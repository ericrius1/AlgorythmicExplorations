Dome Projection · Part 2

The Fisheye Map

A dome master is a circle where every pixel stands for a direction. The view above slides between the room and the image that fills it.

Scroll down to read ↓

Part one named points in a circle. A dome adds one idea: each point in the image stands for a direction in space. The centre of the image is straight up, the zenith. The edge is the horizon. The angle around the image is the compass direction.

The hero shows it happening. For a few seconds you're in the room, looking up at the dome. Then every pixel slides toward the direction it has in a fisheye master, and the dome flattens into a circle with the same stars in the same places. The rings are every 10 degrees down from the zenith, and in the master they come out evenly spaced.

That circle is how dome content is usually delivered. It isn't meant to look natural on a flat monitor. It's a lookup table for directions.

Step 1 · Direction firstFrom the dome to the image

A point on the dome can be given by two angles: the azimuth φ, which way round the room it is, and the zenith angle θ, how far it tilts from straight up. In a 180-degree master, θ = 0 is the centre of the image and θ = 90° is its edge. 

// direction from the dome angles, z up
dir.x = sin(θ) * cos(φ)
dir.y = sin(θ) * sin(φ)
dir.z = cos(θ)

That vector is the thing that matters. The fisheye image is one way of packing it into pixels: the angle around the image is φ, and the distance from the centre, ρ, is some function of θ. Which function is what a lens is.

The fisheye on the left is filled pixel by pixel, backwards, the way part one filled its circle. Each pixel's distance from the centre goes through the chosen law to give a zenith angle, and the figure reads the dome's test pattern in that direction. On the right is the same dome sliced in half, with rays every 15 degrees, and under it a plot of ρ against θ for all four laws. Press the law button and watch the rings. Equidistant keeps them evenly spaced. Equisolid and orthographic crowd them toward the edge, and stereographic does the opposite.

Step 2 · LensesFour ways to spend the radius

equidistant    ρ = θ / θmax
equisolid      ρ = sin(θ/2) / sin(θmax/2)
orthographic   ρ = sin(θ) / sin(θmax)
stereographic  ρ = tan(θ/2) / tan(θmax/2)

Equidistant gives every degree the same number of pixels. That makes it the easiest one to reason about, and it's what fulldome content is delivered in: a 180-degree equidistant fisheye, usually called a domemaster. Equisolid keeps areas in proportion, and many fisheye camera lenses are built close to it. Orthographic is a hemisphere seen from far above, and it squashes the horizon almost to nothing: the last 15 degrees get only 3% of the radius. Stereographic preserves angles locally, so shapes near the edge keep their proportions while growing larger.

The lens field of view slider sets θmax. A 180-degree lens fills the circle with one hemisphere. Narrow it and the circle covers less than the dome; the red arcs in the side view are the part the lens can't see. Real camera and projector lenses don't follow any of these laws perfectly, so the practical rule is to name the projection, write the formula down, and check it against a grid before trusting a show file.

Aside · Where "fisheye" comes from

The physicist Robert W. Wood coined the term in 1906. He built a camera filled with water to show how a fish sees the world above the surface: the whole sky squeezed into a circle of about 97 degrees, which is the cone that refraction at the surface lets light through. In the 1920s the meteorologist Robin Hill designed a lens that photographed the whole sky at once, for studying clouds, and fisheye lenses have been the way to put a hemisphere into one image ever since.

Step 3 · BackwardsThe projector asks the question

In a real room the projector isn't at the centre of the dome, its lens has its own distortion, and the dome isn't a perfect hemisphere. The renderer still asks the same backwards question for every output pixel. It just takes more steps to answer: 

projector pixel  → ray out of the lens
ray              → point where it hits the dome
dome point       → direction from the dome centre
direction        → fisheye uv
fisheye uv       → colour

Calibration is the work of making those arrows match the physical room. The maths gives the ideal answer, and measured control points and warp meshes turn it into one that works. That's part four.

Step 4 · DebuggingSeams, poles and grids

The most useful dome texture isn't pretty. It has known azimuths, rings at known zenith angles, a mark at the centre and a visible seam, like the pattern in the figure. Each kind of mistake shows up differently on it: 

seam opens or doubles            → azimuth wrap, or the image is mirrored
rings bunched or stretched       → wrong projection law or field of view
a spoke or pinch at the centre   → something depends on φ at the zenith
labels drift between projectors  → calibration, not content
A fisheye isn't the only way to store directions

A cubemap stores the same sphere of directions as six square images, one per face of a cube. Realtime engines often render a cubemap first, because each face is an ordinary 90-degree camera, and then convert it to a fisheye or sample it straight from the projector pass. The direction vector is the same either way. Part three compares the two.

EpilogueWhat carries over

  • A dome image stores directions. The vector is the real data, and the fisheye is one way of packing it into pixels.
  • A lens is a function ρ(θ). Name it and test it, because everything downstream assumes it.
  • Every step works backwards. However many stages sit between the output pixel and the source, each pixel asks once.
