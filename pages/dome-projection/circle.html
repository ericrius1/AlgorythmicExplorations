Dome Projection · Part 1

The Circle Under the Dome

Seen from below, a dome is a circle, and every point in it has an angle and a distance from the centre. Polar coordinates, seams, and working backwards.

Scroll down to read ↓

This series goes from a circle on paper to a working dome show: the image formats domes use, how several projectors share one dome without visible seams, and how to render a live scene for one. The first step is small. Before a dome is a room or a projector layout, it's a coordinate problem.

The planetarium at the top of the page is ray traced on your GPU, one pixel at a time. The sky turns slowly, and the grid on it is polar: rings every 15 degrees out from the top of the dome, spokes every 30 degrees around it. The orange spoke sweeps round once every 30 seconds, the green arc is the angle it has covered so far, and the blue point slides in and out along it.

A flat screen asks for an x and a y. A dome asks for a direction. Seen from straight below, a dome is a circle, and every point in a circle has two names: x and y, or a distance from the centre and an angle around it. Most of the maths in this series is moving between those names without losing track of where the circle gets cut.

Step 1 · Two namesCartesian and polar

The left half of the figure is the unit circle with one point in it, labelled with its polar name: its radius r and its angle θ. The right half is a tiny texture, twelve texels around by five out. Its horizontal axis is the angle and its vertical axis is the radius, so it's the circle cut along one spoke and unrolled into a rectangle. Drag either point. They're the same state in two coordinate systems.

The circle is filled from the texture. For each pixel inside it, the figure works out that pixel's r and θ, turns them into a texture coordinate, and reads the colour there. A fulldome image works the same way, one dimension up. 

// polar → cartesian
x = r * cos(θ)
y = r * sin(θ)

// cartesian → polar
r = sqrt(x*x + y*y)
θ = atan2(y, x)

Use atan2(y, x), not atan(y / x), to get the angle back. A plain arctangent only sees the slope, so it can't tell (1, 1) from (−1, −1), and it divides by zero anywhere on the vertical axis. atan2 looks at both signs and returns the full angle, from −π to π. Add 2π to the negative ones if you want 0 to 2π, which is what the figure does.

Aside · Where radians come from

A radian is the angle whose arc is as long as the radius, so a full turn is 2π. Measured that way, an arc's length is just r·θ, and the derivative of sin is cos with no 180/π factor hanging off it. Roger Cotes was working with the idea by 1714, but the name came much later: "radian" first appeared in print in 1873, in exam questions James Thomson set at Queen's College, Belfast.

Step 2 · The seamAngles wrap, textures don't

Angles repeat: 0, 2π and 4π all point the same way. A texture doesn't repeat. It has a left edge and a right edge, so the circle has to be cut somewhere, and the cut is the seam. In the figure it's the orange spoke at θ = 0, which is both edges of the texture at once. Press go to the seam and nudge the angle back and forth across it: the point in the circle barely moves, and the point in the texture jumps from one side to the other. 

u = fract(θ / 2π)    // around the circle, 0..1
v = clamp(r, 0, 1)   // centre to edge, 0..1

fract keeps only the fractional part, which turns an angle that can grow without limit into a coordinate that repeats from 0 to 1.

A seam in the coordinates doesn't have to be a seam in the picture. The texture in the figure goes once round a colour wheel, so its last column blends back into its first, and the figure's sampler wraps when it filters, so the circle has no visible join. Two things commonly break that. One is a sampler set to clamp instead of repeat, which stops the filter from blending across the edge. The other is subtler, and it's the classic polar shader bug. A GPU chooses which mipmap level to read from how fast the texture coordinate changes between neighbouring pixels. Right at the seam, one pixel computes u = 0.999 and the pixel next to it u = 0.001, so the GPU decides the whole texture fits between two pixels, reads the smallest mip level, and draws a one-pixel line of averaged colour along the seam. The fix is to hand the sampler gradients that wrap too (textureSampleGrad in WGSL, with derivatives taken from an angle that doesn't jump), or to choose the level yourself.

Aside · Put the seam where nobody looks

A dome's coordinates always have a seam somewhere. When content is built from a panorama, which wraps around the same way as this texture, it makes sense to turn the seam toward the back of the room, behind most of the audience, and to keep bright hard edges away from it. If anything does show there, few people are facing it.

Step 3 · BackwardsSampling is the job

A projection mapper doesn't push content forward and hope it lands in the right place. It works backwards. For each output pixel it asks which direction that pixel shows, turns the direction into a position in the source image, and reads the colour there: 

for each output pixel:
  direction = pixel_to_direction(pixel)
  uv        = direction_to_source_uv(direction)
  color     = sample(source, uv)

That's how the circle in the figure is filled, and how every image in this series is made, the hero included. Going backwards means every output pixel gets one answer. Push source pixels forward instead and some output pixels get none, which shows up as holes, and others get several, which shows up as sparkle where they fight.

The centre is a singular point

At r = 0 every angle names the same point. That's the geometry, and code that depends on θ there misbehaves. Nothing crashes (in JavaScript, Math.atan2(0, 0) returns 0). What goes wrong is anything that varies with the angle, like a pattern of spokes, which pinches into a visible point at the centre. Fade those effects out near r = 0, or pick a fixed angle there. On a dome the same point is the zenith, straight overhead, where a pinched pattern is hard to miss.

EpilogueWhat carries over

  • A point has two names. atan2 gets you from one to the other with the quadrant intact.
  • Angles wrap and textures don't. Decide where the seam goes, and make sure both the filter and the mip selection wrap across it.
  • Work backwards from the output. One lookup per output pixel, and no holes.

Things I'd try next: put the mipmap seam in the figure itself, rendered on the GPU with a switch for the gradient fix, so the one-pixel line is there to see instead of described.
