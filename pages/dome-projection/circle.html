Dome Projection · Part 1

The Circle Under the Dome

Polar coordinates, the seam where a wrapped angle hits a flat texture, and why dome renderers sample in reverse.

Scroll down to read ↓

Lean back in a planetarium seat and look straight overhead, and the entire screen reads as a disk. Before you touch a projector mount, a calibration mesh, or a multi-channel blend, projecting onto a dome starts with a basic coordinate snag: angles loop smoothly around a circle, while GPU textures are rectangles with hard left and right borders.

Up in the hero banner, a GPU ray tracer renders a small planetarium from a rear seat. Over the slowly turning starfield sits a polar grid, with rings spaced every 15 degrees from the apex and spokes every 30 degrees around the rim. The orange spoke sweeps a full revolution roughly every 30 seconds, trailing a green arc for its angle while a blue point slides back and forth along its radius.

Over the next five articles we will build all the way from that simple circle to a calibrated multi-projector show and a real-time 3D rendering loop. Right now, though, everything comes down to converting cleanly between Cartesian (x, y) coordinates on a pixel grid and polar (r, θ) coordinates around a center, while keeping careful track of the cut where the circle opens into a rectangle.

Step 1 · Two namesCartesian and polar

Below, the disk on the left shows a single point described by its radius r and angle θ. Beside it sits a tiny 12-by-5 texture whose horizontal axis tracks the angle and whose vertical axis tracks the radius. Think of the rectangle as that same circle sliced along a single spoke and unrolled flat. Drag either handle (or hit orbit) to see both views move in lockstep.

Every pixel inside that left-hand disk gets its color directly from the texture on the right: take the pixel's (x, y) offset from the center, compute its radius and angle, map those to (u, v), and fetch the texel. Fulldome projection uses the exact same lookup pattern, only with a 3D direction vector in place of a 2D radius. 

// polar → cartesian
x = r * cos(θ)
y = r * sin(θ)

// cartesian → polar
r = sqrt(x*x + y*y)
θ = atan2(y, x)

Always reach for atan2(y, x) when recovering the angle instead of dividing y / x and calling plain atan. Because a ratio discards the individual signs, atan(y / x) confuses (1, 1) with (−1, −1) and blows up along the entire vertical axis where x = 0. atan2 inspects both signs and covers the full circle from −π to π; adding 2π to negative results wraps it cleanly into [0, 2π), which is what the readout above displays.

Aside · Where radians come from

One radian is the angle that sweeps out an arc equal in length to the circle's radius, putting 2π radians in a full turn. In radians, arc length simplifies to r·θ and the derivative of sin is plain cos without stray 180/π factors everywhere. Mathematician Roger Cotes was already using the unit by 1714, though the name arrived more than a century later: "radian" first showed up in print in 1873 on exam papers James Thomson wrote at Queen's College, Belfast.

Step 2 · The seamWrapping angles across flat edges

Geometrically, 0, 2π, and 4π all point along the exact same ray. A rectangular texture, however, stops at u = 0 on the left and u = 1 on the right, so mapping a circle onto a rectangle always introduces a cut. In the figure above, that cut is the orange seam along θ = 0, which corresponds to both vertical edges of the texture simultaneously. Click go to the seam and rock the angle θ slider slightly across zero: the dot inside the circle barely shifts, yet its twin on the texture leaps all the way from one side to the other. 

u = fract(θ / 2π)    // around the circle, 0..1
v = clamp(r, 0, 1)   // center to edge, 0..1

Taking fract keeps only the fractional part, folding any unbounded angle cleanly into [0, 1).

Even though the coordinates jump across the seam, the rendered image can still look completely continuous. Our 12-column texture cycles smoothly around a color wheel so column 11 blends naturally into column 0, and its bilinear filter wraps horizontally. When a visible stripe does appear along θ = 0 in a real shader, one of two bugs is almost always responsible. The obvious culprit is leaving the sampler's horizontal address mode on clamp-to-edge instead of repeat, preventing the filter from blending across the boundary.

The subtler culprit trips up almost everyone who writes their first polar shader: mipmap selection. To choose a mip level, GPU hardware takes screen-space derivatives of u across neighboring 2×2 pixel quads. Straddling the seam, one pixel lands at u = 0.999 and its immediate neighbor lands at u = 0.001. Seeing a jump of nearly 1.0 across a single pixel step, the GPU assumes the entire texture has shrunk into one pixel, fetches the blurriest 1×1 mip level, and paints a crisp one-pixel smear straight along the seam. You prevent that by supplying continuous wrapped gradients explicitly via textureSampleGrad in WGSL (taking derivatives from a continuous angle or Cartesian coordinates before wrapping) or by selecting the mip level manually.

Aside · Put the seam where nobody looks

Whenever dome imagery starts from a cylindrical or equirectangular panorama, some azimuth has to carry the coordinate wrap. Aiming that seam toward the rear wall behind the main seating block and keeping sharp high-contrast artwork away from it is cheap insurance: if an encoder artifact or filter glitch ever slips through, almost nobody is staring directly at it.

Step 3 · Inverse mappingWorking backward from every output pixel

If you tried rendering the disk by iterating over source texels and pushing each one forward to its (x, y) destination, the outer edge would break apart into empty holes where texels spread apart, while texels near the center would pile on top of one another and flicker. Instead, projection shaders always run in reverse, starting at each output pixel and asking where its color comes from: 

for each output pixel:
  direction = pixel_to_direction(pixel)
  uv        = direction_to_source_uv(direction)
  color     = sample(source, uv)

Working backward guarantees that every single output pixel asks once and receives a cleanly filtered color with zero gaps. That three-line loop drives the circle in Figure 1, the ray-traced hero above, and every projection stage we will build for the rest of this series.

The center is a singular point

At r = 0, every angle points to the exact same spot. Nothing crashes in code (in JavaScript, Math.atan2(0, 0) quietly returns 0), yet anything drawn as a function of θ (radial spokes or striped panoramas, for example) pinches into a sharp knot right at the origin. Fading angular detail out as r approaches zero, or switching to a constant angle within a tiny radius, keeps the center clean. Inside a real dome that singular point is the zenith directly overhead, which happens to be one of the easiest spots in the room to notice a pinch.

EpilogueFrom a flat circle to a sky

Recovering full-quadrant angles with atan2, respecting the wrap boundary in both filtering and mip gradients, and sampling backward from output pixels form the bedrock of every fulldome shader. One thing I would still love to add to Figure 1 someday is a live GPU toggle that intentionally breaks the mipmap derivatives at θ = 0 so you can watch that infamous one-pixel seam line pop into view and vanish again under textureSampleGrad.
