Dome Projection · Part 5

Dome-Native Three.js

A live scene on a dome: one shared master that every projector samples, or one render per projector camera. Where the frame time goes in each.

Scroll down to read ↓

Part four split a dome rig into projector cameras, warp meshes and blends. This part is about where the image comes from before those corrections, when the content is live: a Three.js scene that updates every frame.

There are two common answers. Render one shared dome image first, a fisheye or a cubemap, and let each projector sample it through its warp. Or skip the shared image and render the scene once from each projector's camera. Both end with the same warp and blend passes, and both make one image per projector in the end. The difference is the intermediate, and that decides where the frame time goes.

In the hero, the coloured rectangles are what three projector cameras see, each thrown across the dome from the rim. The line sweeping down each one stands in for that camera redrawing its view, which in the direct approach happens every frame.

Method 1 · Shared masterRender one dome image, sample it per projector

The shared-master method keeps the model of a fulldome film, but makes the master live. The scene renders from the dome centre into a fisheye or cubemap render target, and each projector samples that target through its own warp mesh and blend. 

update the scene
render from the dome centre → fisheye or cubemap target
for each projector:
  draw its warp mesh, sampling the shared target
  apply its blend and colour correction

There's one dome image in the middle of the pipeline, which makes it easy to preview on a monitor, easy to record, and compatible with playback tools that expect a domemaster. What it costs depends on how the master is made: a ray-traced fisheye is one pass, while a cubemap means five scene renders before any projector pass starts. Post effects are also harder to reason about on a 180-degree image.

Method 2 · DirectRender from each projector camera

The dome-native path treats every physical projector as a camera. Instead of making a master first, the scene renders from each projector's calibrated point of view, and each of those images goes through a small correction pass. There's no 180-degree image anywhere in the middle. 

update the scene
for each projector:
  set its calibrated camera
  render the scene into its target
  draw its target through the warp, blend and colour correction

This suits installations where the room is the only target and the projector layout is part of the design. Each render sees roughly the region one projector covers, so culling and local effects match what that projector shows. It isn't automatically faster, though: three projectors means three scene renders per frame.

Aside · No fisheye doesn't mean no textures

A WebGL or WebGPU renderer always produces images, and the correction pass always samples one. The question is whether those images are one shared dome master or one image per projector.

CostWhere the frame time goes

The warp and blend passes are cheap: a texture read or two per output pixel. What's expensive is running the whole scene, with its materials, particles, shadows, transparency and post effects, over every pixel of every render. So a useful first estimate is how many pixels get the full scene each frame.

At the defaults, three 1920×1200 projectors rendered directly shade 6.9 million pixels a frame. A 4K fisheye master is a circle 4,096 pixels across, which is 13.2 million. A cubemap as sharp as that fisheye at the centre of each face needs faces 2,608 pixels square, and with the top face plus the upper halves of four side faces that's 20.4 million. Direct rendering wins on this count, even though every overlap gets rendered twice.

Pixels aren't the only cost. Anything paid once per render, like shadow maps, culling and draw-call overhead, is paid five times for the cubemap and once per projector for the direct path. And the fisheye's single render has to be ray traced or vertex-bent, since triangles can't make the curves a fisheye needs. Raise the projector count and resolution in the figure and the direct path climbs past the fisheye; shrink the master and the fisheye gets cheap.

ExamplesWhen each one is better

One projector with a fisheye lens

Shared master. There's one output and it already wants a circular fisheye. Rendering that target also gives you a monitor preview and a recording for free.

Three projectors, light geometry, each covering a narrow slice

Direct cameras. Each camera sees only part of the dome, so culling and level of detail skip everything else, and three projector renders can cost less than five cube faces.

A heavy shader that covers the whole dome

Shared master. If every projector needs the same expensive result, rendering it per projector repeats the work in every overlap. One ray-traced fisheye plus cheap sampling passes does it once.

Effects tied to parts of the room

Direct cameras. A glow, mask or label that belongs to one part of the architecture is easiest to check in the same view that will display it, without translating it through a shared fisheye.

A touring show, or a mix of video and live layers

Master, or both. A fulldome master travels, and each venue maps it with its own calibration. Live layers can still render per projector on top of a shared video master. Real systems often mix the two.

"Dome native" describes the coordinate system the work happens in, not a promise that the GPU does less. It can mean less work, the same work or more, depending on what gets rendered more than once.

CalibrationThe camera isn't enough

With a perfect model, a projector camera and a mathematical dome would be enough. Real domes aren't perfect, lenses distort, mounts shift, and projectors rarely sit exactly where the drawings say. So direct rendering still needs part four's calibration data: 

projector pose    → where the projector is
projector lens    → its frustum and distortion
warp mesh         → measured correction on the dome
blend mask        → the overlap fade, in light
colour correction → matched brightness and white point

The calibrated camera gets the render close, and the warp pass fixes what measurement finds. Because the projector frustum is usually off-centre, the camera needs an asymmetric projection matrix rather than a plain field of view.

ImplementationA Three.js loop

A minimal direct loop updates the scene once and renders it once per projector. Every projector sees the same scene state for the frame; only the camera changes. 

function frame(dt) {
  updateScene(dt);                         // one scene state for every projector

  for (const p of projectors) {
    renderer.setRenderTarget(p.target);    // this projector's camera view
    renderer.render(scene, p.camera);
  }

  renderer.setRenderTarget(null);          // the real outputs
  for (const p of projectors) {
    renderer.setViewport(p.viewport);      // its part of the output canvas
    renderer.render(p.warpScene, orthoCamera);  // warp mesh textured with p.target
  }
}

Each p.camera is a PerspectiveCamera whose projection matrix comes from calibration, and each p.warpScene holds the warp mesh with p.target.texture on it and the blend mask applied. If the master is cheap and the scene is heavy, the shared path can win; if the master would need a big cubemap and each projector covers a narrow slice, the direct path can. It depends on scene complexity, projector count, master resolution, the calibration workflow, and whether you need a portable master at all.

EpiloguePick the intermediate on purpose

The fisheye master isn't mandatory. It's an intermediate, and so is a cubemap. For realtime dome work, use one when it helps with previewing, recording or tool compatibility, and skip it when the room is the only target and per-projector renders keep the work simpler.

  • Count pixels that get the full scene. The warp passes are noise next to them.
  • Count renders too. Per-render costs multiply by five for a cubemap and by the projector count for the direct path.
  • Calibration doesn't go away. Whichever path renders the scene, the warp, blend and black level from part four still finish the frame.

Things I'd try next: measure instead of counting. A real Three.js scene with timestamp queries around each render would show where the crossover sits for a given scene, rather than where the pixel counts say it should.
