Dome Projection · Part 5

Dome-Native Three.js

Two architectures for driving a multi-projector planetarium with live 3D graphics, and how to budget pixels and render passes between them.

Scroll down to read ↓

The moment you want a live Three.js world running at 60 frames a second across a multi-projector dome, you hit a clean fork in the road. In part four we wired up the back half of the theater (projector cameras, measured warp meshes, and light-linear edge blends) assuming a seamless dome image was already sitting in memory. When that image is rendered live every frame, do you still generate a shared 180-degree master first and let every projector sample it, or do you cut out the shared master and render the 3D scene straight from each projector's own camera?

Both architectures end with the exact same warp, blend, and color-correction passes, and both ultimately output one finished buffer per projector. Where they diverge is the intermediate target sitting in the middle of the frame, which completely changes how many pixels get shaded and how many times the engine walks the scene graph.

In the hero above, the three colored rectangles outline the fields of view of three rim-mounted projectors throwing across the dome, with a bright scanline sweeping down each rectangle to show each camera redrawing its own slice every frame.

Method 1 · Shared masterRender one live dome image, sample it per projector

The shared-master route keeps the classic fulldome movie workflow intact and simply replaces the video decoder with a live renderer. Each frame, the engine renders the world from the center of the dome into either a fisheye or a cubemap render target, and then each projector draws its calibrated warp mesh sampling that single shared texture: 

update the scene
render from the dome center → fisheye or cubemap target
for each projector:
  draw its warp mesh, sampling the shared target
  apply its blend and color correction

Having a complete 180-degree frame sitting in memory every tick gives you huge operational perks: you can show a clean circular preview on an operator's monitor, capture a standard domemaster recording straight to disk, or hand the texture off to existing venue playback software. Its cost hinges on how you populate that master: a single-pass ray-traced fisheye takes one draw, while a rasterized cubemap takes five full scene renders before the projector passes even begin, and screen-space post effects still struggle across wide-angle distortion or cube seams.

Method 2 · DirectRender straight from each projector camera

The direct ("dome-native") approach treats every physical projector on the rim as an ordinary 3D camera in the scene. Instead of constructing a full-hemisphere master first, the engine renders the scene directly from each projector's calibrated pose into a dedicated render target, then passes that target through a lightweight warp-and-blend quad: 

update the scene
for each projector:
  set its calibrated camera
  render the scene into its target
  draw its target through the warp, blend, and color correction

When an installation is built specifically for one room and never needs to export a touring domemaster file, this keeps the math refreshingly straightforward. Each camera's frustum tightly hugs the portion of the dome that projector illuminates, so frustum culling and level of detail automatically skip geometry outside its beam. That does mean paying for one complete scene render per projector every frame, which adds up fast once a theater climbs to four or six channels.

Aside · Skipping the fisheye still uses render targets

Even in a direct pipeline, Three.js or WebGPU still renders into offscreen textures first because the final warp mesh and edge-blend mask need a texture to sample. What changes is the geometry of those textures: one shared hemisphere master versus one frustum texture per projector.

CostCounting shaded pixels and render passes

The final warp-and-blend pass is dirt cheap: just a texture sample and a couple of multiplies per projector pixel. What actually dominates frame time is shading the 3D scene itself (PBR materials, lighting, particles, shadows, transparency, and post-processing). A practical first sanity check is simply counting how many pixels receive full scene shading every frame under each design.

At the default settings above, three 1920×1200 projectors rendered directly shade 6.9 million scene pixels per frame. A 4K circular fisheye master (4,096 pixels in diameter, covering π/4 of the square) shades 13.2 million pixels. To match that 4K fisheye's angular sharpness at the center of each face, a cubemap needs faces 2,608 pixels across (2 × 4096 / π); counting the full top face plus the upper halves of the four side faces puts the cubemap at 20.4 million shaded pixels. At three WUXGA projectors, direct rendering shades roughly a third as many pixels as the cubemap even though overlap regions get rendered twice.

Pixel count is only half of the bill, though. Per-render work (CPU draw-call submission, frustum culling, shadow updates, and state switches) gets paid five times for a cubemap and once per projector on the direct path, while the fisheye's single pass only works if your scene can be ray traced or vertex-warped cleanly. Slide projectors up to 4 and bump projector size to 3840×2400 in the chart above and watch direct rendering shoot right past a 4K fisheye; drop fisheye master down to 2K and the shared path becomes dramatically cheaper.

ExamplesWhere each path pays off

One projector with a fisheye lens

Shared master. With a single fisheye projector on the floor, you already want one circular image, and that same render target doubles as your monitor preview and recording feed.

Three projectors, light geometry, each covering a narrow slice

Direct cameras. Each projector sees only a slice of the hemisphere, so culling drops out-of-view objects cleanly and three frustum renders beat five cube faces on both pixel count and pass count.

A heavy shader that covers the whole dome

Shared master. Running an expensive ray marcher or volumetric sky independently per projector wastes GPU time recomputing overlapping bands twice. One shared fisheye pass followed by cheap warp lookups shades every sky pixel once.

Effects tied to parts of the room

Direct cameras. Site-specific masks, architectural alignments, or horizon labels anchored to one wall are far easier to author and verify inside the exact projector view that displays them.

A touring show, or a mix of video and live layers

Master, or both. Touring shows need a portable domemaster that any theater can calibrate onto its own rig. Many live installations blend both worlds, decoding a shared background master while rendering crisp interactive 3D foregrounds per projector.

Calling a pipeline "dome-native" simply describes working in projector camera space from the start; whether it runs faster or slower than a shared master depends entirely on projector count, target resolution, and how heavy each render pass is.

CalibrationWhy a calibrated camera still needs a warp mesh

On paper, placing virtual cameras at the exact physical projector mounts sounds like it should make warp meshes obsolete. In a real room, optical lens distortion, off-axis lens shift, sagging brackets, and an imperfect plaster hemisphere still push pixels off target, so direct rendering carries forward the entire calibration bundle from part four: 

projector pose    → physical position and orientation
projector lens    → asymmetric frustum and optical distortion
warp mesh         → measured surface correction on the dome
blend mask        → light-linear overlap fade
color correction  → matched black floor, brightness, and white point

The virtual camera lines up the gross 3D perspective (usually using an asymmetric projection matrix to match off-center projector lens shift), and the final warp mesh absorbs the residual optical and structural errors measured in the room.

ImplementationA minimal Three.js loop

In Three.js, a direct multi-projector loop steps the simulation once so every channel freezes on the exact same instant, renders each projector's camera into its own offscreen target, and composites the warped outputs into side-by-side viewports (or spanning displays) on the main canvas: 

function frame(dt) {
  updateScene(dt);                         // one scene state for every projector

  for (const p of projectors) {
    renderer.setRenderTarget(p.target);    // this projector's camera view
    renderer.render(scene, p.camera);
  }

  renderer.setRenderTarget(null);          // real projector outputs
  renderer.setScissorTest(true);           // keep each clear inside its own viewport
  for (const p of projectors) {
    renderer.setViewport(p.viewport);      // this projector's slice of the output canvas
    renderer.setScissor(p.viewport);
    renderer.render(p.warpScene, orthoCamera);  // warp mesh textured with p.target
  }
  renderer.setScissorTest(false);
}

Here each p.camera is a PerspectiveCamera loaded with the asymmetric projection matrix from room calibration, and p.warpScene holds the calibrated warp mesh sampling p.target.texture through that channel's blend and color-correction shader. Whether this loop beats a shared master comes down to concrete profiling: scene complexity, projector count, master resolution, and whether you ever need to record or ship a portable domemaster.

EpilogueChoosing your intermediate deliberately

Once you trace the whole pipeline from end to end, both the fisheye domemaster and the cubemap reveal themselves as optional intermediates rather than rigid rules. Keep a shared master when you want easy operator previews, portable recordings, or single-pass full-sky shading; skip it and render per projector when a dedicated room's geometry makes direct frustums leaner. Either way, budget both shaded pixels and total render passes, and remember that physical calibration (warp, gamma-aware edge blending, and black-level matching) finishes the frame on every path.

The natural next step past our pixel counter in Figure 1 would be profiling a real Three.js scene with GPU timestamp queries around every pass, plotting the actual millisecond crossover between direct cameras, cubemaps, and ray-traced fisheyes as geometry complexity climbs.
