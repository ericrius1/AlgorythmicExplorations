Dome Projection · Part 3

Six Square Cameras

Why real-time 3D engines pack a dome sky into 90-degree cube faces instead of drawing a fisheye directly, and how the two formats convert into each other.

Scroll down to read ↓

Try asking a standard 3D rasterizer to draw a 180-degree fisheye directly and you hit a brick wall right away: straight edges in 3D space curve dramatically across a fisheye disk, and GPU triangle rasterizers only draw straight lines between vertices. The time-tested workaround is to bolt six ordinary 90-degree perspective cameras back-to-back at the center of the dome (one straight up, one straight down, and four facing around the horizon) so their square images tile the entire sphere without any curved projection math at all.

Up in the hero banner, each face of that cube gets its own subtle tint and square grid, with bright borders along the cube edges where one camera's frustum hands off to the next. Notice how one face brightens at a time in sequence, mimicking the pass-by-pass loop a real-time engine runs to fill a cubemap.

Picking between a circular fisheye from part two and a cubemap shapes your entire render path, preview workflow, and frame budget. Planetariums overwhelmingly exchange finished movies as fisheyes, while real-time 3D engines usually render to cubemaps first; plenty of production pipelines keep both around at different stages.

Step 1 · Same directionTwo ways to index a unit vector

Because both formats store the exact same hemisphere of directions, every lookup begins from the 3D vector pointing out from the dome center: 

dir = normalize(domePoint - domeCenter)

fisheye lookup:
  θ  = acos(dir.z)
  φ  = atan2(dir.y, dir.x)
  uv = 0.5 + 0.5 * ρ(θ) * (cos φ, −sin φ)

cubemap lookup:
  face = the axis where |dir| is largest
  st   = the other two components ÷ that largest one   // each −1..1

Look at how lean the cubemap math is: zero inverse trig, just find whichever component has the biggest magnitude and divide the other two by it. GPU texture units execute that lookup directly in silicon, so sampling a cubemap by 3D direction runs at essentially the same speed as sampling an ordinary 2D image.

Both sides above display the identical ring-and-meridian dome pattern. On the right, the five cube faces needed for an upper hemisphere unfold into a cross: the zenith face (+Z) sits in the middle with the four horizon faces hinged along its edges, matching the compass orientation of the fisheye on the left. Since the lower half of each side face looks below the horizon (the floor of the theater), those regions appear dimmed. Drag either handle, or jump straight to the zenith or horizon, to watch the two coordinate systems stay synced.

Near the zenith the rings look nearly circular in both layouts, while out toward the edges of a cube face the grid stretches noticeably. A flat 90-degree perspective camera spends pixels unevenly across angle: halfway along any face edge, a single degree of angular travel spans twice as many pixels as it does right at the face center. You can see the active face and its two perspective ratios updating live in the readout underneath.

Step 2 · FisheyeBuilt for delivery and playback

Packing the whole hemisphere into a single square image is why fulldome video standardized on the fisheye master. You can hand one video file to any planetarium, preview the whole sky on a normal monitor, and let the theater's own playback rig remap that single stream onto however many projectors hang on its walls.

Drawing a fisheye cleanly in real time takes extra effort, though. You inherit the zenith singularity and azimuth seam from part one, pixel density varies with the chosen lens law, and standard rasterization refuses to curve long triangle edges. Real-time fisheye generators therefore have to either ray trace the scene directly (the way part two's hero works in a single fragment shader), bend vertices in a vertex shader over densely tessellated meshes to hide chord error, or render an intermediate cubemap first and convert it.

Step 3 · CubemapBuilt for 3D engines

Every face of a cubemap is just an ordinary 90-degree pinhole camera, which means every existing feature of a 3D engine (frustum culling, shadow cascades, level of detail, PBR shaders, particle systems) works right out of the box without custom projection hacks. Better still, a hemisphere only needs the top face plus the upper halves of the four side faces: five render passes instead of six, with a scissor rectangle trimming the unused lower halves.

That convenience comes with two familiar headaches. First, rendering the scene five times per frame multiplies geometry submission and per-pass overhead dramatically. Second, any post-processing pass that depends on neighboring screen pixels (bloom, screen-space ambient occlusion, motion blur, temporal antialiasing) hits an abrupt wall at every cube edge because each face only knows about its own pixels, often leaving faint brightness steps right where two faces meet.

Aside · Cube maps, 1986

Ned Greene introduced cube environment maps in a 1986 paper as a way to capture every incoming light direction at a point using six perspective projections, mainly for reflections on curved surfaces. Graphics hardware took more than a decade to catch up: consumer GPUs gained dedicated cubemap texture sampling around 1999, and OpenGL folded it into the core specification with version 1.3 in 2001.

Step 4 · ConversionCombining both in one pipeline

Because converting from a cubemap to a fisheye is just a single full-screen texture lookup per pixel, many real-time dome rigs treat them as adjacent steps in one pipeline: render the 3D world into five cube faces, then either resample to a fisheye for recording and touring playback, or read the cubemap directly inside each projector's calibration pass. 

cubemap → fisheye:
  for each fisheye pixel:
    dir   = fisheyeUvToDirection(uv)
    color = sampleCube(dir)

fisheye → projector:
  for each projector pixel:
    dir   = calibratedPixelToDirection(pixel)
    color = sampleFisheye(directionToFisheyeUv(dir))

cubemap → projector:
  for each projector pixel:
    dir   = calibratedPixelToDirection(pixel)
    color = sampleCube(dir)

Skipping the intermediate fisheye in that third route eliminates one resampling pass (and one round of texture filtering blur) when you already control the projector outputs. Even when you do convert, a full-screen lookup pass is tiny next to shading complex 3D geometry.

The planetarium heroes across this very series lean on a cubemap for the exact same reason. Computing the layered 3D noise for the Milky Way and faint nebulae on every pixel of every frame would bog down mid-range GPUs, so at startup the shader bakes those diffuse clouds once into a 512×512-per-face cubemap. Each animation frame simply samples that cube by direction and adds procedural stars on top so pinpoint stars stay sharp at any screen resolution.

Step 5 · ChoosingMatching the format to the bottleneck

A film for festivals or touring domes

Fisheye. When a finished file has to travel between venues, a standard equidistant domemaster lets every theater plug it straight into its own calibration rig.

A real-time 3D world with meshes, shadows, and particles

Cubemap first. Five 90-degree perspective cameras keep the entire rendering engine intact. You can resample to a fisheye at the end or sample the cube straight in each projector pass.

One projector with a fisheye lens

Fisheye. The optical hardware already expects a circular frame. Going through a cubemap only makes sense if your engine needs standard pinhole cameras to render the scene.

A heavy shader that covers the whole dome

Fisheye, ray traced. If a fragment shader can evaluate a color straight from a direction vector in one pass, rendering the circular fisheye directly avoids shading extra corner pixels across five cube faces.

Video plates and live 3D layers together

Both. Pre-rendered backdrops typically show up as fisheye or equirectangular video while live foreground objects render into a cubemap. Composite everything by direction vector, then output whatever the venue expects.

Step 6 · DebuggingCatching flipped faces and bad seams

Each representation fails in its own signature way. Fisheye errors show up radially: bunched elevation rings, pinched zenith spokes, or a split azimuth seam. Cubemap errors show up right along the 90-degree borders: mismatched face orientations, an upside-down side panel, or a bright bloom seam stopping dead at an edge. Designing a dedicated test pattern for whichever format you are debugging catches those bugs in seconds: 

fisheye test pattern:
  azimuth labels around the circle
  rings at known zenith angles
  a visible seam and a center mark

cubemap test pattern:
  a bold name on every face
  an arrow showing each face's up direction
  labels straddling face borders
  a crisp horizon line across the four side faces

Always verify the rig with ugly labeled grids before loading finished art. A dense starfield or swirling nebula can easily disguise a mirrored cube face until someone tries reading a planet label during dress rehearsal.

EpilogueFrom one sky to multiple projectors

Both fisheyes and cubemaps are just containers for the same 3D direction vectors, and swapping between them costs a single texture fetch per pixel. Choosing between them comes down to where your work sits: delivery pipelines lean toward a single fisheye frame, rasterized 3D worlds lean toward standard 90-degree cube cameras, and hybrid setups comfortably mix the two. If I were extending Figure 1, I would render the hero's live starfield into five actual cubemap render targets side by side and turn on a per-face bloom pass so those cube-edge seams become impossible to miss.

Up to now we have pretended the dome receives one clean seamless image all by itself. Once a theater grows larger than a small inflatable tent, though, one projector can no longer light the entire ceiling, and multiple overlapping beams have to stitch that master back together in physical space.
