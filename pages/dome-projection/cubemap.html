Dome Projection · Part 3

Six Square Cameras

A cubemap stores the same directions as a fisheye, as ordinary 90-degree camera images. Which one to use depends on what's expensive.

Scroll down to read ↓

Part two described a fisheye master as a lookup table for directions. A cubemap is another lookup table for the same directions. Put six cameras at the centre of the dome, each with a 90-degree field of view: one pointing up, one down, and four around the horizon. Their square images together cover every direction exactly once.

In the hero, each face of that cube is tinted and drawn with its own grid, and the bright lines are the cube's edges, where one camera's view ends and the next one's begins. One face at a time lights up, the way a renderer draws them, one after another.

The choice between the two formats changes the render path, the preview, the calibration and the frame budget. A fisheye is usually how fulldome video is delivered. A cubemap is often the easier thing for a realtime 3D engine to render. Plenty of dome systems use both.

Step 1 · Same directionTwo addresses for one direction

Start from the direction, not the image format. To find the colour at a point on the dome, take the direction from the dome centre to that point, then ask either image for it: 

dir = normalize(domePoint - domeCenter)

fisheye lookup:
  θ  = acos(dir.z)
  φ  = atan2(dir.y, dir.x)
  uv = 0.5 + 0.5 * ρ(θ) * (cos φ, −sin φ)

cubemap lookup:
  face = the axis where |dir| is largest
  st   = the other two components ÷ that largest one   // each −1..1

The cubemap lookup has no trigonometry at all: find the biggest component, divide the other two by it, and you have a position on that face. GPUs do this inside the texture unit, so sampling a cubemap by direction costs about the same as sampling a flat texture.

Both layouts in the figure are filled with the same test pattern. The cross on the right is the five faces a dome needs: the top face in the middle, and each side face folded out from the edge it shares with the top, so it reads the same way round as the fisheye. Only the inner half of each side face is above the horizon; the dimmed halves would be the floor. Drag a point in either layout and the other follows.

Near the zenith the two agree closely. Toward the horizon the cube stretches the pattern, because a 90-degree perspective camera spreads its edges over more pixels than its centre: halfway along each edge of a face, a degree of travel toward the edge covers twice as many pixels as it does in the middle. The readout under the figure shows the face and the two divided components the lookup would use.

Step 2 · FisheyeWhen delivery matters

A fisheye master stores the whole dome in one image. It's the standard way to deliver fulldome video: a planetarium's playback and calibration system takes one master and maps it onto whatever projectors that room happens to have. It's easy to preview on a monitor and easy to record.

The costs: the centre is a singular point, the seam at φ = 0 needs the care from part one, and pixel density changes across the image with the lens law. And a rasterizer can't draw a true fisheye directly, because straight edges in the scene become curves in the image and triangles can't bend. So a realtime renderer either ray traces the fisheye, the way part two's hero does in one fragment shader, bends each vertex and subdivides finely enough to hide the error, or renders something else first and converts.

Step 3 · CubemapWhen rendering the scene is the hard part

Each face is an ordinary perspective camera with a 90-degree field of view, the one thing every engine already knows how to draw. Shadows, culling, level of detail, materials and most effects work per face without changes. A dome needs the top face and the upper halves of the four side faces: five renders, where a full sphere would need six.

The costs are the number of renders and the seams between them. Five scene renders is a lot more work than one, even though the side faces only need their top halves drawn, which a scissor rectangle handles. And anything that works in screen space, like bloom, motion blur, temporal antialiasing or ambient occlusion, can stop dead at a face edge, because each face is processed without knowing what's on its neighbour.

Aside · Cube maps, 1986

Ned Greene introduced cube environment maps in a 1986 paper, as a way to store everything visible from one point: six perspective images on the faces of a cube, read back by direction, mainly for reflections. Hardware caught up more than a decade later. Consumer GPUs started sampling cube maps around 1999, and they became part of core OpenGL in version 1.3, in 2001.

Step 4 · ConversionWhere the extra pass goes

The two formats are often two stages of one pipeline. A realtime scene renders to a cubemap, then one pass resamples the cubemap into a fisheye for recording or venue playback. The same cubemap can also be sampled straight from each projector's calibration pass: 

cubemap → fisheye:
  for each fisheye pixel:
    dir   = fisheyeUvToDirection(uv)
    color = sampleCube(dir)

fisheye → projector:
  for each projector pixel:
    dir   = calibratedPixelToDirection(pixel)
    color = sampleFisheye(directionToFisheyeUv(dir))

cubemap → projector:
  for each projector pixel:
    dir   = calibratedPixelToDirection(pixel)
    color = sampleCube(dir)

The last path skips the fisheye, but not the texture: the projector pass samples the cube instead. A conversion pass is one texture read per output pixel, which is small next to rendering a scene.

The heroes in this series use a cubemap the same way. The Milky Way and the nebulae are slow noise, too expensive to evaluate for every pixel of every frame, so they're baked once into a cubemap with 512-pixel faces, and the main pass reads it by direction. The stars are computed fresh each frame so they stay sharp at any resolution.

Step 5 · ChoosingPick the expensive part

A film for festivals or touring domes

Fisheye. The file has to travel, and every venue's system can map a domemaster through its own calibration without knowing anything about your scene.

A realtime 3D world with meshes, shadows and particles

Cubemap first. Five 90-degree cameras match how the engine already renders. Convert to a fisheye afterwards, or sample the cube in the projector pass.

One projector with a fisheye lens

Fisheye. The projector already wants the circular image. A cubemap only adds a conversion step, unless your engine has an easier time rendering one.

A heavy shader that covers the whole dome

Fisheye, ray traced. If the effect can compute a colour from a direction in one pass, render the fisheye directly. A cubemap would run it over five faces with overlapping borders and more pixels than the circle needs.

Video plates and live 3D layers together

Both. Pre-rendered material often arrives as fisheye or equirectangular video while the live layers render to a cube. Composite by direction, in whichever format loses the least detail, and output what the room needs.

Step 6 · DebuggingWhat breaks differently

Fisheye mistakes are radial: rings bunch up, spacing feels wrong toward the edge, the centre pinches, or the seam opens. Cubemap mistakes happen at face boundaries: edges don't match, a face is flipped or rotated, or a screen-space effect stops at a face edge. So each format wants its own test pattern: 

fisheye test pattern:
  azimuth labels round the circle
  rings at known zenith angles
  a visible seam and a centre mark

cubemap test pattern:
  a name on every face
  an arrow showing each face's up direction
  labels that cross face borders
  a horizon line around the four side faces

Test with labels before testing with art. A beautiful nebula can hide a flipped face right up until the show is in the room.

EpilogueWhat carries over

  • The direction is the contract. A fisheye and a cubemap are two ways of storing it, and either can be converted to the other with one lookup per pixel.
  • A cube face is a normal camera. That's the whole reason to use one: everything the engine already does keeps working.
  • Choose by cost. Delivery favours the fisheye, rendering a 3D scene favours the cube, and plenty of pipelines use both.
