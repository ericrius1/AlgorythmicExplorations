Small Minds · Part 3

Rolling Downhill

How a blank grid of random numbers teaches itself to fit a world: loss valleys, slopes as plain sensitivity, Adam, and backpropagation you can check on a pocket calculator.

Scroll down to read ↓

Imagine standing blindfolded on a foggy mountainside. Your goal is to reach the lowest valley floor, where the model's prediction error — its loss — is as small as possible. You cannot see a map of the whole mountain range. All you can feel is the tilt of the ground directly beneath your boots: uphill to your left, downhill slightly ahead and to the right.

So you take a careful step downhill. Pause, feel the slope under your boots again, take another step downhill, and repeat. That simple local rule — called gradient descent — is how virtually every neural network learns, whether it has three weights on a scratchpad or a trillion weights spread across a datacenter.

The contour map overhead shows three different hikers tackling the exact same narrow canyon: plain SGD (coral), Momentum (amber), and Adam (mint). Before diving into a 70,000-weight WebGPU Transformer, this essay builds the entire learning engine on small, transparent numbers you can verify by hand.

Step 1 · SensitivityWhat is a derivative? (No calculus required)

Think of a neural network as a giant audio mixing board covered in adjustable knobs — the weights and biases. At the top sits one glowing red meter displaying the loss L: how badly the network guessed on the current training examples. The goal of training is to twist the knobs until that red meter drops as close to zero as possible.

For any single knob w, ask one dead-simple question:

If knob w nudges upward by a tiny amount h (say +0.001), how much does the loss L change per unit of nudge? 

slope of L with respect to w   =   ∂L / ∂w   ≈   ( L(w + h) − L(w) ) / h

That ratio — rise over run, straight from ninth-grade algebra — is the entire meaning of a partial derivative ∂L/∂w! The curly ∂ simply warns you that there are other knobs on the board, and you are holding all of them still while testing knob w alone.

  • Suppose ∂L/∂w = +4.0. Twisting w up makes the error shoot up four times as fast. Therefore, to reduce the error, twist w down (subtract a small piece of +4.0).
  • Suppose ∂L/∂w = −2.5. Twisting w up makes the error go down. Subtracting a negative number (− (−2.5) = +2.5) automatically twists w up!
  • Suppose ∂L/∂w = 0.0. The ground is flat — you have reached the bottom of a bowl (or a ridge/saddle), so w stops moving.

Put every knob's slope into one vector — the gradient ∇L — and notice that it always points straight uphill (the direction that increases loss fastest). Walking in the exact opposite direction gives the universal learning update: 

w_new  =  w_old  −  lr · (∂L / ∂w)

Here lr is the learning rate (step size): a small positive number like 0.01 or 0.001 controlling how bold each downhill step is.

Step 2 · Navigating valleysLearning rates, ravines, Momentum, and Adam

If every loss surface looked like a round soup bowl, plain gradient descent would stroll straight to the center and call it a day. Real neural networks never live in round bowls. Some weights (like an output scale) sit on razor-steep cliffs where a tiny nudge swings the loss wildly, while other weights (like a rare word's embedding) sit on an almost flat plain. Put those two axes together and you get a long, steep, narrow ravine — steep side walls and a gently sloping canyon floor.

In a narrow ravine, plain gradient descent (SGD) hits a miserable Catch-22:

  • Pick a tiny learning rate and SGD safely avoids bouncing off the steep walls, yet crawls along the flat canyon floor at a glacial pace.
  • Pick a larger learning rate to move faster down the floor, and SGD slams back and forth across the narrow walls in violent zig-zags — push just a hair too far and each bounce lands higher than the last until the numbers explode to Infinity!

Two clean physics-inspired upgrades solve the ravine problem so well that almost every modern LLM uses them:

  1. Momentum (a heavy rolling ball): Instead of forgetting your previous step, keep a running velocity v = β·v + g (usually β ≈ 0.9). Back-and-forth zig-zags across the canyon walls point alternately + and −, so they cancel each other out in the running average! Meanwhile, the gentle consistent push down the canyon floor keeps adding in the same direction, building smooth speed straight toward the minimum.
  2. Adam (a personal speedometer for every single knob): Along with tracking the running average slope m (momentum), Adam also tracks the running average of the squared slope v = β₂·v + (1 − β₂)·g² for each individual weight, then divides the step by √v: 
    // Adam gives steep axes tiny steps and flat axes bolder steps:
m = β₁·m + (1 − β₁)·g            // smoothed direction (1st moment)
v = β₂·v + (1 − β₂)·g²           // typical squared slope (2nd moment)
w = w − lr · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε)        // divide by typical slope magnitude!
    If one axis has slopes 10× steeper than another, its √v is 10× larger too, so dividing by √v automatically rescales the ravine back into a comfortable round bowl!

Deep dive: what are the little hats m̂ and v̂ in Adam?

Both running averages m and v start at 0 on step 0. With β₂ = 0.999, after step 1 the running average is only 0.001 · g² — artificially 1,000× too small because it is still mostly mixed with its starting zero! Dividing by (1 − βt) ("bias correction") cancels that cold-start shrinkage exactly on early steps and smoothly approaches 1.0 as step t grows. You will see those exact lines — mHat = m / (1.0 - pow(b1, step)) — running inside the real GPU optimizer shader ops.wgsl in Part 6!

Step 3 · Credit assignmentThe chain rule and backpropagation by hand

Wait a second. Earlier, the essay showed how to estimate one slope by nudging a weight: (L(w + 0.001) − L(w)) / 0.001. Why not just use that nudge trick for every weight in a language model?

Do the math! Running the whole network once just to test one knob takes one forward pass. For the tiny 70,000-weight browser Transformer, measuring all 70,000 slopes by nudging them one by one would require 70,001 full forward passes just to take a single tiny learning step! For a 70-billion-parameter model, one step would take centuries.

Backpropagation is the brilliant algorithm (popularized in 1986 by David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and Ronald Williams) that computes every single slope for all 70,000 — or 70 billion — weights in ONE single reverse sweep costing roughly the same as two forward passes!

Its entire secret is one intuitive rule from calculus: the chain rule. Picture three interlocking bicycle gears — A, B, and C:

  • If turning gear A by 1 degree makes gear B turn by 3 degrees (∂B/∂A = 3),
  • and turning gear B by 1 degree makes gear C turn by 2 degrees (∂C/∂B = 2),
  • then turning gear A by 1 degree makes gear C turn by 3 × 2 = 6 degrees!
∂C / ∂A  =  (∂C / ∂B) · (∂B / ∂A)     // sensitivities along a chain simply multiply!

A neural network is nothing more than a chain of tiny, elementary math gates — multiply, add, SiLU bend, square the error — and every single gate knows its own dead-simple local sensitivity:

Simple gate Forward rule Local backward rule ("multiply incoming blame by…") Intuition
Add z = u + b ∂z/∂u = 1, ∂z/∂b = 1 Addition is a gradient distributor: it copies incoming blame unchanged to both branches!
Multiply u = w · x ∂u/∂w = x, ∂u/∂x = w Multiplication is a gradient swapper: each input's slope is the other input's value!
Squared error L = ½(ŷ − y)² ∂L/∂ŷ = (ŷ − y) Blame equals raw prediction error: too high → push down; too low → push up.
SiLU bend h = z · σ(z) ∂h/∂z = σ(z)·(1 + z·(1 − σ(z))) Scales incoming blame by the local steepness of the bend at z.

Every box displays two numbers: its blue forward value computed left-to-right, and its amber backward gradient (∂L/∂node) swept right-to-left by multiplying local slopes along the chain. Check the chain-rule bar along the bottom with a calculator, drag any weight slider to watch every number recompute instantly, and press take 1 step repeatedly to watch backpropagation train the circuit right in front of you!

Stop and check one concrete step by hand at the starting settings (x = 1.5, w₁ = 0.60, b₁ = −0.20, w₂ = −0.80, y = 1.20): 

// 1. FORWARD PASS (left → right):
u    = w₁ · x       = 0.60 × 1.50 = +0.90
z    = u + b₁       = 0.90 − 0.20 = +0.70
h    = SiLU(0.70)   = 0.70 × 0.668 = +0.468
ŷ    = w₂ · h       = −0.80 × 0.468 = −0.374     // way below target 1.20!
L    = ½(ŷ − y)²    = ½(−1.574)²    = 1.239

// 2. BACKWARD PASS (right → left, multiplying local slopes):
∂L/∂ŷ  = ŷ − y              = −1.574             // "prediction is too low, push ŷ UP!"
∂L/∂w₂ = (−1.574) × h       = −0.736             // negative grad → increase w₂ (make it less negative!)
∂L/∂h  = (−1.574) × w₂      = +1.259             // since w₂ is negative right now, bigger h makes ŷ WORSE!
∂L/∂z  = (+1.259) × SiLU'   = +1.037
∂L/∂b₁ = (+1.037) × 1       = +1.037             // add gate passes blame straight through
∂L/∂w₁ = (+1.037) × x       = +1.556             // positive grad → nudge w₁ down while w₂ is negative

Now click take 1 step five or six times and watch something delightful happen: as w₂ crosses from negative to positive, the sign of ∂L/∂w₁ flips automatically! Backpropagation instantly realizes that increasing w₁ now pushes ŷ upward toward 1.20, reverses course, and drives the loss all the way down to 0.000.

Every giant AI training run on Earth is literally this exact diagram scaled up from 3 parameters to millions or billions — with matrix multiplies replacing single scalar multiplies, and cross-entropy replacing squared error.

Step 4 · Reality checksMini-batches, overfitting, and why the practice test lies

Before moving to real GPU matrix math in Part 4, two practical realities govern every real training loop — including the live browser trainer you will run in Part 6:

1. Mini-batches (Stochastic Gradient Descent). If your training dataset holds a million sentences, computing the exact average loss over all one million before taking a single step is hopelessly slow. Instead, grab a small random handful — a mini-batch of, say, 4 or 64 snippets — compute the average gradient on that handful, take one optimizer step, and grab the next random handful. Each step is slightly noisy (which is why real training loss curves wiggle up and down instead of falling smoothly), yet taking 1,000 fast approximate steps beats taking 1 slow exact step by orders of magnitude — and that slight noise even helps shake weights out of shallow traps!

2. Memorizing vs. Understanding (Overfitting). Imagine a student who memorizes the exact answer key to a 20-question practice sheet (1-B, 2-D, 3-A…) without learning how to solve the problems. On the practice sheet, the student scores 100% (loss near zero!). On the real exam with fresh numbers, the student bombs.

Neural networks can cheat in the exact same way: given enough parameters and enough steps on a small corpus, a network can memorize every single sentence verbatim while failing miserably on new text. That failure mode is called overfitting.

The cure is scientific discipline: before training begins, lock away a held-out chunk of text (typically 10%–15%) called the validation set that the optimizer is never allowed to update weights on. Track two curves side by side: 

training loss dropping + validation loss dropping   ──▶  genuinely learning patterns!
training loss dropping + validation loss CLIMBING   ──▶  overfitting (memorizing noise!)

Every experiment in Part 7's Browser AutoResearcher uses that exact held-out validation exam so a bloated model that merely memorizes the training text gets rejected immediately.

Aside · Why small random weights beat all-zeros

What happens if you initialize every weight in a neural network to 0.0? Check Figure 2's multiply gate: ∂u/∂w = x and ∂u/∂x = w! If every weight starts at zero, every neuron in a hidden layer receives identical inputs, computes the identical output 0, and gets the exact same gradient on every step — all 64 neurons stay identical clones forever! Starting every weight as tiny random Gaussian static breaks that symmetry so each neuron can peel off and specialize in a different feature.
