Small Minds · Part 2

Numbers That Point

Vectors, dot products, matrices, and neurons — the compact toolkit of linear algebra that turns words into geometry and folds space to separate ideas.

Scroll down to read ↓

The phrase "linear algebra" sounds like an intimidating sophomore college course full of row-reduction worksheets and Greek letters. In practice, almost every modern neural network — from a 70,000-parameter browser model up to the largest AI systems ever built — leans on just three geometric ideas you can see with your own eyes in two dimensions:

  1. A vector: a list of numbers that acts like an arrow in space.
  2. A dot product: one multiply-and-add rule that measures how much two arrows point the same way.
  3. A matrix: a grid of numbers that stretches, rotates, and shears an entire space at once.

Add one tiny fourth ingredient — a smooth nonlinear bend so stacked layers do not collapse back into a single flat line — and you have the core mathematical vocabulary of deep learning. Watch the grid overhead smoothly morphing, shearing, and twisting: every frame is literally a 2 × 2 grid of four numbers moving every point on the plane in lockstep.

Step 1 · Lists as arrowsWhat is a vector?

If you have ever plotted a point (3, 2) on graph paper in algebra class, you have already used a vector. A vector is simply an ordered list of numbers — and it helps to think of it in two ways at the same time:

  • To a programmer or data scientist: a vector is a list of measurements or features describing one thing. A pet might be described by [size, fluffiness, energy] = [0.2, 0.9, 0.7]. A house might be [square_feet, bedrooms, age]. In Part 4, every letter or word in a language model gets its own list of 64 (or 128, or 4,096) learned coordinates!
  • To a geometer: a vector is an arrow starting at the origin (0, 0) and landing at those coordinates. Two similar things produce two arrows pointing into the same neighborhood of space; opposite concepts point in opposite directions.

Vectors obey two super-clean arithmetic rules, both done slot-by-slot: 

// 1. Adding two vectors (walking arrow a, then arrow b):
[2, 1] + [1, 3]  =  [2+1, 1+3]  =  [3, 4]

// 2. Scaling a vector by a plain number (stretching or flipping the arrow):
2.5 × [2, -1]    =  [5.0, -2.5]

// 3. Length (magnitude) — straight from the Pythagorean theorem!
|[3, 4]|  =  √(3² + 4²)  =  √(9 + 16)  =  5

Notice that Pythagoras does not care whether your list has 2 numbers or 4,096 numbers. The length of a 64-number vector is still just the square root of the sum of all 64 squares: √(v₁² + v₂² + … + v₆₄²). Humans cannot picture 64-dimensional space directly, yet every geometric rule — length, distance, angle, and shadow — works identically in 64 dimensions as it does on flat 2D graph paper.

Step 2 · Measuring alignmentThe dot product: AI's similarity detector

Suppose you have two vectors of the same length, a = [a₁, a₂] and b = [b₁, b₂]. How do you ask a single arithmetic question that checks whether they point in the same direction? You compute their dot product: multiply matching slots together, then add the products into one single number: 

a · b  =  a₁·b₁ + a₂·b₂          // in 2D
a · b  =  a₁·b₁ + a₂·b₂ + … + aₙ·bₙ   // in any number of dimensions!

At first that formula looks weirdly arbitrary — why multiply matching coordinates and add them? Because of a miraculous geometric identity you can verify with basic trigonometry (the Law of Cosines): 

a · b  =  |a| · |b| · cos(θ)

where θ is the angle between the two arrows! Look at what cos(θ) does as the angle changes:

  • When a and b point the exact same way (θ = 0°), cos(0°) = +1, so the dot product hits its maximum positive value +|a||b|.
  • When they sit at a right angle (θ = 90°, called orthogonal), cos(90°) = 0, so the dot product is exactly zero. In geometric terms, moving along a makes zero progress along b.
  • When they point in opposite directions (θ = 180°), cos(180°) = −1, so the dot product is maximally negative: −|a||b|.

Divide the dot product by both lengths — (a · b) / (|a||b|) = cos(θ) — and you strip away arrow size completely, leaving pure directional alignment between −1 and +1. Machine learning calls that cosine similarity.

Drag the tips of the blue arrow a and amber arrow b directly on the grid below. Watch the green shadow cast by a onto the line of b, and check that the simple multiply-and-add formula on the top right always equals |a|·|b|·cos(θ) on the bottom right to the last decimal place!

Try lining a up perpendicular to b (or press perpendicular (0)): the green shadow collapses to a single dot at the origin and a · b hits 0.00. Now drag a past 90° into the opposite half-plane: the shadow flips backward along the dashed line and the dot product turns negative.

Why is this single operation the beating heart of modern AI? Because every time a neural network asks a question, it uses a dot product:

  • Feature detection: Store a template pattern in a weight vector w and dot it with input x. High score means "this input matches my pattern!"
  • Attention (Part 5): Every token broadcasts a Query arrow ("what am I looking for?") and dots it against every earlier token's Key arrow ("what do I have?"). Large positive dot product = pay strong attention!
  • Predicting the next word: Take the Transformer's final thought arrow and dot it against every word vector in the vocabulary. The words pointing closest to the thought arrow get the highest logits!
Deep dive: why a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ equals |a||b|cos(θ)

Write both arrows using polar angles from the positive x-axis: a = [|a| cos α, |a| sin α] and b = [|b| cos β, |b| sin β]. Plug them into the multiply-and-add formula: 

a · b = (|a| cos α)(|b| cos β) + (|a| sin α)(|b| sin β)
      = |a| |b| (cos α cos β + sin α sin β)
      = |a| |b| cos(α − β)

That last step uses the classic high-school angle-difference identity cos(α − β) = cos α cos β + sin α sin β — the exact same trigonometry identity that will come back in Part 5 to power Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE)!

Step 3 · Grids that warp spaceWhat is a matrix, really?

One dot product asks one question about an input vector x. What if a neural layer wants to ask 64 different questions about x at the exact same time, producing a new 64-number vector of answers?

Stack those 64 weight vectors on top of each other as rows in a rectangular grid. That grid is a matrix W! Multiplying a matrix W (say 2 rows × 2 columns) by a vector x (2 numbers) simply runs one dot product per row: 

┌ a  b ┐ ┌ x₁ ┐   ┌ a·x₁ + b·x₂ ┐   ← dot(row 0, x)
└ c  d ┘ └ x₂ ┘ = └ c·x₁ + d·x₂ ┘   ← dot(row 1, x)

There is a second, equally useful way to read the exact same multiplication — by columns instead of rows: 

┌ a  b ┐ ┌ x₁ ┐       ┌ a ┐       ┌ b ┐
└ c  d ┘ └ x₂ ┘ = x₁ ·└ c ┘ + x₂ ·└ d ┘

Look closely at what that column view says:

  • Feed in the unit right-arrow î = [1, 0] and out pops the first column [a, c].
  • Feed in the unit up-arrow ĵ = [0, 1] and out pops the second column [b, d].
  • Every other vector [x₁, x₂] is carried smoothly along for the ride as x₁ steps along the new î plus x₂ steps along the new ĵ!

That means a matrix is not just a static spreadsheet of numbers — a matrix is a geometric verb. It takes the entire coordinate space and rotates it, stretches it, shears it, mirrors it, or squashes it onto a line, while always keeping grid lines straight, parallel, and evenly spaced (which is why this whole subject is called linear algebra).

Try warping space yourself in Figure 2 below. Move the four sliders a, b, c, d (or hit the preset buttons) and drag the dashed blue input vector x around the plane. Watch the bright pink output W·x move with the warped grid, and watch the green/amber basis arrows always match the columns of W.

Press squash (rank 1) and watch what happens: both columns line up along the exact same slope, so the entire 2D plane collapses onto a single 1D line and the shaded unit square loses all its area (det = 0). In neural networks, rectangular matrices do this deliberately all the time: a 128 × 64 matrix lifts a 64D vector up into a roomier 128D workspace (like an FFN "up-projection"), and a 64 × 128 matrix compresses it right back down to 64D!

And what about multiplying two matrices together, A × B? If B is a batch of vectors stacked side-by-side as columns (say, all 48 tokens in a sentence!), then A × B simply transforms every token vector in the sentence simultaneously — or equivalently, applies transformation B followed by transformation A. In Part 4, that single grid-times-grid operation turns out to account for over 95% of all arithmetic inside an LLM.

Aside · Matrix shapes rule: inner numbers must match!

Whenever you see a matrix multiply like [48, 64] × [64, 128] → [48, 128] later in this series, use one quick visual check: the inner two dimensions (64 and 64) must match because those are the lengths of the vectors being dotted together, and the outer two dimensions (48 and 128) give the shape of the result! Keeping track of tensor shapes like this is literally half the skill of reading any ML paper or GPU shader.

Step 4 · Adding a bendFrom matrices to neural networks (and why straight lines fail)

You are now one line of algebra away from a real artificial neuron. Take an input vector x, dot it with a weight vector w, add one shift number called a bias b (so the dividing boundary does not have to pass through (0, 0)), and pass the result through a nonlinear bend f: 

// One artificial neuron:
z = w · x + b                  // linear score (dot product + threshold shift)
a = activation(z)              // nonlinear bend (ReLU, tanh, GELU, or SiLU)

// A whole layer of H neurons at once (matrix × vector!):
h = activation(W · x + b)      // W has one row of weights per neuron

Wait — why bother with that activation(z) bend at the end? Why not just stack ten matrices in a row: W₁₀ · W₉ · … · W₂ · W₁ · x?

Because the product of two matrices is just another single matrix! Rotate a sheet of graph paper, then shear it, then stretch it: grid lines still stay perfectly straight and parallel. You could stack a thousand linear layers deep and the entire mega-network would mathematically collapse into one flat linear layer Wcombined · x. And a flat linear classifier can only ever slice space with one straight line (or flat plane in higher dimensions).

In 1969, Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert famously pointed out a dead-simple puzzle that no single straight line can ever solve: XOR ("exclusive OR"). Put blue dots in the bottom-left and top-right corners, and mint dots in the top-left and bottom-right corners. Try drawing one straight line across the square that puts both mint corners on one side and both blue corners on the other. It is geometrically impossible!

Now watch what happens the instant you insert a tiny nonlinear bend — like ReLU(z) = max(0, z) (which bends negative numbers flat to zero), tanh(z) (a smooth S-curve), or modern Transformers' favorite SiLU(z) = z / (1 + e−z) — between two matrix layers. Instead of merely stretching and rotating the grid, the hidden layer can crease, fold, and curl space itself!

Deep dive: the four famous activation curves (ReLU, tanh, GELU, SiLU)

Almost any smooth nonlinear curve works in theory (the Universal Approximation Theorem proves that even a single sufficiently wide hidden layer with a non-polynomial bend can approximate any continuous function on a bounded region). In practice, modern deep learning settled on a few favorites because of how nicely their slopes behave during training: 

ReLU(z) = max(0, z)                 // crisp hinge; fast, but negative side goes totally flat (slope 0)
tanh(z) = (eᶻ − e⁻ᶻ) / (eᶻ + e⁻ᶻ)   // smooth S-curve in (−1, +1); classic for tiny nets
GELU(z) ≈ 0.5·z·(1 + tanh(…))       // smooth probabilistic ReLU used in GPT-2 and BERT
SiLU(z) = z / (1 + e⁻ᶻ)             // "Swish": smooth dip below zero, used inside SwiGLU (Llama, Qwen, Small Minds)

Notice how SiLU(z) behaves almost like ReLU(z) for large positive and negative numbers, yet has a gentle, everywhere-differentiable curve around zero so gradients never hit a sudden dead cliff.

Looking aheadThe missing half: where do the weights come from?

Take a breath and notice how far two essays have already climbed. You now understand every single piece of the forward pass of a neural network:

  • Inputs and concepts are vectors (lists of coordinates / arrows in space).
  • Alignment and feature matching are dot products (multiply matching slots and add).
  • A layer of neurons is a matrix multiply (warping space / running many dot products at once) followed by a nonlinear bend that folds space.
  • At the top, raw scores (logits) turn into probabilities via softmax and get graded by cross-entropy surprise.

Wait — in Figure 3 above, those weights W₁, b₁, W₂, b₂ started out as random junk, yet within two seconds the network folded XOR and the concentric rings to 100% accuracy all by itself. How? Nobody typed in the right numbers for W₁. The network figured out which way to twist every single knob automatically by measuring slopes and rolling downhill.

Understanding that automatic knob-turning engine — gradient descent and backpropagation — is the heart of all machine learning, and it is surprisingly visual once you strip away scary calculus notation.
