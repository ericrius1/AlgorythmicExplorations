Small Minds · Part 4

The Grid That Thinks

How plain sentences become lists of vectors, why a language model is 95% matrix multiplication, and how 256 GPU threads team up inside tiny 16×16 squares of fast memory.

Scroll down to read ↓

Parts 1 through 3 built the core conceptual engine of machine learning on small, inspectable examples: predicting probability distributions in Part 1, representing ideas as vectors and transforming them with matrices in Part 2, and adjusting weights downhill via backpropagation in Part 3. Now comes the leap from toy 2D planes to a real modern Transformer running straight on your computer's graphics chip using pure WebGPU and hand-written WGSL compute shaders — no ML frameworks, no PyTorch in the browser, no hidden black boxes.

Two huge practical questions stand right at the entrance of any real language model:

  1. The language bridge: Matrix multipliers only speak floating-point numbers, never letters or words. How does a sentence like "The small cat saw a star" become a clean grid of numbers without losing meaning or exploding vocabulary size?
  2. The hardware bottleneck: Strip away the mystique around a Transformer and almost every layer — projecting tokens into attention queries, routing concepts through feed-forward memory, predicting the next symbol, and sending gradients backward — is one operation repeated hundreds of times per second: multiplying two dense grids of numbers. Why does a naive GPU shader run slowly, and what single memory trick makes it fly?

Watch the animation overhead: three grids of squares — activations A, weights B, and output C — pulsing as small 16 × 16 tiles glide across rows and columns while a real WebGPU compute shader benchmarks itself live on your GPU. Before unpacking that hardware race, start with how English enters matrix A in the first place.

Step 1 · Slicing languageTokenization and Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)

To feed text into a computer, you first have to chop the raw string into discrete chunks and assign each chunk an integer ID — a process called tokenization. Three obvious designs immediately suggest themselves, and comparing them reveals why modern AI systems settled on the third:

Strategy Example: "unbelievable!" Vocab size Strength & Weakness
Characters ['u','n','b','e','l','i','e','v','a','b','l','e','!'] ~98–256 Tiny vocabulary and can spell anything, but sequences become very long (13 slots for one word).
Whole words ['unbelievable', '!'] 500,000+ Short sequences, but crashes on typos ("unbeleivable"), slang, invented names, or code identifiers.
Subwords (BPE) ['un', 'believ', 'able', '!'] ~32K–128K Common words become 1 token; rare words cleanly break into meaningful reusable roots and pieces!

Almost every frontier language model (GPT, Claude, Llama, Qwen, Gemini) uses a dead-simple compression algorithm invented in 1994 called Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) to build its subword vocabulary automatically from raw text:

  1. Start with every single character (or raw UTF-8 byte) as its own token.
  2. Scan the entire training text and count every adjacent pair of tokens.
  3. Take the single most frequent pair (for instance 't' + 'h') and mint a brand-new token ID for "th", replacing every adjacent 't','h' across the text with "th".
  4. Repeat step 2 and 3 — hundreds of times for a small demo, or 50,000 to 100,000 times for a frontier model!

Walk through four merges on a miniature example — "low lower lowest": 

start:   l o w · l o w e r · l o w e s t          (16 tokens)
merge 1: 'l'+'o' → "lo" (×3)    ⇒  lo w · lo w e r · lo w e s t
merge 2: "lo"+'w' → "low" (×3)  ⇒  low · low e r · low e s t
merge 3: "low"+'e' → "lowe" (×2)⇒  low · lowe r · lowe s t   (8 tokens — 2× shorter!)

Notice the elegance: without anyone teaching the tokenizer a word of English linguistics, pure frequency counting naturally discovers roots ("low"), suffixes ("ing", "tion", "able"), common spaces, and code keywords!

Aside · Why famous AI models struggled to count the "r"s in "strawberry"

Subword tokenization solves sequence length so well that it creates a hilarious blind spot. To a BPE tokenizer, the word "strawberry" is often not ten letters — it might arrive as just two or three integer IDs like ["str", "aw", "berry"] (or even one single ID!). Asking a model to count individual letters inside a fused token is like asking a human to count how many brush strokes make up a Chinese character from memory without looking at it. The interactive browser trainer in Parts 6 through 8 sidesteps that quirk entirely by using a crisp 98-token printable-ASCII vocabulary (<bos>, <eos>, newline, plus ASCII 32–126): that keeps the final output matrix tiny ([N, 98] instead of [N, 100000]) so a full Transformer can train live at thousands of tokens per second on a browser GPU while spelling arbitrary English, punctuation, and chord progressions letter-by-letter!

Step 2 · IDs into arrowsEmbeddings: a learnable dictionary of meaning

Suppose tokenization turns "cat sat" into integer IDs [14, 72, 3, 55]. A beginner's natural instinct is to feed those integers straight into a neural network. Why doesn't that work?

Because token IDs are arbitrary locker numbers, not measurements! If 'a' is ID 68, 'b' is ID 69, and 'z' is ID 93, feeding raw integers tells the matrix math that 'z' is "37% larger" than 'a', or that 'b' sits halfway between 'a' and 'c' in meaning — total nonsense! Worse, in a 50,000-word BPE vocabulary, word #4102 ("guitar") and word #4103 ("Tuesday") have nothing in common.

The fix is an embedding table W_E: a big rectangular matrix with one row per token in the vocabulary (V rows) and D columns (called dModel — 64 in Part 6's browser trainer, 128 in Part 8's pretrained companions, up to 12,288 in giant frontier models). 

// Embedding lookup: simply fetch row [token_id] from table W_E[V × D]
token ID  0 ("<bos>") ──▶ row 0  of W_E: [ +0.12, -0.44, +0.08, … ]   (D numbers)
token ID 35 ("A")     ──▶ row 35 of W_E: [ -0.31, +0.19, +0.62, … ]   (D numbers)
token ID 68 ("a")     ──▶ row 68 of W_E: [ -0.28, +0.22, +0.59, … ]   (D numbers)

Look closely at rows 35 and 68 above. On step 0 of training, every row of W_E is filled with random Gaussian numbers — every token is dropped at a random spot in D-dimensional space. As backpropagation runs (Part 3), W_E is treated like any other weight matrix and nudged by gradients on every step! Tokens that play similar roles in text — vowels together, digits together, punctuation together, or in word-level models cat / kitten / dog and Paris / London / Tokyo — receive similar gradients and drift into tight geometric neighborhoods.

In large word-level models, that geometry becomes famously algebraic: directions in embedding space start encoding real concepts like gender, tense, or geography, so vector arithmetic like vec("king") − vec("man") + vec("woman") lands right next to vec("queen")! And mathematically, looking up row id in W_E is identical to multiplying a "one-hot" vector (all zeros except a single 1 in slot id) by W_E — just without wasting time multiplying by all the zeros.

Once a 48-token sentence looks up its 48 rows from W_E, the entire sentence is now a crisp [48 × 64] matrix of floating-point numbers: 48 rows (one per position in the sentence), 64 columns (each token's coordinate arrow). Now every layer of the Transformer simply transforms that [48 × 64] grid by multiplying it with weight matrices!

Step 3 · Hardware realityWhy GPUs wait on memory, not math

Before writing a single Transformer layer, pause and ask: why does modern AI run on graphics cards (GPUs) instead of ordinary CPUs?

Compare the two chips like two very different kitchens:

  • A CPU is a team of 8–16 Michelin-star chefs. Each chef is blazingly fast, handles tricky branching recipes (if/else, pointer chasing, operating-system tasks), and has a huge private counter of ultra-fast cache memory right next to them.
  • A GPU is a stadium holding thousands of line cooks working in lockstep. Each cook is slower individually and terrible at unpredictable branching, yet all thousands of them can chop identical vegetables at the exact same millisecond. That design was built to shade millions of video-game pixels 60 times a second — and by pure mathematical luck, computing every cell C[row, col] of a matrix multiplication is 100% independent, just like pixels!

There is, however, one brutal bottleneck inside every GPU on Earth — from a phone chip to an NVIDIA H100: math is ridiculously cheap, while moving numbers from memory is agonizingly slow.

Picture a chef who can chop 1,000 carrots per second, except every single carrot lives in a pantry across the street and only one slow runner fetches them two at a time. The chef spends 99% of the day standing idle staring out the window! Computer architects measure this balance with a single number called arithmetic intensity: how many floating-point math operations (FLOPs) you perform for every byte hauled across the main memory bus (VRAM or unified DRAM).

Check the arithmetic intensity of the textbook matrix multiply loop — assigning one GPU thread to compute each output cell C[row, col] as the dot product of row row of A and column col of B: 

// Naive WGSL kernel (matmul_naive in matmul.wgsl):
// Every thread computes one dot product straight out of global DRAM
for (var k = 0u; k < dims.K; k++) {
  acc = fma(A[row * dims.K + k], B[k * dims.N + col], acc);
}

Count the math and the memory traffic for one single iteration of that inner loop (fma stands for fused multiply-add: a * b + acc): 

math performed  = 1 multiply + 1 add                 = 2 FLOPs
bytes fetched   = 1 float from A (4 B) + 1 from B (4 B) = 8 bytes
arithmetic intensity = 2 FLOPs / 8 bytes             = 0.25 FLOP/byte!

Wait — a modern laptop GPU can often execute 30 to 80 FLOPs for every single byte its memory bus can deliver! At 0.25 FLOP/byte, your graphics chip's arithmetic units sit idle well over 95% of the time waiting for numbers to crawl across the memory wires. Even worse, look at how wasteful those reads are: for a 1024 × 1024 matrix, 1,024 different threads all read the exact same row of A from slow main memory 1,024 separate times!

Aside · How video games accidentally built the AI era

In 2012, Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton trained a deep vision network (AlexNet) on two consumer NVIDIA GTX 580 gaming graphics cards tucked into a bedroom desktop — shattering the ImageNet benchmark by more than 10 percentage points. The hardware was designed to shade dragons and explosions in 3D games, yet its massively parallel matrix pipes turned out to be the exact engine neural networks had been waiting for since 1986.

Step 4 · Shared memorySixteen-by-sixteen cooperation

The trick that breaks the memory wall is pure teamwork. Neighboring output cells C[row, col] inside a 16 × 16 square block all need the exact same 16 rows of A and the exact same 16 columns of B.

Inside a GPU, threads are launched in tiny neighborhoods called workgroups. All 256 threads in a 16 × 16 workgroup live on the same compute core and share a tiny, blazing-fast patch of on-chip SRAM (declared in WGSL as var<workgroup>) that sits micrometers away from the math circuits — orders of magnitude faster than global VRAM.

Instead of each thread marching all the way across global memory alone, the 256 threads march in 16 × 16 tiles:

  1. Cooperative fetch: Each thread loads just one float from A into tileA and just one float from B into tileB. Together, the 256 threads populate both 256-float scratchpads in parallel.
  2. First barrier (workgroupBarrier()): Every thread pauses until all 256 teammates have finished setting their numbers on the shared counter.
  3. Fast SRAM math: Now every thread runs 16 fused multiply-adds using its row of tileA and column of tileB — zero global memory reads!
  4. Second barrier (workgroupBarrier()): Everyone finishes reading the current tile before anyone overwrites it with the next tile along K. 
var<workgroup> tileA: array<f32, 256>;
var<workgroup> tileB: array<f32, 256>;

for (var t = 0u; t < numTiles; t++) {
  tileA[ly * 16u + lx] = A[row * K + t * 16u + lx];        // 1 read from A
  tileB[ly * 16u + lx] = B[(t * 16u + ly) * N + col];      // 1 read from B
  workgroupBarrier();                                      // wait until tile is loaded
  for (var k = 0u; k < 16u; k++) {
    acc = fma(tileA[ly * 16u + k], tileB[k * 16u + lx], acc); // 16 FMAs from fast SRAM!
  }
  workgroupBarrier();                                      // wait before loading next tile
}

Re-run the arithmetic intensity math on this tiled inner loop: each thread still fetches only 8 bytes from global VRAM per tile, yet now executes 16 fused multiply-adds (32 FLOPs) out of fast on-chip SRAM before touching VRAM again! Global memory traffic instantly drops by 16×, jumping arithmetic intensity from 0.25 straight up to 4.0 FLOP/byte.

Deep dive: why three matmul layouts live in matmul.wgsl (and eliminate every transpose)

Every linear layer in a neural network stores its weight matrix W with shape [D_out, D_in] (one row per output neuron) and multiplies a batch of token vectors X of shape [N, D_in] by W's transpose: Y = X · Wᵀ, giving shape [N, D_out].

When backpropagation runs in Part 6, that same linear layer needs two more matrix multiplies to send gradients backward (the matrix version of Part 3's multiply-gate swap!): 

forward:          Y  = X   · Wᵀ     // [N, D_in]  × [D_out, D_in]ᵀ → [N, D_out]   (matmul_nt)
activation grad:  dX = dY  · W      // [N, D_out] × [D_out, D_in]  → [N, D_in]    (matmul_tiled)
weight grad:      dW += dYᵀ · X     // [N, D_out]ᵀ × [N, D_in]     → [D_out, D_in](matmul_tn, alpha=1)

Writing those three tiled entry points directly in matmul.wgsl — where the tile loader simply swaps indices while filling tileA or tileB — means the GPU never wastes memory or time copying transposed buffers during training. Production libraries like cuBLAS push this same idea further with register-level sub-tiling (each thread computes an 8 × 8 micro-tile) and dedicated Tensor Cores that multiply 16 × 16 half-precision tiles in a single hardware clock cycle!

The engine is hummingWhat tokens still cannot do yet

Pause and check what is now alive on your GPU:

  • Raw text slices cleanly into token IDs (BPE in frontier models; 98-symbol ASCII in the live browser trainer).
  • Each token ID fetches its learned vector arrow from the embedding table W_E, turning a sentence into a [T × D] grid of numbers.
  • A pure-WebGPU 16 × 16 tiled SGEMM engine transforms those grids in parallel in all three forward and backward orientations.

Yet one giant limitation still remains: right after the embedding lookup, every token row in that [T × D] matrix sits in complete isolation. The vector for "bank" looks identical in "river bank" and "bank vault"; the vector for "its" has no clue whether it refers back to "cat" or "star". To understand a sentence, tokens need a fast, differentiable way to look backward across the sequence and talk to one another.
