Small Minds · Part 1

The Guessing Game

Before a single neuron or matrix enters the room, a language model is a remarkably simple machine: guess the next symbol, measure how surprised you were, and roll weighted dice.

Scroll down to read ↓

Modern AI chat assistants feel almost magical when you first talk to them. You ask a question about biology, drop in a half-broken line of code, or ask for a bedtime story about a sleepy octopus, and fluent paragraphs stream across the screen. It is natural to imagine a giant encyclopedia hidden inside the server, or thousands of hand-written grammar rules stitched together by engineers.

Neither picture is right. Strip away the chat window, the system prompt, and the billion-dollar server farms, and every large language model on Earth is playing one single game over and over again:

Read the text so far, assign a probability to every possible next piece of text, pick one, glue it onto the end, and repeat.

Watch the figure overhead for a few seconds. A line of poetry slides in from the left, one character at a time. At every step the machine pauses on the empty box, scores every character in its alphabet, turns those scores into percentages that add up to 100%, and picks the next character. There is no separate "grammar module," no built-in dictionary of facts, and no separate "reasoning chip." Fluency, rhyme, coding skill, and world knowledge all have to emerge as side effects of becoming extraordinarily good at predicting what comes next.

This eight-part series builds a complete modern language model from absolute zero right inside your browser tab — no Python frameworks, no hidden cloud servers, and no prior machine-learning or linear-algebra background assumed. The path climbs one honest rung at a time:

Step 1 · Before neural networksA language model made of pure counting

Before inventing artificial brains, ask the simplest possible engineering question: how far can you get at predicting text using nothing more than a tally sheet?

Suppose you open a play by Shakespeare and walk through it one character at a time with a pencil and a giant grid. Every time you see the letter t followed immediately by h, you add one tally mark to the box (t → h). Every time t is followed by o, you add a mark to (t → o). After a few thousand characters, divide every row by its row total so the counts become probabilities: 

// Example row after tallying a small English passage:
after "t":   'h' 38%   ' ' 21%   'o' 12%   'e' 11%   'i' 7%   'r' 5%   …   'z' 0%
after "q":   'u' 99%   ' '  1%   (almost everything else: 0%)
after "th":  'e' 52%   'a' 16%   'i' 11%   'o'  9%   'r' 5%   …

That tiny lookup table is called an n-gram model (or a Markov chain of order k, where k is how many previous characters you look at before guessing). Notice how embarrassingly simple it is: zero calculus, zero matrices, zero GPUs — just counting how often each suffix followed each prefix in the training text.

Even so, it already behaves like a miniature language model! Look at the live table builder below. Pick one of the four built-in texts (Tiny Shakespeare, Nature Haiku, Jazz Progressions, or Companion Dialogue), drag the context k slider from 1 up to 5, and click generate or step 1 char. You can also type your own prefix into the box to inspect the exact tally marks sitting behind that prefix.

Play with context k carefully, because the progression from 1 to 5 reveals the central tension of all machine learning:

  • At k = 1 (looking only at the single previous letter), the output looks like alien gibberish that vaguely resembles English syllable patterns: vowels alternate with consonants, q drags u along, spaces show up every few letters, yet real words rarely survive.
  • At k = 2 and k = 3, recognizable fragments click into focus — the, and, soft, Cmaj7 -> — because three characters of context is enough to finish common short words and chord symbols.
  • Push to k = 5 on these short texts and something suspicious happens: the output suddenly reads like flawless Shakespeare or haiku — because it has stopped generating and started copy-pasting! Almost every 5-character prefix in a 2,000-character passage appeared only once, so there is only a single candidate with 100% probability and the table simply replays the original text verbatim.

Even worse, type a slightly unusual 4-letter prefix like star or moon into the prefix box when Tiny Shakespeare is selected and k = 4. The left panel flashes red: zero hits. The counting table is completely blind to any sequence it did not witness byte-for-byte during training. It has no idea that "The little cat" and "The small kitten" mean almost the same thing.

Aside · Claude Shannon's 1948 parlor game

In his landmark 1948 paper A Mathematical Theory of Communication, Claude Shannon built English Markov approximations by hand — opening a book at random, searching for the last two letters he had written, copying down whatever letter came next, and repeating! By third-order characters he already got eerie pseudo-English like "IN NO IST LAT WHEY CRATICT FROURE BIRS GROCID", and by second-order words he got "THE HEAD AND IN FRONTAL ATTACK ON AN ENGLISH WRITER THAT THE CHARACTER OF THIS POINT IS THEREFORE ANOTHER METHOD." Shannon was measuring how predictable English is — and in the process invented the mathematical foundation of both data compression and modern LLM training.

Step 2 · The combinatorial wallWhy counting tables die around k = 6

At first glance, the fix for a blind counting table sounds obvious: "Just feed it the entire internet and make k = 50 so it can see the whole sentence!" Look at the red bars on the right side of Figure 1 to see why that plan crashes straight into the laws of physics.

The browser trainer later in this series uses a compact alphabet of 98 symbols (printable English letters, digits, punctuation, newline, and start/end markers). How many cells does a complete lookup table need if you condition on k previous characters? There are 98k possible prefixes, and each prefix needs 98 probabilities for the next character, giving 98k+1 cells in total:

Context k Example prefix Table cells 98k+1 Rough physical scale
k = 1 "t" 9,604 Fits in 38 KB (smaller than one photo)
k = 2 "th" 941,192 ~3.7 MB (a single MP3 song)
k = 3 "the" 92,236,816 ~370 MB (already heavy for a phone)
k = 4 "the " 9.0 × 109 ~36 GB (more RAM than most laptops)
k = 10 "Once upon " 8.0 × 1021 More cells than grains of sand on Earth
k = 48 One short sentence 3.7 × 1097 1017 times more cells than atoms in the observable universe

That explosion is called the curse of dimensionality. Even if you owned a hard drive the size of a galaxy, you could never fill those boxes because almost every 12-word English sentence you speak today has never been written before in human history.

This is the exact moment neural networks earn their keep. Instead of memorizing every possible prefix in a separate cubbyhole, a neural network tries to build a smooth, compressed function that takes a sequence of symbols, discovers shared structure (that cat and dog act similarly, that he refers back to a masculine subject, that an opening parenthesis expects a closing one), and outputs a probability distribution over the next symbol — even for sentences it has never seen before.

Before building that function in Part 2, however, two crucial pieces of machinery belong right here on the ground floor: how to score a probability guess, and how raw numbers turn into probabilities in the first place.

Step 3 · Grading a guesserSurprise, logarithms, and cross-entropy loss

Suppose two weather forecasters predict rain tomorrow. Forecaster A says "90% chance of rain." Forecaster B says "55% chance of rain." The next morning it pours. Both forecasters leaned toward rain, yet Forecaster A clearly made a better prediction. What if it had stayed sunny instead? Forecaster A, who was 90% sure of rain and gave sunshine only 10%, deserves a much harsher penalty than Forecaster B.

A language model needs a single score — called the loss — that grades how well its predicted probability p matched the symbol that actually appeared next. What properties should a fair penalty score have?

  1. If the model puts p = 1.0 (100% probability) on the true next letter, its error should be 0 — zero surprise, nothing left to improve.
  2. If the model puts a tiny probability like p = 0.01 on the true letter, the penalty should be huge, climbing toward infinity as p → 0 (being 100% confident and dead wrong should hurt infinitely!).
  3. When predicting two independent letters in a row with probabilities p₁ and p₂, the combined probability multiplies (p₁ × p₂), and it would be wonderfully clean if the total surprise simply added (surprise(p₁) + surprise(p₂)).

In high-school algebra, there is only one family of functions that turns multiplication into addition: the logarithm (log(a × b) = log(a) + log(b))! Since probabilities sit between 0 and 1, their logarithms are negative, so putting a minus sign in front flips them positive: 

surprise in bits = −log₂(p)          // how many yes/no questions to identify the symbol
loss in nats     = −ln(p)            // natural log (base e ≈ 2.718), preferred for calculus
bits             = nats / ln(2)      // divide by 0.6931 to convert nats → bits

Pause and check a few numbers in your head — logarithms stop feeling abstract the instant you plug in coin flips: 

p = 1.00   (certain)          → −log₂(1.00) = 0.00 bits   (0.00 nats)   // zero surprise
p = 0.50   (fair coin)        → −log₂(0.50) = 1.00 bit    (0.69 nats)   // 1 yes/no question
p = 0.25   (1 in 4)           → −log₂(0.25) = 2.00 bits   (1.39 nats)   // 2 yes/no questions
p = 0.125  (1 in 8)           → −log₂(0.125)= 3.00 bits   (2.08 nats)   // 3 yes/no questions
p = 0.01   (1 in 100)         → −log₂(0.01) = 6.64 bits   (4.61 nats)   // shocked!
p = 1/98   (blind uniform)    → −log₂(1/98) = 6.61 bits   (4.58 nats)   // untrained model baseline

Look at that last line: 4.58 nats (or 6.61 bits). Remember that number! When an untrained 98-character neural network initializes with random noise in Part 6, it assigns roughly 1/98 ≈ 1.02% probability to every character, so its starting loss chart always begins right around 4.58 nats. If a freshly built model does not start near ln(vocab_size), you immediately know there is a bug in your initialization before running a single training step!

Averaging −ln(ptrue) over every character in a batch of text is called cross-entropy loss (or negative log-likelihood). Lower cross-entropy means the model puts higher probability on reality, is less surprised by real English, and — by Shannon's coding theorem — could literally compress the text into fewer bits per character.

Deep dive: why compression and intelligence are the same metric

Using a trick called arithmetic coding, any predictor that assigns probability pi to the true i-th character can losslessly pack an entire book into roughly Σ −log₂(pi) bits, and decompress it back character-for-character using the exact same model. An uncompressed ASCII file spends 8 full bits on every letter. A naive frequency table drops English to ~4.5 bits per character. The tiny 780K-parameter browser models shipped in Part 8 reach ~1.29 bits per byte on cozy English stories, and giant frontier LLMs push below 0.7 bits per byte. To compress a murder mystery down to 0.7 bits per character without losing a word, the compressor eventually has to deduce who the killer is before the final chapter reveals it!

Step 4 · Scores into oddsLogits, Softmax, Temperature, and Top-p

Inside a neural network, layers do not naturally output clean percentages between 0% and 100% that sum to 1. They just multiply and add numbers, spitting out one arbitrary real number per vocabulary symbol — maybe +2.6 for 'h', +1.3 for 'o', −0.3 for 'a', and −1.8 for 'z'. Those raw, unconstrained scores are called logits.

How do you turn any list of raw logits (negative, positive, huge, tiny) into a valid probability distribution? Three steps, packed into a single function called softmax:

  1. Exponentiate every score: replace each logit zi with ezi (where e ≈ 2.71828). Why ez? Because ez is strictly positive for every real number (even e−1.8 = 0.165 > 0), and it widens the lead of higher scores smoothly.
  2. Sum all the exponentials: compute Z = Σ ezj.
  3. Divide each entry by the sum: pi = ezi / Z. Now every probability sits cleanly between 0 and 1, and all of them add up to exactly 1.0 (100%). 
// Worked example with 4 scores:  z = [ 2.0,  1.0,  0.0, -1.0 ]
exp(z)    = [ 7.389, 2.718, 1.000, 0.368 ]
sum       = 7.389 + 2.718 + 1.000 + 0.368 = 11.475
softmax(z)= [ 64.4%, 23.7%,  8.7%,  3.2% ]   // sums to 100.0%

There is one extra dial you have probably seen in AI playgrounds: temperature T. Before exponentiating, divide every logit by T: softmax(z / T).

  • When T = 1.0, nothing changes — you sample straight from the model's learned distribution.
  • When T < 1.0 (say T = 0.25), dividing by a small number stretches the gaps between logits by 4×. After exponentiating, the winner steals almost all the probability mass. As T → 0, the model becomes deterministic ("greedy decoding"), always picking the single highest-scoring token — safe and factual, but often repetitive.
  • When T > 1.0 (say T = 2.0), dividing shrinks all gaps toward zero. Since e0 = 1, every token starts looking equally likely — creative at T ≈ 1.1, chaotic word salad at T = 2.5.

Even at moderate temperature, a 50,000-word vocabulary has a dangerous "long tail": 49,950 nonsense tokens each holding 0.0002% probability can add up to a 10% chance of derailing a sentence with a bizarre random word. Top-p (nucleus) sampling fixes that cleanly: sort tokens from highest probability to lowest, walk down the list until the cumulative probability crosses p (typically 90%), and chop off the entire tail before rolling the dice.

Deep dive: the numerical stability trick inside every real softmax

A computer stores 32-bit floats up to about 3.4 × 1038. If a logit ever reaches 90, computing exp(90) overflows straight to Infinity, and Infinity / Infinity collapses into NaN (Not a Number), poisoning the entire model.

Fortunately, subtracting the same constant m from every logit does not change softmax at all, because ezi − m / Σ ezj − m factors out e−m from top and bottom and cancels it identically! So every real GPU shader (including ops.wgsl and attention.wgsl in this series) first finds m = max(z) and computes exp(zi − m). The largest exponent is now always e0 = 1.0, making overflow mathematically impossible.

Putting it togetherFrom tally sheets to geometry

Look at what is already in place before writing a single neural equation: 

text so far  ──▶  [ some predictor ]  ──▶  raw logits z[0..V-1]
                                               │
                                               ▼  softmax(z / T)
                                          probabilities p[0..V-1]
                                          ├─ training: grade with loss = −ln(p[true])
                                          └─ chatting: filter by top-p and roll the dice

The whole outer shell of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and the WebGPU model in this series is already sitting right there in those four lines. Only the box in the middle — [ some predictor ] — needs to be replaced. A counting table fails inside that box because prefixes explode as 98k and never share knowledge between similar phrases.

To replace a lookup table with a smooth mathematical machine that generalizes to brand-new sentences, text has to stop being isolated labels and start living as geometry — points, arrows, angles, and spaces that can be stretched, rotated, and folded. That geometry takes only three simple building blocks: vectors, dot products, and matrices.
