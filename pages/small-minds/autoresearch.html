The Browser That Researches Itself

Bit-packed 4-bit inference on WebGPU, then a closed-loop autonomous researcher that evolves better Transformers right inside your tab.

Scroll down to read ↓

Training is compute-heavy because you process hundreds of tokens at once in wide matrix multiplies. Generating a reply one token at a time is the exact opposite: every decode step multiplies a single token vector by every giant weight matrix in the model. That turns matrix-matrix multiplication (GEMM) into matrix-vector multiplication (GEMV), where arithmetic intensity collapses back to 0.5 FLOP/byte and memory bandwidth becomes the absolute speed limit.

Step 1 · Smaller weights, faster tokensKV caches and 4-bit dequantization

Two hardware tricks make browser inference feel instantaneous. First, a KV cache stores every previous token's projected Key and Value vectors so generating token t + 1 only computes projections for the single newest token instead of re-running the whole prompt.

Second, if memory bandwidth is the bottleneck, ship fewer bits across the wire. Instead of storing every weight as a 32-bit float, we chop each row into groups of 32 weights, record one shared float scale per group, and pack four INT8 bytes or eight INT4 nibbles into a single u32 word. Inside the WGSL decode shader, each thread loads one u32, unpacks 8 weights in registers with bit shifts, multiplies by the group scale, and accumulates — cutting VRAM bandwidth by nearly 6.4× with tiny numerical error: 

// Unpack 8 signed 4-bit weights from a single u32 register word
let nib = (packedWord >> (lane * 4u)) & 0xFu;
let w   = f32(i32(nib) - 8) * groupScale;
acc = fma(w, x[col + lane], acc);

Look at the RMSE column alongside the memory bars: INT8 is virtually indistinguishable from FP32 (error in the third decimal place) at a quarter the memory, and even 4-bit weights reconstruct dot products closely enough that billion-parameter models fit comfortably inside a laptop's unified memory.

Step 2 · The research loopAutoresearch inside the browser

Once a full training run on your browser GPU takes less than a second, something remarkable becomes possible. Inspired by Andrej Karpathy's autoresearch, we can hand the steering wheel to an automated research loop right inside the tab:

  1. Start from a baseline Transformer genome and measure its held-out validation bits per byte (val_bpb).
  2. Propose one concrete scientific mutation — switching GeGLU to SwiGLU, testing Muon against AdamW, adding cosine warmdown, adjusting RoPE frequency base θ, widening dModel, or expanding the FFN ratio.
  3. Allocate a fresh pure-WebGPU model, train it for the exact same fixed step budget, and grade it on held-out validation tokens.
  4. If val_bpb drops, commit the new genome as the frontier; if it regresses, revert and log what we learned.

Below, that exact loop runs live on your GPU. Pick a search strategy — greedy Hill Climb, an exploration-aware UCB Bandit that tracks which mutation axes pay off most, or an Evolutionary pool that branches from the top-3 models — and hit auto-research to watch your browser discover better architectures on its own.

Watch the green frontier line step downward across trials. Almost every run independently rediscovers the same empirical truths modern LLM labs spent years finding: SwiGLU beats GeGLU at small widths, cosine warmdown shaves off late-step noise, and hotter early learning rates pay off hugely under tight compute budgets.

Why fixed-budget validation bits-per-byte is the cleanest compass

Comparing raw training loss across models is easy to fool — a wider model memorizes training batches faster even while overfitting. Splitting off a held-out 15% validation slice and measuring val_bpb = val_loss / ln(2) under an identical step budget forces every mutation to earn its keep through genuine generalization.
