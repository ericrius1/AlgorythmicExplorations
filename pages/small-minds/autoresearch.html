Step 1 · Two speeds of thoughtPrefill vs. Decode (and why chatting is memory-bound)

Have you ever noticed that an AI assistant can swallow a 50-page PDF in two seconds, yet still streams its reply at maybe 60 words per second? That gap is not artificial throttling — it is pure linear-algebra physics separating the two phases of LLM inference:

  • Phase 1 · Prefill (reading your prompt): Every token in your prompt is already known up front! Just like training in Part 6, all 1,000 prompt tokens march through every weight matrix together in big, dense matrix × matrix multiplies (GEMM). Part 4's 16 × 16 shared-memory tiling shines here, reusing every weight across hundreds of tokens at once. Prefill is compute-bound and blazingly fast.
  • Phase 2 · Decode (writing the reply): Generating a reply is strictly sequential (autoregressive). The model cannot even start thinking about token #12 until it finishes sampling token #11! So every single generated token pushes a single 1-row vector through every giant weight matrix in the network — turning matrix × matrix (GEMM) into matrix × vector (GEMV).

Check the arithmetic intensity of a 32-bit float matrix-vector multiply y = W · x: every single weight float in W (4 bytes) gets hauled all the way out of VRAM across the memory bus, used for one fused multiply-add (2 FLOPs), and immediately thrown away until the next token! That drops arithmetic intensity right back down to 0.5 FLOP/byte. 

// The hard physical speed limit of single-user LLM token generation:
max tokens / second  ≈  GPU memory bandwidth (GB/s)  ÷  total model size (GB)

// Example on a laptop with 150 GB/s memory bandwidth:
7B model in FP32 (28 GB)  ──▶  won't even fit in RAM (or crawls at ~5 tok/s)
7B model in INT4 (3.8 GB) ──▶  150 ÷ 3.8  ≈  ~39 tokens / second!

That single division explains almost every headline in local AI engineering: during token generation, your GPU's trillion-operation math cores are mostly bored waiting on VRAM wires. To generate tokens faster, you either avoid redundant work (KV caching) or ship dramatically fewer bytes across the wire (quantization).

Step 2 · Caching & shrinkingKV caches and 4-bit bit-packing

Trick 1: The KV Cache. Suppose your prompt is 200 tokens long and the model is about to generate token #201. Remember Part 5's causal mask: earlier tokens can never attend to later tokens! That means token #17's projected Key vector K[17] and Value vector V[17] are completely frozen forever the moment token #17 is first processed.

Without a cache, generating a 200-token reply recomputes K and V for every earlier token on every single step — wasting 1 + 2 + 3 + … + 200 ≈ 20,000 token passes instead of 200! A KV cache simply parks every past token's K and V vectors in a GPU buffer; each new decode step projects only the single newest token, appends its (K_new, V_new) to the cache, and attends over the stored history. (This is also why huge 100,000-token context windows eat gigabytes of VRAM — the GPU has to hold K and V for every token in every layer!)

Trick 2: Weight Quantization (FP32 → INT8 → INT4). Every weight trained in Part 6 is stored as a 32-bit floating-point number (f32, 4 bytes), capable of distinguishing billions of microscopic gradations like 0.14285715 vs. 0.14285719. Does a neural network actually need 7 decimal digits of precision on every weight just to predict the next word?

Not even close! Imagine measuring ingredients for soup: a laser micrometer accurate to 0.001 mm is pointless when rounding to 16 or 256 tidy steps gives practically the exact same bowl — especially because when you sum hundreds of rounded weights inside a dot product, small positive and negative rounding nudges largely cancel each other out.

There is just one catch: if a weight row contains 1,000 normal numbers near ±0.2 and one giant outlier weight at +4.0, scaling the whole row to fit +4.0 would crush all 1,000 normal weights into a tiny handful of integer buckets! Group-wise quantization prevents that neatly: chop each row into bite-sized groups of 32 weights, save just one shared 32-bit float scale = max(|group|) / maxInt for each group, and snap the 32 weights in that group to small signed integers:

  • INT8 (8-bit, 255 levels, −127..+127): Pack four 8-bit weights inside a single 32-bit unsigned integer (u32). Including one float scale per 32 weights, memory drops by 3.56× with virtually zero accuracy loss! This exact Q8 format is what powers the .smw ("Small Minds Weights") checkpoints in this project — shrinking each 780K-parameter companion brain in Part 8 from 3.12 MB down to just 857 KB!
  • INT4 (4-bit, 16 levels, −8..+7): Pack eight 4-bit "nibbles" inside a single u32 word! Inside quant.wgsl, one memory load hauls 8 weights onto the chip at once, unpacks each nibble in registers with a fast bit shift and mask, multiplies by groupScale, and accumulates — cutting VRAM bandwidth by nearly 6.4×! 
// Unpack 8 signed 4-bit weights from ONE 32-bit GPU register word (quant.wgsl)
let nib = (packedWord >> (lane * 4u)) & 0xFu;
let w   = f32(i32(nib) - 8) * groupScale;
acc = fma(w, x[col + lane], acc);

Benchmark all three real WGSL decode kernels (gemv_fp32, gemv_int8, and gemv_int4) live on your GPU in Figure 1 below. Adjust weight dim up to 2048×2048 and inspect both the memory-footprint bars and the live numerical error (RMSE = root-mean-square error compared against exact FP32).

Look closely at the RMSE numbers on the left: INT8 reproduces the exact 2048-element dot products to the third decimal place while slashing memory by nearly 4×, and even ultra-compact INT4 (just 16 possible steps per group!) tracks the true floating-point dot product closely enough that billion-parameter LLMs run comfortably on an ordinary laptop!

Step 3 · An honest compassValidation bits-per-byte (val_bpb)

Now turn to the second half of this essay: how do ML researchers systematically discover better model architectures and training recipes?

Before letting an automated loop touch a single knob, you need one incorruptible scoreboard. Remember Part 3's warning about overfitting: raw training loss is dangerously easy to game. Make a model 3× wider and its training loss plummets simply by memorizing training lines verbatim — even while it gets worse on new text! Worse still, if you change a model's tokenizer (Part 4), raw loss-per-token is no longer even comparable between two models because longer tokens carry more letters per guess.

The gold-standard compass solves both problems at once:

  1. Hold out the final 15% of the corpus as an unseen validation exam that backpropagation never trains on.
  2. Convert validation cross-entropy nats into validation bits per byte (val_bpb): val_bpb = val_loss / ln(2) (for these 1-byte ASCII tokens, dividing by ln(2) ≈ 0.6931 converts directly from nats into Shannon bits per character!).
  3. Enforce an identical fixed compute budget (the exact same number of training steps or wall-clock seconds) for every candidate!

Under a fixed budget, every architectural idea has to earn its keep honestly: a wider layer that runs slower or overfits loses; a cleaner activation or better learning-rate schedule that generalizes faster wins.

Step 4 · The research loopAutoresearch inside the browser

Inspired by Andrej Karpathy's autoresearch — where an AI agent edits a training script, runs a timed GPU experiment, checks val_bpb, and commits or reverts like a tireless scientist — BrowserAutoResearcher brings that exact closed scientific loop straight into your browser tab:

  1. Establish baseline: Start from a deliberately vanilla 2-layer Transformer genome (dModel = 48, GeGLU, AdamW, constant lr = 0.005, RoPE θ = 10000) and measure its held-out val_bpb.
  2. Propose one concrete mutation: Pick a single scientific hypothesis along one of 10 axes — switching GeGLU → SwiGLU, testing Muon vs. AdamW, adding cosine learning-rate warmdown over the final 35% of steps, nudging lr up or down, tuning weightDecay, shifting RoPE θ to 1000, widening dModel, expanding the FFN ratio from 2× → 3×, or adding a 3rd Transformer layer.
  3. Allocate, train, and grade: Spin up a fresh WebGPU model, train it on the exact same deterministic mini-batch order for the exact same step budget, and grade it on the held-out validation split.
  4. Commit or revert: If val_bpb drops by more than 0.002, KEEP the new genome as the frontier; otherwise DROP it, revert to the parent, and record the lesson.

Notice the deeper algorithmic question lurking inside Step 2: which mutation axis should the researcher try next? If changing the learning rate paid off twice, should it keep exploiting learning rate forever, or explore untouched axes like SwiGLU and cosine warmdown? Computer science calls this the exploration vs. exploitation dilemma, and Figure 2 lets you test three distinct search strategies live on your GPU:

  • Hill Climb: Always mutates the single best model so far using a random axis. Simple and greedy, though it wastes trials re-testing unproductive axes.
  • UCB Bandit (Upper Confidence Bound): Treats each mutation axis like a slot machine in a row of 10 machines, scoring each axis by: 
    UCB(axis) = average_improvement(axis)  +  √( 1.4 · ln(total_trials) / (tries_of_axis + 0.5) )
            └─── exploitation (what worked) ───┘   └──── exploration (curiosity bonus!) ────┘
    Axes that delivered big val_bpb drops get a high first term; axes that have barely been tried get a big square-root curiosity bonus so nothing important gets overlooked!
  • Evolutionary Pool: Keeps the top-3 models alive simultaneously and branches off any of them — avoiding getting trapped in one local peak when two different architectural recipes are competing.

Without any prior knowledge hard-coded into the search, your browser repeatedly rediscovers the exact empirical principles frontier labs spent years publishing: hotter early learning rates win under tight step budgets, SwiGLU edges out GeGLU at narrow widths, and cosine warmdown shaves off late-step noise so weights settle cleanly into the valley floor.

Step 5 · Scaling upWhat the same loop discovered on a cloud GPU

Because this project shares its exact model math and binary .smw checkpoint format between WebGPU and PyTorch (via the companion notebooks in colab/), the exact same fixed-budget AutoResearch loop was also run on an NVIDIA L4 GPU over a much larger 780K-parameter Transformer (6 layers, dModel = 128, 4 heads, ctx = 128) training on real English stories (TinyStories). Look at the subtle real-world engineering lesson hiding in that table — one you could never guess from theory alone! Per individual step, Muon actually learned remarkably fast (reaching 1.444 bpb in just 304 steps!), yet because Muon's Newton-Schulz matrix orthogonalization added overhead at small 128 × 128 widths, AdamW squeezed in twice as many steps (630 vs. 304) within the exact same wall-clock budget and won the fixed-time race at 1.410 bpb! Fixed-budget validation caught that hardware trade-off instantly — and that winning SwiGLU + AdamW + θ = 10000 architecture is the exact blueprint powering the six living companion brains in Part 8.
