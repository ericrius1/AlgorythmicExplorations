Teaching the Weights

Sending gradients backward through every WGSL kernel — and watching coherent words rise out of Gaussian noise on your GPU in real time.

Scroll down to read ↓

The curve dropping overhead right now is not a pre-recorded animation. Every animation frame, a real two-layer Transformer living inside WebGPU storage buffers reads a batch of haiku lines, predicts the next character at every position, computes its cross-entropy error, propagates exact gradients backward through SwiGLU, RMSNorm, FlashAttention, and RoPE, and updates roughly 70,000 weights — all inside a single command encoder submission.

Step 1 · One clean gradientFused softmax and cross-entropy

At the top of the network, the LM head outputs one raw score (logit) per token in the vocabulary. Turning those scores into probabilities via softmax and grading the true next token with negative log-likelihood sounds complicated, yet their combined derivative collapses into the single cleanest formula in deep learning: 

// fused softmax + cross-entropy backward in ops.wgsl
let prob = exp(logits[v] - maxVal) / sumExp;
let y    = select(0.0, 1.0, v == target);
dLogits[v] = (prob - y) / f32(N);

That's it: predicted probability minus truth. If the model gave the right letter 95% probability, the push is tiny (−0.05). If it gave a wrong letter 80%, the gradient pushes that logit straight down by +0.80. Fusing the forward loss and backward gradient into one WGSL pass means a separate probability buffer never even gets allocated.

Step 2 · The residual highwayWhy deep gradients don't die

From dLogits, backpropagation walks backward layer by layer. Notice something crucial about this architecture: every sub-block adds its output back into the main stream via x_next = x + f(RMSNorm(x)). Because the derivative of addition is 1, gradients flow untouched straight down the residual stream from the top layer all the way to the token embeddings, picking up gentle corrections at each layer rather than multiplying together and blowing up.

Try turning Residual: OFF and nudging weight gain just slightly above or below 1.0. Over 12 layers, even a tiny 1.2× factor explodes gradients by an order of magnitude, while 0.8× starves the earliest layers to zero. Flip Residual: ON back on and the highway stabilizes immediately.

How FlashAttention backward avoids atomic contention

In WebGPU, floating-point storage buffers do not support native atomicAdd(f32). Rather than fighting software CAS loops, the attention.wgsl splits attention backward into two clean, race-free compute passes: flash_attn_bwd_dq assigns one thread per query position qt (looping over keys 0..qt), and flash_attn_bwd_dkv assigns one thread per key position kt (looping over future queries kt..T-1). Both recompute exact attention probabilities from the saved scalar log-sum-exp LSE, writing directly to their own rows with zero atomics and zero T × T matrices.

Step 3 · Live trainingTrain your own micro-LLM in WebGPU

Once every parameter buffer has its gradient, the optimizer pass takes a step. You can switch between classic decoupled AdamW (tracking per-weight first and second moments) and a Muon-inspired RMS-steered Nesterov update that equalizes step norms across 2D weight projections.

Pick a corpus below, press train, and watch the live loss curve plunge from random guessing (ln(98) ≈ 4.58 nats) down past 1.5 in just a few seconds. Every 25 steps — or whenever you hit sample now — the model generates fresh text straight from your GPU and renders its live causal attention matrix on the right.

Watch the progression carefully: around step 15 it discovers spaces and line breaks; by step 60 it captures character pairs, speaker colons, or chord arrows; past step 150 real words, rhymes, and jazz progressions emerge from weights that started as pure Gaussian static.
