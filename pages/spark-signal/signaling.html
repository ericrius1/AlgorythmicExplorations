Spark & Signal · Part 1

How Neurons Signal

From a membrane holding a voltage to a spike, a synapse and a small circuit, with each step simulated live. The cells above are firing at each other right now.

Scroll down to read ↓

The cells at the top of the page are a small live network. Each one adds up the input reaching its cell body, and when the total crosses a threshold it fires. A bright pulse runs down its axon, splits where the axon branches, and at each synapse starts a dim signal that spreads along the next cell's dendrite toward its cell body. It's all slowed down a lot. In real tissue the whole exchange takes milliseconds.

This article builds up what's happening in that picture, starting from a patch of membrane. Each step has a figure running a real model: Hodgkin and Huxley's equations for the membrane and the axon, a probabilistic model of transmitter release for the synapse, and integrate-and-fire cells for the circuit. The numbers in the text come out of those models, so you can check them against the figures.

Step 1 · The partsAn input end and an output end

A typical neuron has four parts, and each does one job in the chain:

dendrites   → collect input from other cells   (small, graded voltage changes)
cell body   → adds the inputs up and decides whether to fire
axon        → carries an all-or-none spike to other cells
terminals   → turn the spike into a chemical message

The dendrites branch into a tree that can hold thousands of synapses. What arrives there are small voltage changes, a millivolt or so each, and they add up at the cell body. If the total crosses a threshold, a spike starts at the base of the axon and travels down it. Axons can be long: the ones that run from your spinal cord to the muscles of your foot are about a metre. At the terminals the signal becomes chemical. The terminal releases a neurotransmitter, the next cell's receptors pick it up, and the cycle starts again in that cell.

Aside · Counting neurons

The usual figure for the human brain, 86 billion neurons, comes from a 2009 study by Suzana Herculano-Houzel's group. They turned brain tissue into a suspension of cell nuclei and counted samples of it, replacing the round 100 billion that textbooks had been repeating. For signalling, the wiring matters more than the count: a single cortical neuron receives several thousand synapses.

Step 2 · Resting potentialA battery made of salt water

A neuron is a bag of salt water sitting in slightly different salt water. The bag is a lipid membrane about 5 nanometres thick that ions can't cross on their own, studded with proteins called ion channels that let particular ions through. The inside is rich in potassium (K⁺) and poor in sodium (Na⁺), and the outside is the reverse. A pump in the membrane keeps it that way, spending ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ it brings in.

Now open some K⁺ channels. Potassium leaks out, down its concentration gradient, and each ion that leaves takes a positive charge with it, so the inside goes negative. The more negative it gets, the harder the voltage pulls K⁺ back in. At some voltage the two pulls balance and the net flow stops. That's the potassium equilibrium potential, given by the Nernst equation, and in the model on this page it's −77 mV. Sodium has its own balance point, +50 mV, on the other side of zero.

The membrane has channels for both, so it settles in between, pulled toward each ion's equilibrium in proportion to how open that ion's channels are: 

// each ion pulls the voltage toward its own equilibrium, weighted by conductance
V_rest = (g_K·E_K + g_Na·E_Na) / (g_K + g_Na)

At rest in this model the K⁺ conductance is about ten times the Na⁺ conductance (0.61 against 0.064 mS/cm²), so the cell rests at −65 mV, much closer to −77 than to +50. The model is Hodgkin and Huxley's squid axon, so these are a squid's numbers. A mammalian neuron works the same way with shifted values: its K⁺ equilibrium is nearer −90 mV, and it rests somewhere around −60 to −70.

In the figure, the left side is a voltage trace that scrolls like an oscilloscope. The right side is a patch of membrane, with K⁺ leaving through its channels and Na⁺ coming in through its own. The sliders set the leak channels, the ones that are always open. Drag the Na⁺ leak to zero and the membrane settles at −76.9 mV, a hair above E_K. Drag the K⁺ leak up and the resting voltage also sinks toward E_K, reaching −71 mV at the top of the slider.

Keep an eye on the numbers under the patch while you drag. Whatever the settings, once the trace is flat the outward K⁺ current and the inward Na⁺ current are equal and opposite, 7.36 µA/cm² each way at the defaults. A resting membrane has a steady trickle of both ions crossing it. In a real cell the pump's job is to carry them back so the concentrations don't run down. The model leaves the pump out and holds the concentrations fixed, which is a fair shortcut over seconds but not over hours.

Now push the Na⁺ leak past about 0.165 mS/cm². A balance point still exists, near −59 mV, but it's no longer stable, and the cell starts firing on its own at about 70 spikes a second. It doesn't start slowly and speed up; it jumps straight to around 70. Bring the leak back down a little and it keeps firing anyway. It only stops once the leak is down to about 0.12, so in between the cell can either rest or fire, depending on what it was doing a moment ago. Both quirks belong to the squid axon and its equations. Hodgkin sorted crab nerve fibres by this kind of behaviour in 1948: some can fire at any rate down to almost zero, and others, like the axon in this model, only start at a fixed minimum.

How much charge a resting potential takes

Very few ions have to move to set up −65 mV. The membrane is a thin insulator between two conducting solutions, which makes it a capacitor, about 1 µF per square centimetre (the value in the model). Charging that to 65 mV takes 65 nC/cm², about 4 × 10¹¹ ions per square centimetre. For a cell body 20 µm across that's around 5 million ions, roughly one in 70,000 of the K⁺ inside it. The concentrations barely change. The voltage lives in a thin layer of charge on each face of the membrane.

The membrane's resistance times its capacitance is its time constant: how long an input takes to leak away. It's about 1.5 ms for the squid model at rest, and more like 10 to 30 ms for the cell body of a cortical neuron. That number comes back in Step 5, where it decides how well inputs add up.

Step 3 · The spikeTwo gates and a delay

Some channels open and close with voltage, and those turn the resting membrane into something that can fire. Hodgkin and Huxley worked out how in 1952, measuring currents in the giant axon of the squid, which is about half a millimetre thick, wide enough to thread a wire down its middle. They found two voltage-gated currents and described each kind of channel with gates that swing open and shut at voltage-dependent rates: 

// Hodgkin–Huxley, per cm² of membrane
C·dV/dt = −g_Na·m³h·(V − E_Na) − g_K·n⁴·(V − E_K) − I_leak + I_stim
dm/dt   = α_m(V)·(1 − m) − β_m(V)·m       // the same form for h and n

m is the sodium channel's fast activation gate, h is its slower inactivation gate, and n is the potassium channel's slow activation gate. With g_Na = 120 and g_K = 36 mS/cm², those lines are the whole model behind the next figure, stepped forward 0.01 ms at a time. Nothing in it says "spike". The spike comes out of the timing.

A small push depolarizes the membrane a little. That opens a few Na⁺ channels (m is fast), Na⁺ rushes in, the membrane depolarizes further, and more Na⁺ channels open. Past a certain point the loop runs away and the voltage shoots up toward E_Na. Then the slow gates catch up: h shuts the Na⁺ channels, n opens the K⁺ channels, and K⁺ flowing out drags the voltage back down, past rest, toward E_K. The K⁺ channels close slowly, so the voltage stays below rest for a while before it recovers.

The slider below sets the size of a 1 ms current pulse (the amber step under the voltage), and the button delivers it. The lower panel shows how many Na⁺ and K⁺ channels are open, as conductance. Starting from rest, the default 10 µA/cm² gives a spike that peaks at +39 mV, 2.5 ms after the pulse starts, and then dips to −76 mV. Work your way down, waiting a few seconds between presses so the membrane is back at rest. At 7 µA/cm² you still get a spike, nearly as tall but later, 5.3 ms after the pulse. At 6.5 the voltage creeps up to −59 mV and sinks back. Nothing in between shows up at the slider's resolution. That's what all-or-none means.

Until you touch it, the figure alternates a 4 µA/cm² pulse, which only lifts the voltage to about −61 mV, with a 10 µA/cm² pulse, which fires. In the lower panel you can see the order of events. The Na⁺ conductance peaks at about 31 mS/cm² and is already falling when the K⁺ conductance peaks, at about 12.5, roughly 1.4 ms later. These numbers are for 6.3 °C, the temperature of Hodgkin and Huxley's experiments (squid live in cold water). A mammalian spike at body temperature is narrower.

Now press stimulate soon after a spike. The line under the figure says how long it's been since the last one, and the threshold depends on it. In this model, for about 9 ms after a spike nothing on the slider will fire the cell (at 5 ms it would take 77 µA/cm²). At 10 ms it takes about 16, and at 12 ms about 10. Around 17 ms the threshold dips a little below its resting value, to 5.8, and by 30 ms it's back to 6.9. That stretch of reduced excitability is the refractory period. The Na⁺ channels' h gates are still shut and the K⁺ channels are still open, and both have to recover before the next spike.

The threshold isn't quite sharp

Strictly, the Hodgkin–Huxley equations don't have a threshold. In this model a 1 ms pulse of 6.92124 µA/cm² fires and 6.92123 doesn't, and if you search the gap between them finely enough you find responses of every height: a pulse 10⁻⁹ µA/cm² above the boundary peaks at +5 mV, about 13 ms late. Richard FitzHugh pointed this out in 1955. The window is far narrower than anything an experiment could control, so in practice the spike is all-or-none.

Aside · A model computed by hand

Hodgkin and Huxley fitted their equations to voltage-clamp recordings, where a feedback circuit holds the membrane at a set voltage and measures the current it takes to keep it there. Then they had to check that the equations would produce a travelling action potential. Cambridge's computer, EDSAC, was out of action for modifications at the time, so Huxley integrated the equations step by step on a hand-cranked mechanical calculator. One propagating action potential took him about three weeks. It came out at 18.8 m/s against a measured 21.2, and in 1963 the two of them shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with John Eccles.

Step 4 · PropagationA fuse that relights itself

A spike at one spot on an axon doesn't stay there. Current from the depolarized patch flows along the inside of the axon into its neighbours and charges their membrane past the point where their own Na⁺ channels run away, so they fire too. The pulse is rebuilt at every point it passes, which is why it arrives at the far end the same size it started.

The next figure is a 10 cm length of squid axon, 476 µm thick (the diameter Hodgkin and Huxley used), split into 200 compartments half a millimetre long. Each compartment runs the same membrane equations as before, and each is joined to its neighbours through the resistance of the axon's interior, 35.4 Ω·cm. There's no rule about pulses or directions anywhere in it: 

// compartment i: its own membrane currents, plus current from its two neighbours
C·dV[i]/dt = −I_membrane(V[i]) + (d / 4R_i)·(V[i−1] − 2·V[i] + V[i+1]) / Δx²

The plot on top is the voltage along the axon right now, and the tube under it is the axon itself, coloured by voltage. Two probes, A and B, 4 cm apart, time each pulse as it passes and report its speed. At the default diameter it's 12.3 m/s. The diameter slider shows why squid grew such a thick axon (it drives their escape jet): the speed goes up with the square root of the diameter, from 5.6 m/s at 100 µm to 17.8 m/s at 1,000 µm. A wider axon has less internal resistance, so the current reaches further ahead of the pulse.

Click the middle of the axon while it's quiet and you start two pulses, one running each way. An axon conducts in both directions. In a living cell the spike starts at the cell body, so it only goes one way. Then press "fire both ends", which waits for the axon to recover from the last pulse before it fires. The two pulses meet in the middle and both vanish, because each runs into membrane the other has just used.

The pink shading behind a pulse marks membrane that hasn't recovered yet: its Na⁺ channels are still inactivated and its K⁺ channels are still open. Click inside it and nothing happens. In this model the same jolt at the same spot fails for about 10.6 ms after a pulse has gone by, which at 12.3 m/s is a wake about 13 cm long, longer than the axon on screen. That refractory wake is what stops a pulse from turning around, and it caps how often a neuron can fire. Most neurons top out at a few hundred spikes a second.

Aside · Myelin

Mammals don't rely on giant axons. Most of their fast axons are wrapped in myelin, layers of insulating membrane laid down by glial cells, with short gaps called nodes of Ranvier every millimetre or so. The insulation cuts the membrane's capacitance and leak, so current from one node charges the next quickly and the spike skips from node to node. A myelinated axon 10 to 20 µm thick can conduct at up to about 120 m/s, much faster than the squid's half-millimetre axon. Unmyelinated mammalian fibres, such as many that carry pain and temperature, manage around 1 m/s. In multiple sclerosis the immune system strips myelin away, and conduction slows or fails.

Step 5 · The synapsePackets of transmitter

At the end of the axon the signal has to cross to the next cell. At most synapses the two cells don't touch; there's a cleft about 20 nm wide between them. When a spike reaches the terminal it opens voltage-gated calcium channels, Ca²⁺ flows in, and the calcium makes small vesicles of neurotransmitter fuse with the membrane and empty into the cleft. The transmitter diffuses across in microseconds and opens receptor channels on the other side. At an excitatory synapse those channels let sodium in (and a little potassium out), so the receiving cell depolarizes slightly.

In the early 1950s Bernard Katz, working with Paul Fatt and then José del Castillo, recorded from the junction between nerve and muscle in frogs and found that this response comes in steps. Even with no nerve impulse there were tiny spontaneous blips, all about the same size, and the response to an impulse was a whole number of blips that changed from trial to trial. Each blip was one vesicle's worth of transmitter, a quantum. Release is a game of chance: a spike gives each docked vesicle some probability of fusing.

The figure is built on that picture. Each input axon reaches the receiving cell's dendrite through 10 release sites, each holding one docked vesicle. A spike makes each docked vesicle fuse with the probability on the slider, after 0.5 ms for the calcium to act. An empty site refills after 100 ms on average, which is quicker than most real synapses (they take from a few hundred milliseconds to seconds); I sped it up so the figure recovers in a few seconds of screen time. Each vesicle opens an AMPA-type receptor conductance that peaks at 0.55 nS, rising in 0.2 ms and decaying over 2 ms, on a membrane that rests at −70 mV with a 20 ms time constant: 

// a spike arrives: every docked vesicle rolls the dice
for (const site of sites) if (site.docked && random() < p) fuse(site);
g_syn += released × 0.55 nS                 // one quantum per vesicle

// the receiving cell (200 pF, 10 nS leak, so τ = 20 ms)
C·dV/dt = −g_leak·(V + 70 mV) − g_syn·(V − 0 mV)

One vesicle lifts the cell by 0.38 mV. One spike in one input releases 3 vesicles on average, about 1.1 mV, which is in the usual range for a single connection between two cortical neurons. The cell fires at −55 mV, 15 mV above rest, so one input can't do it alone.

The figure starts with 12 inputs firing together. One spike from all twelve releases about 36 vesicles and lifts the cell by around 12 mV, which usually falls short; over many trials in the model it fires about one time in twenty. Press "burst of 5" to send five spikes 10 ms apart. Each bump arrives before the last has leaked away, so they pile up, and from a fully stocked terminal the cell fires on 99% of trials. Adding up inputs that arrive close together in time is temporal summation. Adding up inputs from many synapses at once, as the twelve do, is spatial summation.

Watch the vesicles in the boutons during a burst. Each spike uses some up and they refill slowly, so later spikes release fewer: on average 36, 26, 20, 16 and 14 vesicles. The line under the figure lists the counts for the burst you just fired. That's synaptic depression, and it's why a burst adds up to less than five single spikes would. Then try the inputs slider. With 16 inputs a single spike fires the cell about 70% of the time. With one input you can see a single connection's millivolt, and how much it varies from one spike to the next.

Aside · Glutamate and GABA

Most fast excitatory synapses in the brain use glutamate, the transmitter modelled here. Most fast inhibitory ones use GABA, whose receptors let chloride in and hold the cell near −70 mV or below, away from threshold. Dozens of other transmitters, such as dopamine and serotonin, mostly act more slowly, changing how excitable whole circuits are rather than carrying a point-to-point signal. Acetylcholine is the fast transmitter at the junction between nerve and muscle, where Katz did his experiments.

Electrical synapses

Some cells are joined directly. At a gap junction, channels made of connexin proteins connect the insides of two cells, so current flows straight across with no wait for release. Heart muscle cells are coupled this way, which lets a wave of contraction sweep across the heart, and some groups of neurons use gap junctions to fire in tight synchrony. Chemical synapses cost about half a millisecond each, but they can amplify, they can excite or inhibit, and their strength can change with use, which is where most learning is thought to be stored.

Step 6 · The circuitSeven cells and a loop

One neuron adds up its inputs and fires or doesn't. What a group of them does depends on how they're wired. For the last figure I switched to the simplest model that keeps that logic, the leaky integrate-and-fire cell. It's a capacitor with a leak (rest −70 mV, τ = 20 ms) plus a rule: when the voltage reaches −55 mV, record a spike, reset to −70 and hold there for 2 ms. It has no Na⁺ or K⁺ channels, and the spike from Step 3 becomes a single event, which makes networks cheap to simulate. 

// each cell, each step
v += (−70 − v) / 20 · dt + noise
if (v ≥ −55) { spike(); v = −70; refractory = 2 ms; }
// each spike reaches its targets 6 ms later and kicks their voltage by w mV

The wiring is a short chain with a loop in it. A drives B and C hard enough to fire each of them alone (+18 mV). D gets +9 mV from each of B and C, so it fires only when both arrive together; one alone leaves it 6 mV short. D and E excite each other. F is inhibitory: D drives it, and it kicks D back down. G is the output, driven by E. Every connection takes 6 ms.

The figure stimulates A every 60 ms (the auto input button turns that off), you can click any cell to make it fire, and the line under the figure says what D did. Spikes travel the connections as dots, and the raster at the bottom marks every spike in the last 150 ms. With the inhibition at its default of 20 mV, each stimulus makes one wave. D fires once, E and F fire, and 6 ms later E's +18 mV and F's −20 mV reach D at the same moment and cancel to −2. D stays silent. Now drag the inhibition down. At 3 mV or less, E's kick wins, D fires again, and the D–E loop keeps relighting itself every 12 ms for as long as you let it. One input has turned into activity that doesn't stop.

That loop is a crude version of two real things. Recurrent excitation is one proposed way for circuits to hold on to information after the input has gone, as in working memory. Left unchecked, it's also what a seizure looks like. Around a fifth of the neurons in the cortex are inhibitory interneurons, and keeping excitation in check is a large part of their job.

The noise slider adds random jitter to every cell's voltage, standing in for the thousands of unrelated inputs a real neuron gets. Up to about 3 mV of jitter nothing fires by itself. At 4 mV cells fire now and then with no input, and with the inhibition turned down a stray spike from D or E can start the loop. At 6 mV the whole circuit chatters, and the clean wave from A is hard to pick out of the raster.

Why bother with spikes

A voltage spreading passively along an axon fades with distance. In the squid axon of Step 4 the length constant is about 7 mm, so a steady voltage falls to about a third every 7 mm, and over a metre there'd be nothing left. A spike pays to rebuild the signal at every point. Attwell and Laughlin estimated in 2001 that one action potential in a rodent neuron costs on the order of 10⁸ ATP molecules, mostly to pump back the Na⁺ that came in, and that signalling uses most of the energy grey matter consumes.

EpilogueWhat carries over

The same few ideas came up at every step:

  • A voltage is a balance of currents. The resting potential is where K⁺ leaking out cancels Na⁺ leaking in, and moving either leak moves it.
  • Timing makes the spike. Fast Na⁺ activation racing slower inactivation and K⁺ activation is enough to turn two equations into an all-or-none event.
  • Propagation is local. Each patch of axon only talks to its neighbours. Direction, speed and the refractory wake all follow from that.
  • Synapses are noisy and they add up. One input gives about a millivolt that varies from spike to spike. Firing a cell takes many inputs together or a quick run of them.
  • Wiring decides behaviour. The same seven cells either answer each input once or ring forever, depending on one inhibitory weight.

Things I'd try next: wrap the axon in myelin, with nodes every millimetre, and watch the speed jump; give the synapse facilitation as well as depression, since many low-probability synapses get stronger during a burst; and swap the integrate-and-fire cells in the circuit for Hodgkin–Huxley ones to see which of its behaviours survive.

That's the whole chain, from a membrane holding a voltage to a few cells passing a signal around. The network at the top of the page runs on the same logic: dim graded potentials creeping along dendrites, cell bodies adding them up, and bright pulses that keep their size all the way to the next cell.
