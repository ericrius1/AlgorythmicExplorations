Spark & Signal · Part 1

How Neurons Signal

From salty water on both sides of a thin membrane up to twenty thousand cells firing together, all running live on your GPU.

Series overview · Spark & Signal Spark & Signal reconstructs how biological neurons compute, scaling from individual salt ions drifting across a thin membrane up to a firing patch of cortex on the GPU. Across a sequence of live WebGPU simulations, this essay builds a cell's resting voltage from random-walking ions, assembles stochastic Hodgkin–Huxley action potentials, races myelinated axons against bare wires, releases neurotransmitters across a chemical synapse, and wires twenty thousand cells together into a miniature slab of cortex.

Watch the neuron overhead for a few seconds and you'll catch it catching fire. Most of the time, tiny synaptic inputs patter onto the spines along its branches and die out as faint purple ripples. Then, every few seconds, enough inputs land close enough in time to push the cell over the edge. The simulation automatically drops into bullet time (about 1/2,500 of real speed) so you can actually watch the spike ignite at the base of the axon and tear down its length.

Nothing up there is keyframed. The cell is divided into roughly six hundred short cylindrical compartments, each running the Hodgkin–Huxley equations, trading electrical current with its neighbors on every tick. Everything visible is drawn out of particles (up to about 900,000 depending on your display). The membrane itself shifts color with local voltage, bright streaks inside the branches trace axial current, amber sparks mark sodium rushing inward, and cyan sparks mark potassium pouring back out wherever ion channels open. Below, the essay pulls that whole cell apart into its working pieces and puts it back together from the bottom up.

Color key · shared across every figure

Indigo marks membrane sitting at rest, shifting through violet as it depolarizes and flaring orange to white during a spike. Amber is Na⁺, cyan is K⁺, green is glutamate (the main excitatory transmitter), and magenta is GABA (the main inhibitory one).

Prologue · AnatomyFour jobs along one cell

Before zooming down to individual ions, it helps to see the whole layout at once, because a neuron's geometry maps almost directly onto how a signal moves through it. The dendrites are the listening antenna: thousands of synapses sit along their branches (mostly on tiny mushroom-shaped spines), each nudging the local voltage up or down by a fraction of a millivolt. Those small bumps spread passively toward the soma (the cell body) and weaken as they go. Right where the axon leaves the soma sits a short, densely packed strip of sodium channels called the initial segment. If the combined signals arriving there cross threshold, the cell fires a full-sized spike, and the axon carries it all the way to the axon terminals without losing strength, where it gets handed off chemically to the next cell.

Try poking different parts of the tree while keeping an eye on the soma voltage trace in the bottom right. Clicking out near the tip of the apical tuft sends a whisper down the long trunk that arrives at the soma smaller and later than a click right next to the cell body, because current leaks out across the membrane along the way. Clicking straight on the axon triggers a spike on the spot, and because axons conduct equally well in either direction when freshly rested, you'll see it race both forward toward the terminals and backward into the soma. You can also pull the synaptic input slider down near 2 to quiet the background chatter, or push it up to 10 and watch the cell fire steadily on its own.

If you spot occasional magenta flashes right on the soma, those are inhibitory synapses. Real neurons tend to pack inhibitory inputs close to the cell body and initial segment for a very practical reason: sitting right next to the trigger gives them veto power over excitation coming in from anywhere out in the dendritic tree.

How the branching model is built and solved

The tree is generated procedurally from persistent random walks: a tapering apical trunk with oblique side branches, a top tuft that forks three times, a skirt of basal dendrites around the soma, and a myelinated axon alternating wrapped internodes with bare nodes of Ranvier before branching into terminals. Different regions get different channel densities: dendrites are mostly passive cable, while the initial segment and nodes of Ranvier are crammed with many times the sodium channel density of the soma.

On every time step, the whole tree is solved simultaneously with an implicit backward Euler step so tight electrical coupling between tiny neighboring compartments never blows up. Michael Hines worked out in 1984 that if you number every parent compartment ahead of its children, solving the resulting branched linear system takes just two linear passes: one sweep inward from the branch tips to the root, and one sweep back out. It scales linearly with the number of compartments and is still the backbone of the NEURON simulator today.

Step 1 · Resting potentialWhere −70 mV comes from

Even when a neuron is doing absolutely nothing, its interior sits around 70 millivolts negative compared to the fluid outside. That voltage is the battery that powers everything else in the nervous system, and you don't need any exotic biology to explain where it comes from: just two facts about the salt water on either side of the cell membrane. 

// typical mammalian neuron concentrations, mM
           inside   outside
K⁺          140        5
Na⁺          12      145
Cl⁻          10      110
A⁻ (proteins, can't cross)  ~140 inside

First, the salt concentrations are deliberately skewed: potassium (K⁺) is crowded inside the cell, while sodium (Na⁺) is crowded outside. Second, at rest the membrane is fairly leaky to potassium and nearly watertight to everything else. Potassium leak channels let K⁺ slip across roughly a thousand times more readily than Na⁺.

Left alone, potassium ions simply wander down their concentration gradient and out of the cell. Every K⁺ that escapes carries a positive charge out with it, leaving an unmatched negative charge (mostly large protein molecules that are far too big to slip through the membrane) trapped inside. As negative charge builds up on the inner face of the membrane, it starts pulling positively charged potassium ions back in. Eventually the inward electrical pull balances the outward crowd pressure, and net flow stops. For potassium alone, that equilibrium is given by the Nernst potential: 

E = (kT / ze) · ln(c_outside / c_inside)
E_K  = 26.7 mV · ln(5 / 140)   ≈ −89 mV     // at 37 °C
E_Na = 26.7 mV · ln(145 / 12)  ≈ +66 mV

Below, 240,000 simulated ions run that experiment from scratch on the GPU. Each particle does an independent random walk on its side of the membrane and can only cross when it bumps into a channel of its own kind. Whenever an ion attempts to pass through, the shader flips a weighted coin using Boltzmann's rule: moving downhill with the electric field always succeeds, while climbing uphill succeeds with probability e−zeV/kT. Nothing in the code hardcodes −70 mV. The voltage is literally recounted every frame by tallying the net charge that has crossed the membrane and dividing by its capacitance.

Hit reset to zero the voltage and watch the battery charge itself from scratch. A burst of cyan potassium ions streams outward through the open cyan pores, the voltage plunges, and within a second or two it settles around −75 mV (bouncing slightly with random crossings). Notice it stops shy of EK (−89 mV) because a handful of amber sodium leak channels let a trickle of Na⁺ sneak inward, tugging the voltage back upward. Where the cell comes to rest is simply a tug-of-war between those two gradients, weighted by how many pores each ion gets.

Keep an eye on the concentration numbers at top left while you reset. Even as the voltage swings by more than 70 mV, the internal potassium concentration barely twitches. Charging this whole patch takes only about 150 ions out of more than fifty thousand; in a real cell the proportion is tinier still, on the order of a few ions per hundred thousand. Because a lipid membrane is so thin, it takes almost no ions at all to build a hefty voltage across it, which means charging the membrane barely dents the salt reserves.

You can bend the tug-of-war in either direction with the sliders. Closing all the K⁺ leak channels lets sodium win and pulls the voltage upward toward ENa; cranking Na⁺ leak channels up drags rest toward zero. If you switch the pump off, nothing dramatic happens right away, yet over time the concentrations start to bleed together: sodium creeps up inside, potassium drains away, and the resting voltage slowly collapses. And if you turn the ion count up toward a million, the jittery trace smooths right out because any single ion crossing counts for a smaller slice of the voltage.

Aside · Why your brain burns so many calories

Those violet rings embedded in the membrane are Na⁺/K⁺ pumps. Every cycle burns one molecule of ATP to haul three Na⁺ ions back out and two K⁺ ions back in, pushing both uphill against their gradients. Because the leak channels never fully close, the pumps run nonstop for your entire life, by some estimates accounting for roughly half of the brain's total energy budget. And because each cycle ships one net positive charge outward, the pump directly contributes a few extra negative millivolts to the resting potential on its own.

Deep dive: how few ions does it take to make −70 mV?

A cell membrane is a double layer of lipid molecules roughly 5 nanometers thick, an insulator sandwiched between two salty conductors, giving it a capacitance of about 1 µF/cm². Charging that capacitor to 70 mV requires Q = CV ≈ 7 × 10⁻⁸ coulombs per square centimeter, which works out to around 4,400 excess ions per square micrometer. Meanwhile, a cubic micrometer of cytoplasm just underneath holds roughly 80 million potassium ions.

In the simulation above, capacitance is the single scaling constant converting "net ions crossed" into millivolts. Since a GPU buffer can hold hundreds of thousands of particles rather than trillions, each crossing here nudges the voltage by roughly half a millivolt, big enough to watch single-ion noise in action, whereas a real membrane averages over billions of ions and looks glass-smooth.

Step 2 · Action potentialOpening the floodgates

Once you have a charged membrane sitting at −70 mV, firing an action potential means pulling off a controlled one-millisecond short circuit: briefly throwing open the doors to sodium so the voltage rockets positive, then slamming them shut and opening potassium to yank it right back down. Doing that automatically requires two kinds of voltage-gated ion channels whose probability of opening depends on the voltage across them.

In 1952 (more than twenty years before patch-clamp recordings let anyone observe a single channel molecule), Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley deduced the kinetics of these pores from squid giant axons. In their picture, each channel is guarded by a handful of independent molecular gates that snap open or closed randomly at voltage-dependent rates. A sodium channel has three fast m gates that open when the cell depolarizes, plus one slower h gate that closes when it depolarizes; sodium can only pass when all four are open at once. A potassium channel is simpler, with four slow n gates that open on depolarization. That mismatch in gate speed is what creates the entire spike waveform.

This figure takes the same patch of membrane and adds 1,600 voltage-gated sodium channels and 480 potassium channels (organized in clusters of eight, which is why the sliders show 200 and 60 clusters), each flipping its own stochastic gates every step. Press stimulate to inject a quick pulse of positive charge through the glass electrode. Raising the voltage by about 20 mV is enough for a few sodium channels to catch all three of their fast m gates open simultaneously. Na⁺ rushes inward, driving the voltage higher still, which flips open even more m gates in an explosive positive feedback loop. The membrane shoots past 0 mV toward ENa, peaking near +50 mV.

Almost immediately, the slower gates catch up and shut the party down. The sodium h gates swing closed, blocking further Na⁺ entry even while the voltage is still high, and the potassium n gates swing open. Cyan flares of K⁺ take over from the amber sodium rush, pulling the voltage rapidly back down past rest into a brief undershoot before the gates relax. Everything plays roughly a thousand times slower than real life so you can follow the two waves swapping places. 

threshold         ≈ 15 mV above rest
peak              ≈ +40 to +50 mV       // chasing E_Na, cut short by h gates
undershoot        ≈ −90 mV              // chasing E_K
duration          ≈ 1 ms
refractory period ≈ 1–2 ms              // until h gates reset

Play with the sliders to test the edge cases. Dial the stimulus down below threshold and the small bump simply fizzles back to rest; nudge it just above threshold and you get the exact same full-height spike whether the kick was barely enough or twice as big. That all-or-none behavior is why neuroscientists can treat spikes almost like clean digital pulses. Try hitting stimulate twice quickly: while the h gates are still locked shut during the refractory period, the second kick can't trigger another spike. You can also see each channel's job directly by zeroing it out. Drop the Na⁺ channels to zero and the patch goes completely mute; drop the K⁺ channels to zero and it still spikes, then creeps painfully slowly back down through the ordinary leak channels.

What's emergent here, and where the sim takes a shortcut

In the resting-potential figure, every ion crossing comes purely from Brownian particles bumping into pores and passing a Boltzmann coin flip. Once you add thousands of voltage-gated channels, a scaling issue pops up: ions in a simulated box diffuse far more slowly relative to channel throughput than real ions in warm water, so open channels starve for particles right outside their mouths. To keep the current accurate, each open channel here issues crossing "tickets" at Hodgkin and Huxley's exact rate I = γ(V − E), and nearby particles of the right species claim them and cross. Real ions carry the current from the spot right next to each channel, and the voltage is still strictly counted charge.

The gate kinetics also run 3× faster than Hodgkin and Huxley's raw constants (which were measured in squid axons chilled to 6 °C), with their threshold curves shifted to match this patch's resting potential. Finally, because firing a full spike in a tiny simulated box would noticeably drain its salt supply, the top and bottom boundaries act like the huge reservoir of a real cell and extracellular fluid, swapping ions that hit the walls back to baseline concentrations.

Aside · Noisy pores

When only a few thousand protein channels are flipping randomly, thermal noise shows up right on the voltage trace. Real cells deal with the same physics: in very small neurons or razor-thin axons, a handful of sodium channels popping open by sheer chance can occasionally kick off an unsolicited spike. Packing in more channels averages that noise down, which is one reason biology hits a lower limit on how skinny a reliable wire can be.

Step 3 · ConductionHow a spike travels

A spike firing at one spot on an axon immediately disturbs its neighbors. All that positively charged sodium pouring into the active patch flows sideways down the salty core of the axon (the bright streaks traveling inside the tube) and charges up the adjacent stretch of membrane. As soon as that neighboring patch crosses threshold, its own sodium channels blow open and amplify the pulse back to full height. Because the spike rebuilds itself fresh at every stop, it can travel a full meter down your leg without getting any weaker.

Why doesn't the wave wash backward and bounce back and forth forever? Because the membrane it just left behind is still refractory, with its sodium h gates locked shut. Fresh, excitable membrane lies ahead while spent membrane trails behind, forcing the pulse to move strictly forward.

The race above puts two identical-diameter axons side by side and kicks them at the same instant. The top fiber is bare membrane, so the action potential has to painstakingly recharge and regenerate every tiny patch from start to finish. The bottom fiber is wrapped in myelin, tightly wound sheaths of fatty glial membrane that slash both the capacitance and the electrical leak underneath by a factor of thirty to fifty, interrupted every so often by bare gaps called nodes of Ranvier. When one node fires, current barely wastes time charging or leaking out of the insulated segment, so it zips almost instantly to the next node and triggers it instead. Physiologists call this saltatory conduction, from the Latin word for leaping.

In this simulation the myelinated axon finishes roughly five times faster. In real animals the advantage is even starker: thin unmyelinated fibers plod along near 1 m/s, while thick myelinated motor axons hit up to 120 m/s, fast enough for a large vertebrate to catch itself before hitting the ground. In multiple sclerosis, the immune system strips patches of that insulation away, leaking current out of the exposed segments so signals slow down, jitter in time, or stall out entirely.

You can click anywhere along either axon to inject a spike mid-flight. Click dead center on the bare fiber and two waves split apart in opposite directions; then quickly click near both ends and watch two oncoming spikes slam into each other and vanish completely, because each hits the dead refractory wake of the other and has nowhere left to go. Slide slow motion to the right if you want to speed the race up.

Deep dive: the cable equation

Electrically, a bare axon behaves like a leaky garden hose. Current flows down the interior against the cytoplasm's resistance and leaks outward across the membrane. Without active channels, a local voltage bump spreads out and fades exponentially over a characteristic length constant λ, which scales with the square root of the axon's radius. Meanwhile, each patch takes time to charge up, governed by the membrane time constant τ = RC.

A bare axon's conduction speed goes roughly as λ/τ, meaning it improves only with the square root of radius: to go 10× faster, a bare nerve has to be 100× thicker. Squid paid that tax with a single giant escape axon half a millimeter wide. Vertebrates found a better deal with myelin: slashing C shrinks τ across the wrapped stretches so conduction speed scales linearly with diameter, letting thousands of fast fibers fit inside a nerve no thicker than a thread.

Step 4 · Chemical handoffAcross the synapse

Most neurons never actually fuse together. At the far tip of an axon, the spike arrives at a swollen terminal called a bouton, separated from the receiving cell by a fluid-filled gap (the synaptic cleft) only about 20 nanometers wide. Electrical current can't meaningfully jump that salty gap, so the nervous system switches mediums and sends a burst of molecules instead.

When the incoming spike depolarizes the bouton, voltage-gated calcium channels snap open at the active zone and Ca²⁺ floods inside (watch for the pale yellow sparks). Calcium is the molecular green light: it triggers tiny bubbles of membrane called vesicles, already docked at the surface, to fuse with the outer wall and dump their cargo (a few thousand molecules of glutamate each) straight into the cleft. Glutamate diffuses across 20 nanometers in microseconds and latches onto receptor proteins waiting on the receiving dendritic spine. Each bound receptor opens an ion pore, letting Na⁺ trickle into the next cell and nudging its voltage upward in a small bump called an EPSP (excitatory postsynaptic potential).

Every green dot above is an individual glutamate molecule performing a 3D random walk on the GPU, about fifty thousand molecules packed into 36 vesicles. After a vesicle pops open, its molecules scatter across the gap, briefly bind and unbind from receptors on the far face, and eventually drift out of the cleft (where nearby glial cells would vacuum them back up in a real brain). The gap is drawn hundreds of times wider than real life just so you can see inside it.

Press presynaptic spike once and you'll see a few vesicles release and produce a modest voltage bump on the postsynaptic scope, not nearly enough to make the receiving neuron fire on its own. Now press burst of 5: the spikes arrive faster than each EPSP can fade away, so they pile on top of one another (temporal summation) until the next cell crosses threshold and fires. Try dragging release probability down toward 10% and firing single spikes: most of them release zero vesicles at all. Real cortical synapses are surprisingly unreliable like this. Lowering the receptors slider weakens each response proportionally, and changing how many receptors sit in that tiny patch is one of the primary physical knobs the brain turns when you learn something new. 

cleft width             ≈ 20 nm
glutamate per vesicle   ≈ 2,000–5,000 molecules   // 1,400 here
release probability     ≈ 0.1–0.5 per spike, per site
one EPSP                ≈ 0.2–1 mV                // scaled up here so you can see it
synaptic delay          ≈ 0.5 ms
Aside · Glutamate, GABA, and everything else

Almost all fast point-to-point excitation in the brain runs on glutamate, and almost all fast inhibition runs on GABA, whose receptors open chloride pores that pin the membrane below threshold. The famous neuromodulators (dopamine, serotonin, acetylcholine, norepinephrine) work mostly on slower timescales, tuning how excitable cells are and how eagerly those fast synapses respond. Caffeine, alcohol, general anesthetics, and antidepressants all intervene somewhere along this chemical machinery.

Deep dive: Bernard Katz and quantal release

In the 1950s, Bernard Katz recorded from a frog's neuromuscular junction while nothing was stimulating it and noticed tiny spontaneous voltage blips, all almost identical in height. When he did stimulate the nerve weakly, the responses came out as whole-number multiples of that same tiny blip: 0×, 1×, 2×, 3×. He realized neurotransmitter wasn't leaking smoothly through a faucet; it was being dumped in fixed packets, one vesicle per quantum. The release-probability slider implements his exact model: every docked vesicle flips an independent coin, and the EPSP counts how many came up heads.

Step 5 · Cortical circuitsTwenty thousand neurons

A single neuron is mostly a threshold detector. Interesting behavior shows up when thousands of them feed back into one another. The final simulation below wires up a small patch of cortex: twenty thousand neurons (80% excitatory, 20% inhibitory), each connected to a hundred nearby neighbors with transmission delays proportional to distance. That works out to two million live synapses evaluated every step on the GPU.

Running full branching Hodgkin–Huxley cables for twenty thousand cells at once would melt a browser tab, so each cell here is stripped down to a leaky integrate-and-fire unit that keeps the core logic of everything above: incoming spikes nudge the voltage up or down, voltage leaks passively toward rest, crossing threshold emits a spike, and firing resets the cell for a brief refractory pause. The bright arcs flashing across the slab show spikes in flight along a sample of the wiring.

At the starting settings, the slab sits in what theoretical neuroscientists call a balanced state. Every cell is constantly hit by a heavy barrage of excitatory spikes paired with a slightly stronger barrage of inhibitory ones, and it only fires when random fluctuations momentarily tip the sum over threshold. Look at the spike raster on the right: it looks like quiet TV static, with cells firing irregularly and out of step with one another. Healthy awake cortex looks much the same, and Nicolas Brunel's classic 2000 paper worked out the exact phase diagram for why sparse networks settle into this asynchronous hum.

Now pull the inhibition slider down. Right around 4×—the point where the 20% inhibitory population stops counterbalancing the 80% excitatory majority—stability cracks. Firing rates surge, rhythmic waves of synchronized bursts start rolling across the slab, and below 3× the whole network locks into runaway epileptic firing, every cell slamming out spikes as fast as its refractory period lets it. You can also click anywhere on the tissue to poke a local patch and watch how sharply strong inhibition snuffs out the ripple before it can spread.

Deep dive: why deterministic circuits look noisy

In the balanced regime, each neuron's average net input (excitation minus inhibition) hovers just below firing threshold. Spikes only happen when transient coincidences bump it over the line, so the spike trains come out looking Poisson-random even though every internal rule in the network is deterministic. Strong, fast inhibition also shadows every upward swing in excitation within milliseconds, canceling incipient waves of synchrony before they can snowball. Dial that feedback down and nothing stops the positive loop anymore.

Putting it togetherThe full relay

Step back and look at what a single thought or sensation actually travels through: a repeating relay that flips back and forth between analog sums, digital pulses, and diffusing chemistry: 

dendrites   graded potentials       // analog: summing many tiny inputs
soma / AIS  threshold               // analog → digital
axon        action potential        // digital, rebuilt at every patch or node
synapse     transmitter release     // digital → chemical → analog again
next cell   repeat

The figures on this page obviously strip away plenty of real biology. Living dendrites carry active channels of their own and can fire local dendritic spikes; glial cells do far more than wrap myelin and mop up spare glutamate; neuromodulators quietly reshape whole circuits over minutes and hours. Biggest of all, none of the two million synapses in the last figure change strength with experience yet—and synaptic plasticity is where memory and learning actually live.
