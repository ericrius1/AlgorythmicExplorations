Spark & Signal · Part 1

How Neurons Signal

From a single ion to twenty thousand neurons, simulated live. The cell above is listening to its inputs and firing on your GPU right now.

The neuron at the top of this page is a simulation, not an animation. Its shape is split into about six hundred short segments, and each segment is a patch of membrane running the Hodgkin–Huxley equations: a voltage, a few channels opening and closing, current leaking to its neighbours. Synaptic input rains onto the little spines on its branches. Usually that input just makes the branches flicker. Every so often enough of it lines up, the cell fires, and the simulation clock drops to about 1/2,500 of real time so you can watch the spike leave.

Everything you can see is made of particles, up to about 900,000 depending on your screen. The membrane glows with its own voltage. Bright streaks inside the branches are current flowing along them. The amber sparks are sodium ions pouring in and the cyan ones are potassium pouring out, drawn wherever the model's channels are open.

This article takes that cell apart and builds it back up, one layer of physics at a time: where the voltage comes from, how a spike happens, how it travels, how it crosses to the next cell, and what twenty thousand of them do once they're wired together. Each step has a live simulation you can poke at.

Colour key · used in every figure

Indigo is membrane at rest, brightening through violet as it depolarizes and running orange to white during a spike. Amber is Na⁺, cyan is K⁺, green is glutamate (the main excitatory transmitter) and magenta is GABA (the main inhibitory one).

Prologue · The partsOne cell, end to end

A neuron is a signalling pipeline with four stages, and they line up along its shape. The dendrites collect input: thousands of synapses, most of them on spines, each nudging the local voltage up or down by a fraction of a millivolt. Those nudges spread toward the soma, the cell body, and fade as they go. At the start of the axon sits a short stretch packed with sodium channels, the initial segment. This is where the cell decides. If the summed input pushes it past threshold, it fires an all-or-none spike, and the axon carries that spike, unchanged, to the terminals, where it becomes a chemical message to the next cell.

Things to try. Click a branch near the tip of the apical tuft, then one close to the soma. The scope shows the soma's voltage, and the distant input arrives there smaller and later, because charge leaks out through the membrane of a thin branch as it travels down it. Click the axon and the spike starts right there and runs both ways at once, back into the cell body and out toward the terminals. Turn the synaptic input down to 2 and the cell goes quiet. Turn it up to 10 and it fires again and again.

The magenta flashes on the soma are inhibitory synapses. They tend to cluster on or near the cell body, which gives them the last word: an inhibitory input there can cancel excitation from anywhere in the tree.

How the model is built

The shape is grown procedurally: a persistent random walk for each branch, a tapering apical trunk with oblique side branches, a tuft that forks three times, a skirt of basal dendrites, and an axon that alternates myelin with nodes before splitting into a terminal arbor. Each segment's membrane gets the channels its job calls for. Dendrites are mostly passive. The initial segment and the nodes of Ranvier carry many times the sodium channel density of the soma.

Each time step solves the whole tree at once using an implicit (backward Euler) method, which stays stable no matter how tightly the segments are coupled. Michael Hines noticed in 1984 that if every segment's parent is numbered before it, that solve takes one sweep from the leaves to the root and one sweep back. It costs about as much as visiting every segment twice, and it's still how the NEURON simulator works today.

Step 1 · The batteryWhere −70 mV comes from

A neuron sits at about 70 millivolts negative inside, relative to the fluid around it. That voltage is stored energy, and every signal in the nervous system is a controlled release of it. It comes from two facts about the salt water on either side of the membrane. 

// typical mammalian neuron, mM
           inside   outside
K⁺          140        5
Na⁺          12      145
Cl⁻          10      110
A⁻ (proteins, can't cross)  ~140 inside

First, the concentrations are lopsided: potassium is concentrated inside the cell and sodium outside it. Second, the membrane at rest is leaky to potassium and almost sealed to everything else. Potassium channels let K⁺ through about a thousand times more easily than Na⁺.

So potassium wanders out, down its concentration gradient. Each K⁺ that leaves takes a positive charge with it and leaves an unpaired negative charge (mostly the big protein anions, which can't follow) behind. The inside goes negative, and that negative voltage starts pulling potassium back in. The voltage keeps falling until the pull of the voltage exactly balances the push of the gradient. For potassium, that balance point is the Nernst potential: 

E = (kT / ze) · ln(c_outside / c_inside)
E_K  = 26.7 mV · ln(5 / 140)   ≈ −89 mV     // at 37 °C
E_Na = 26.7 mV · ln(145 / 12)  ≈ +66 mV

The figure below runs this from scratch. It has 240,000 ions, each one a GPU particle doing its own random walk, on either side of a patch of membrane. The only way across is through a channel of the right kind. When an ion tries to cross, the simulation applies Boltzmann's rule: downhill against the voltage it always goes, uphill it goes with probability e−zeV/kT. The membrane potential is never set anywhere. It's the net charge that has crossed, divided by the membrane's capacitance, recounted every step.

Press reset and watch it start from 0 mV. Potassium flares cyan as it streams out through the cyan channels, the voltage drops fast, and within a second or two it levels off around −75 mV, give or take the noise. It doesn't reach EK because a few sodium leak channels (the amber rings) let Na⁺ trickle in and pull the voltage back up. The resting potential is a tug of war between the two, weighted by how many channels each ion has.

Now look at the concentration readout. While the voltage falls by 70 mV, the potassium inside barely changes. It takes about 150 ions, out of more than fifty thousand, to charge the membrane. In a real cell the fraction is smaller still, roughly a few ions in every hundred thousand. The membrane is a very thin capacitor, and charging it to −70 mV doesn't use up the battery.

Things to try. Drag the K⁺ leak channels to zero and the cell drifts up toward ENa. Raise the Na⁺ leak and the resting potential creeps toward zero. Turn the pump off and nothing happens at first, but the concentration readout starts to drift: sodium builds up inside, potassium drains out, and the voltage slowly rises. Push the ion count up to a million and the voltage trace gets steadier, because each individual crossing moves it less.

Aside · The pump

The violet rings are Na⁺/K⁺ pumps. Each cycle spends one ATP to carry three Na⁺ out and two K⁺ in, against both gradients. Leaks never stop, so the pumps never stop either. By some estimates they account for about half of the brain's energy use. Because each cycle moves one net positive charge out, the pump also makes the inside a few millivolts more negative on its own.

Deep dive: how many ions is −70 mV?

A cell membrane is two layers of lipid about 5 nm thick: an insulator between two salt solutions, so a capacitor, at about 1 µF per cm². Charging it to 70 mV takes Q = CV ≈ 7 × 10⁻⁸ coulombs per cm², which is about 4,400 ions on every square micrometre. A square micrometre of cytoplasm one micrometre deep holds about 80 million potassium ions.

In the figure, the membrane's capacitance is the one constant that turns "net charge that crossed" into millivolts. A real patch this size would hold millions of times more ions, so relative to the ions it has, the figure's capacitance is huge, and each crossing moves the voltage by about half a millivolt. That's why you can watch individual ions change it, and why the trace is noisy. A real membrane averages over billions of ions and looks smooth.

Step 2 · The spikeOpening the floodgates

The resting potential is a stored state. The action potential is the release: about a millisecond in which the membrane briefly lets sodium flood in and then lets potassium rush out. It needs two new kinds of channel, both voltage-gated: they open and close depending on the voltage across them.

Hodgkin and Huxley worked out how these channels behave in 1952, more than twenty years before anyone could record a single one. Their model gives each channel a few independent "gates". Each gate flips open or shut at random, at rates that depend on voltage. A sodium channel has three fast m gates, which open when the membrane depolarizes, and one slow h gate, which closes when it depolarizes. It conducts only while all four are open. A potassium channel has four slow n gates. That small difference in timing is the whole trick.

This is the same patch of membrane with voltage-gated channels added: 1,600 sodium channels and 480 potassium channels, in clusters of eight, each with its own coin- flipping gates. Press stimulate. The electrode (the glass needle) pushes the voltage up by about 20 mV. That's enough for a few sodium channels' m gates to all land open. Na⁺ floods in through them, which depolarizes the membrane further, which opens more m gates. That loop is the spike. The voltage shoots past zero toward ENa, peaking around +50 mV.

Two slower things end it. The h gates close and lock the sodium channels shut, and the n gates open the potassium channels. K⁺ pours out (watch the cyan flares replace the amber ones) and drags the voltage back down, past rest, into a brief undershoot. The whole thing plays in slow motion here, at about a thousandth of real speed. 

threshold         ≈ 15 mV above rest
peak              ≈ +40 to +50 mV       // heading for E_Na, never reaching it
undershoot        ≈ −90 mV              // heading for E_K
duration          ≈ 1 ms
refractory period ≈ 1–2 ms              // while the h gates recover

Things to try. Lower the stimulus until nothing happens. Below threshold the kick just decays away. Above it you get a full spike every time, the same height no matter how far above. That's what all-or-none means, and it's why a spike can be treated as a bit. Press stimulate twice in quick succession: the second press does nothing while the h gates are still closed. Set the sodium channels to zero and the cell can't fire at all. Set the potassium channels to zero and it still fires, but it can only recover slowly, through the leak.

What's exact and what's a shortcut

The resting figure is fully emergent: every crossing is a particle passing a Boltzmann test. With voltage-gated channels, a second constraint shows up. Ions in this box diffuse far more slowly, relative to the channels, than real ions do, and channels starve. So here each open channel issues "tickets" at the rate Hodgkin and Huxley's current law gives, I = γ(V − E), and nearby ions of the right kind claim them and cross. The current has the right size and direction at every voltage. The ions carrying it are real particles, taken from right next to that channel. The voltage is still nothing but counted charge.

The gates run 3× faster than Hodgkin and Huxley's numbers, which were measured in squid at 6 °C, and their voltage dependence is shifted to sit the same distance above this patch's resting potential as it does above theirs. Each spike here moves far more ions, relative to the box, than a real one would, so the top and bottom walls stand in for the rest of the cell and the fluid around it: an ion that reaches them may be swapped for whichever ion that side is short of, the way a real cell's huge interior keeps its concentrations steady.

Aside · Channel noise

With a few thousand channels flipping at random, the voltage trace never quite sits still. Real neurons have this noise too. In very small cells and thin axons a handful of channels opening by chance can fire a spike nobody asked for. More channels average it out, which is one reason thin axons are expensive to make reliable.

Step 3 · The wireHow a spike travels

A spike at one spot on an axon doesn't stay put. The sodium pouring in at the firing patch spreads along the inside of the axon as current (the bright streaks in these figures) and charges the patch next door. When that patch reaches threshold it fires too, and passes the current on. The spike rebuilds itself at every step, which is how it can travel a metre without fading.

It only goes forward because the membrane behind it is refractory: its sodium channels are still inactivated. Fresh membrane is ahead and spent membrane is behind.

Both axons are the same thickness and get the same kick at the same moment. The top one is bare membrane, so the spike has to regenerate patch by patch all the way along. The bottom one is wrapped in myelin, layers of fatty membrane that cut the capacitance and leak underneath by thirty to fifty times, with bare nodes of Ranvier in the gaps. Current from a firing node hardly charges or leaks out of the wrapped stretch, so it reaches the next node almost immediately. The spike jumps from node to node. This is called saltatory conduction, from the Latin for "to leap".

Here the myelinated axon wins by about five to one. In a body the gap is bigger: thin bare axons conduct at about 1 m/s, and large myelinated ones at up to 120 m/s. Myelin is why a vertebrate can be big and still react quickly. Multiple sclerosis is the immune system stripping it off. Spikes slow, smear out in time, or fail at the damaged stretches.

Things to try. Click the middle of the bare axon: two spikes leave, one each way. Click near both ends in quick succession and the two spikes run into each other in the middle and both disappear, because each one runs into membrane the other has just left refractory. Drag the slow-motion slider right to speed the race up.

Deep dive: the cable equation

Treat the axon as a leaky pipe. Current flows along the inside through the cytoplasm's resistance and leaks out through the membrane. A voltage bump with no channels to regenerate it spreads out and decays over a distance λ, the length constant, which grows with the square root of the axon's radius. It also takes time to charge each patch, set by the membrane time constant τ = RC.

A bare axon's speed scales roughly as λ/τ, so it only grows with the square root of thickness. The squid's way out was a giant axon half a millimetre across. Myelin is the vertebrate solution: lowering C makes τ tiny under the wrapping, so speed grows in proportion to thickness instead, and a nerve can stay thin.

Step 4 · The gapAcross the synapse

Most neurons don't touch. At the end of the axon, the spike reaches a swelling called a bouton, separated from the next cell by a cleft about 20 nanometres wide. No current crosses that gap. The signal crosses as chemistry instead.

When the spike depolarizes the bouton, voltage-gated calcium channels at the active zone open, and Ca²⁺ rushes in (the pale yellow sparks). Calcium is the trigger: it lets the vesicles docked at the membrane fuse with it and dump their contents, a few thousand molecules of glutamate each, into the cleft. Glutamate diffuses across in microseconds and binds receptors on the other side. Each one that binds opens a channel, Na⁺ flows into the next cell, and its voltage rises a little. That rise is an EPSP, an excitatory postsynaptic potential.

Every green point here is one glutamate molecule doing its own random walk on the GPU, about fifty thousand in all. They start packed into vesicles, are released into the cleft, and may stick to a receptor. They let go a moment later and are gone once they wander out of the cleft, where in a real brain transporters on the surrounding glia would mop them up. The cleft is drawn hundreds of times too wide, or you couldn't see into it.

One spike releases a few vesicles and produces one EPSP, which fades. One isn't enough to fire the next cell. Press burst of 5: the releases come faster than each EPSP can fade, they stack (temporal summation), and the next cell crosses threshold. Set the release probability to 10% and many spikes release nothing at all. Real synapses fail like this a lot of the time. Fewer receptors make a weaker synapse. Adding receptors is one of the main ways synapses strengthen when an animal learns, which is the subject of part two. 

cleft width             ≈ 20 nm
glutamate per vesicle   ≈ 2,000–5,000 molecules   // 1,400 here
release probability     ≈ 0.1–0.5 per spike, per site
one EPSP                ≈ 0.2–1 mV                // far bigger here, so you can see it
synaptic delay          ≈ 0.5 ms
Aside · Glutamate and GABA

Almost all fast excitation in the brain uses glutamate. Almost all fast inhibition uses GABA, whose receptors let in chloride and hold the membrane below threshold. Dopamine, serotonin, acetylcholine and the rest act more slowly, mostly by changing how strongly these fast synapses respond. Caffeine, alcohol, anaesthetics and antidepressants all work somewhere in this machinery.

Deep dive: quanta

In the 1950s Bernard Katz noticed that a frog's neuromuscular junction, left alone, produced tiny voltage blips of nearly the same size, and that evoked responses came in whole multiples of that size. Transmitter is released in packets: one vesicle, one quantum. The release-probability slider is his picture directly: each docked vesicle is a coin flip, and the EPSP counts the heads.

Step 5 · The crowdTwenty thousand neurons

One neuron is a decision. A network of them is where computation happens. The last figure is a slab of cortex: twenty thousand neurons, four in five excitatory and one in five inhibitory, each wired to a hundred neighbours with delays that grow with distance. That's two million synapses, all simulated on the GPU each step.

To afford that, each neuron is simplified to a leaky integrate-and-fire model. It keeps the logic of the last four sections and drops the detail. Input adds to the voltage, the voltage leaks back toward rest, crossing threshold means a spike, and a spike means a reset and a brief refractory pause. Every spike adds its weight into each target's input after that connection's delay. The streaks are spikes in flight along a sample of the connections.

At the default settings the network is balanced. Each cell gets a flood of excitation and a matching flood of inhibition, and fires when the two happen to tip its way. The raster looks like static: every cell fires now and then, irregularly, never in step with the rest. That is what healthy cortex looks like too, and Nicolas Brunel's 2000 analysis of exactly this kind of network shows why it settles there.

Now drag the inhibition down. Around 4×, where inhibition stops outweighing the cells' excitatory input, the balance breaks. Firing rises, waves of synchronized activity sweep the slab, and below 3× it runs away: almost every cell fires as fast as its refractory period allows. That is a seizure in miniature. In epilepsy, something has pushed a real circuit across the same line. Click anywhere to kick a region and watch how far the burst spreads before inhibition reins it in.

Deep dive: why balance makes noise

Each neuron's mean input, excitation minus inhibition, sits just below threshold. Fluctuations around that mean push it over at random moments, so spikes come out irregular even though every rule in the model is deterministic apart from the outside input. The randomness is produced by the network. Strong inhibition also tracks excitation quickly, so any wave of synchrony gets cancelled before it builds. Weaken it and nothing cancels the waves.

EpilogueThe pipeline

Put together, a neural signal is a chain of conversions, each with its own physics: 

dendrites   graded potentials       // analog: summing many small inputs
soma / AIS  threshold               // analog → digital
axon        action potential        // digital, regenerated at every patch or node
synapse     transmitter release     // digital → chemical → analog again
next cell   repeat

Every sensation you've ever had came through that chain. The figures above are simplified, but the mechanism in them is the real one: ions on either side of a thin film, channels that open with voltage, current that spreads, molecules that cross a gap.

A lot was left out. Real dendrites have their own channels and can produce local spikes. Glia feed neurons, insulate them and clean up after them. Neuromodulators retune whole circuits over minutes and hours. And synapses change with use, which is the biggest omission of all.
