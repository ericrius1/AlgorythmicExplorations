Strange Matter · Part 1

The Ferrofluid

A magnetic liquid, simulated as 24,000 particles and re-meshed every frame on your GPU. Move your cursor over the pool to bring a magnet down on it.

Scroll down to read ↓

Ferrofluid is oil with grains of magnetite mixed into it, each about ten nanometres across and coated so they don't clump. The grains are small enough that thermal motion keeps them from settling, so the whole liquid answers a magnet instead of the iron separating out. Put a strong magnet under a dish of it and the surface stands up into a regular crown of spikes. That's the Rosensweig instability, and it's what most people picture when they hear the word.

The pool at the top of the page is where this series ends up by part two: every particle magnetized by a uniform field, with your cursor as one extra magnet. This article builds the pieces under it. The simulation reuses the particles, grid sort and double-density relaxation from Sorting Water and the cohesion force from The Lava Lamp, then adds a magnet. The rendering is new. Instead of thresholding a blurry field pixel by pixel, it pulls the surface out as line segments with marching squares, every frame, on the GPU.

Aside · A rocket fuel that never flew

Ferrofluid came out of NASA. In the early 1960s Steve Papell, at what is now the Glenn Research Center, was working on a weightlessness problem: in orbit, fuel floats away from the pump inlet. He proposed grinding magnetic particles into the propellant so a magnetic field could steer it, and patented the fluid in 1965. It was never used for fuel. It ended up in loudspeakers, where it cools and damps the voice coil, and in rotary shaft seals. Ronald Rosensweig worked out much of the theory (he wrote the textbook, Ferrohydrodynamics), and in 1967 he and Michael Cowley explained the spikes, which now carry his name.

Step 1 · Three forcesWhy spikes form

A spike is the result of three forces pulling against each other. Gravity wants the surface flat. Surface tension wants it flat too: it charges for every extra bit of surface, so it rounds off anything sharp. The sim uses the Akinci-style cohesion force the lava lamp used to keep its wax in one piece. The magnet pulls the other way.

A magnetizable liquid gets pulled toward wherever the field is strongest. The force follows the gradient of the field's energy density, F ∝ ∇|B|², and for a dipole magnet that gradient has two parts: 

|B|² ∝ (3cos²θ + 1) / r⁶     // strongest on the magnet's axis
F ∝ ∇|B|²                     // toward the magnet, and sideways toward its axis

The radial part pulls fluid toward the magnet. The angular part is what makes a spike instead of a mound: fluid off to the side gets pushed in toward the axis, so it gathers into a column lined up with the field. Surface tension blunts the tip and gravity limits the height. In a real dish under a uniform field this balance repeats across the whole surface, and the spikes space themselves out at a distance set by gravity and tension alone: the crown first appears with a spacing of 2π times the capillary length, √(σ/ρg). With one small magnet, as here, you get one spike under it.

In code the magnet is one more term in the force kernel, the same way the dome, the tank walls and the lamp's buoyancy were. The rest of the SPH is unchanged: 

// the dipole sits just inside the bar; s is the magnet's size
let dm = p - (mag + magDir * s);
let q = dot(dm, dm) + s * s;                  // softened r²
let g = 1e-7 / (q * q * q);                   // |B|² falls off as 1/r⁶
let radial = -6.0 * r / q * (3.0 * cth * cth + 1.0) * g * rhat;
let angular = 6.0 * cth * g / sqrt(q) * (magDir - cth * rhat);
var macc = magStrength * (radial + angular);
let cap = magCap * gravity;                   // magCap = 0.9
if (length(macc) > cap) { macc *= cap / length(macc); }
acc += macc;

Two details aren't physics. The pull is capped at 90% of gravity, so the fluid can climb toward the magnet but never quite leaves the pool and jumps onto it. And the magnet's bar is solid: fluid that touches it gets pushed back out, the same penalty springs the tray walls use.

Step 2 · Geometry from a fieldMarching squares

The lava lamp drew its wax by splatting each particle into a blurry density field and shading every pixel past a threshold. That worked because wax is soft and the lamp is dim. Ferrofluid is the opposite: glossy and nearly black, so almost everything you see of it is the outline and the reflections along it. I wanted the surface itself, as geometry.

The surface is the contour where the splatted field crosses the threshold, and marching squares extracts it. Lay a lattice over the field and look at each square cell. Mark each of its four corners as inside or outside. That's sixteen possible patterns, and each one says which of the cell's edges the contour crosses. Interpolate along those edges to place the crossing points, and the cell contributes zero, one or two line segments. All the cells together make the surface.

That's the extracted mesh by itself, zoomed in. Each cyan stroke is one segment from one cell of the faint lattice, about 2.6 particle spacings wide, and the dots are the segment ends. With nobody at the controls the magnet drifts slowly back and forth. Put your cursor near the surface and watch the chain rebuild itself under it.

On the GPU the extraction is one compute pass, one thread per cell. A cell that finds a crossing appends its segments to a buffer through an atomic counter, the same wait-free append the gravity series used for its scatters. Then a one-thread pass copies the final count into the arguments of an indirect draw, so the GPU sizes and draws the mesh without the CPU ever waiting on it. The count in the readout is copied back separately, a frame or two late, only so it can be printed.

Aside · Marching cubes

The 3D version, marching cubes, has 256 corner cases instead of 16. William Lorensen and Harvey Cline published it at SIGGRAPH in 1987 for pulling surfaces out of CT and MRI scans, and it became one of the most cited papers in computer graphics. It was also patented, which was part of the reason people used marching tetrahedra instead. The patent expired in 2005. Part three uses marching tetrahedra.

The two saddle cases

Fourteen of the sixteen patterns have only one sensible answer. The other two are saddles: two diagonal corners inside, the other two outside. They can be read two ways, as one blob with a thin neck or as two blobs nearly touching, and the four corners can't tell you which. The shader breaks the tie by sampling the field once more at the middle of the cell. When a drop pinches off a spike, that extra sample decides the frame in which it separates.

Step 3 · AssemblyThe dish

Here's everything together: 20,000 particles stepped five times a frame, the cursor magnet, and the mesh, this time filled and shaded. The field is drawn at half the canvas resolution and blurred twice. A narrow blur takes the particle-sized bumps off the surface, and that's the one that gets meshed. A much wider blur acts as a rough height map. Its slope tilts the shading normal near the edge, so the fluid looks rounded toward you instead of cut out of paper.

The body is almost pure black, because real ferrofluid absorbs nearly all the light that gets into it. What makes it read as liquid is what it reflects. The shader reflects a small made-up studio: a bright softbox overhead, a warm strip on the left, a cool one on the right. The segments along the top of the surface pick up whichever of those their outward normal faces, and the scene goes through the same bloom and filmic tone mapping as the particle demos.

Bring the magnet down toward the pool and a cone climbs up to meet it, leaning after the magnet as you move. Tension shows mostly on the cone's sides: at zero they go ragged, and turning it up smooths them. The magnet strength slider changes how far the pull reaches. It can't make the pull stronger than the 90% cap, which is why the cone never jumps the gap. The view button steps through the stages: final, the mesh, the raw field with its threshold contour, and the particles under all of it, coloured by speed.

Watch the segment count in the readout while you do. It sits a little over 400 with the magnet idling, and goes up when a cone rises, because a longer outline takes more segments. Nothing about the mesh is kept from one frame to the next. Like the gravity series' trees, it's cheaper to rebuild from scratch in parallel than to update in place.

EpilogueWhat carries over

  • A new force is one more term. The magnet slots into the same kernel as pressure and cohesion, and nothing else in the SPH has to know.
  • Spikes are a balance. The magnet pulls fluid in toward its axis, and tension and gravity decide how tall and how sharp the result gets.
  • Geometry can come out of a field every frame. Particles to field to segments is three GPU passes, and the counter and indirect draw keep the CPU out of it.
  • Black things are drawn by their reflections. The fluid's colour barely matters. The room it reflects does all the work.

Things I'd try next: magnetize the fluid itself, so a uniform field can grow a whole crown instead of one spike; move the meshing to 3D, where marching squares becomes marching cubes and segments become triangles; and find an extractor that keeps sharp tips sharp, since marching anything rounds off the exact feature that makes ferrofluid look like ferrofluid. Part two does the first, part three the other two.

Next: Part two, The Crown. Every particle becomes a small magnet, and a field with no pull at all raises a row of evenly spaced spikes.
