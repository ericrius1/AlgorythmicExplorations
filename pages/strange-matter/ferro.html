Strange Matter · Part 1

The Ferrofluid

24,000 magnetic particles re-meshed every frame on your GPU. Move your cursor over the pool to bring a magnet down toward the surface.

Scroll down to read ↓

Grind magnetite into grains roughly ten nanometers wide, coat each grain with a thin surfactant so it refuses to clump, and stir the dust into oil. At that scale thermal motion keeps the iron permanently suspended. Bring a magnet nearby and the particles never separate out of the carrier oil; the whole black puddle rises to meet the field together. Under a strong enough vertical field, a flat dish of the stuff erupts into a tidy geometric crown of spikes, an effect called the Rosensweig instability.

The pool at the top of the page actually jumps ahead to the end of Part 2, where every particle becomes a magnet in its own right under a uniform field (with your cursor dropping in an extra dipole). Before tackling mutual magnetization, we need a working base layer. Fortunately, most of the fluid engine already exists: we can borrow the particle layout, spatial grid sort, and double-density relaxation from Sorting Water, grab the cohesion force from The Lava Lamp, and drop a single dipole magnet into the force pass. The big new piece here is rendering: pulling the liquid's outline out as crisp line segments every frame with marching squares on the GPU.

Aside · A rocket fuel that never flew

Ferrofluid started as a NASA plumbing problem. In the early 1960s, Steve Papell at what is now the Glenn Research Center wanted a way to keep liquid fuel near the pump inlet in zero gravity. His idea was to suspend magnetic dust in propellant and herd it with electromagnets, earning a patent in 1965. Nobody ever flew magnetic rocket fuel, though the fluid found a long career on Earth: damping and cooling loudspeaker voice coils, and forming near-frictionless rotary shaft seals. Around the same time, Ronald Rosensweig built the mathematical foundation (later collected in his textbook Ferrohydrodynamics), and in 1967 he and Michael Cowley worked out why the surface breaks into spikes.

Step 1 · Three forcesWhy spikes form

Every ferrofluid peak is an uneasy truce between three competing pulls. Gravity drags raised fluid back down toward a flat pool, and surface tension helps it by penalizing extra surface area and blunting sharp tips (in our particle sim, that comes from the Akinci-style cohesion term we used to hold lava-lamp wax together). The magnet pulls the opposite way, yanking fluid into whichever region has the strongest magnetic field.

For a magnetizable liquid, the body force tracks the gradient of the field's squared magnitude, F ∝ ∇|B|². Write that out for a single dipole magnet and the gradient splits naturally into two components: 

|B|² ∝ (3cos²θ + 1) / r⁶     // strongest along the magnet's axis
F ∝ ∇|B|²                     // pulls toward the pole and inward toward the axis

The radial part lifts liquid straight up toward the pole. The angular part is what turns a sluggish bulge into a pointed cone: fluid off to either side gets squeezed inward toward the magnet's center axis, piling into a narrow column aligned with the field. Gravity sets a ceiling on how high the column can climb, and surface tension decides how sharp its tip can get. When a uniform field spans an entire dish, this same tug-of-war repeats across the surface at a wavelength dictated purely by gravity and surface tension: the first crown appears with a spacing of 2π times the capillary length, √(σ/ρg). With one small bar magnet hovering over a tray, we get a single spike tracking underneath it.

In the SPH compute shader, the magnet drops cleanly into the existing force pass alongside pressure, cohesion, and walls: 

// dipole sits just inside the bar; s is the softening radius
let dm = p - (mag + magDir * s);
let q = dot(dm, dm) + s * s;                  // softened r²
let g = 1e-7 / (q * q * q);                   // |B|² falls off as 1/r⁶
let radial = -6.0 * r / q * (3.0 * cth * cth + 1.0) * g * rhat;
let angular = 6.0 * cth * g / sqrt(q) * (magDir - cth * rhat);
var macc = magStrength * (radial + angular);
let cap = magCap * gravity;                   // magCap = 0.9
if (length(macc) > cap) { macc *= cap / length(macc); }
acc += macc;

You might notice two pragmatic shortcuts hiding in those lines. First, the magnetic acceleration caps out at 90% of gravity (magCap = 0.9), which lets the fluid stretch eagerly toward the pole without ever tearing free of the pool and plastering itself onto the bar. Second, the bar magnet itself acts as a solid boundary using the same penalty springs as the tray walls, so even if you dip it straight into a wave, particles bounce cleanly off the steel.

Step 2 · Geometry from a fieldMarching squares

For the lava lamp, splatting every particle into a soft density texture and shading any pixel above a threshold worked well enough because glowing wax is soft and forgiving. Ferrofluid is dark, oily, and mirror-glossy. Almost everything your eye reads as liquid lives along the sharp silhouette and the bright reflections skimming its rim, so I wanted to extract the surface itself as explicit geometry.

The boundary we want is the contour where the splatted density field crosses a fixed threshold, and marching squares pulls it out cleanly. Lay a regular square grid over the field and test the four corners of each cell against the threshold. With four binary inside-or-outside corners, there are 16 possible patterns, and each pattern tells you which edges of the square the contour must cross. Linearly interpolate along each crossed edge to find the exact crossing coordinates, connect them, and the cell outputs zero, one, or two line segments. Stitch every cell's output together and you have a complete polyline wrapping the liquid.

Above is a zoomed-in look at that extracted chain by itself. Each cyan stroke comes from one square of the faint background lattice, sized at roughly 2.6 particle spacings wide so you can see the individual segments and the bright dots marking their endpoints. Left alone, the magnet drifts gently back and forth; move your cursor over the pool to take control and watch new segments pop in and out as the crest rises under the pole.

On the GPU, the whole extraction finishes in a single compute pass with one thread per lattice cell. Whenever a cell finds one or two crossings, it reserves slots in a flat segment buffer using an atomic counter, the same lock-free append pattern the gravity demos used for tree building. A tiny single-thread pass then writes that final count straight into an indirect draw buffer. The GPU sizes and renders the mesh completely on its own without ever stalling for the CPU. The segment count printed in the readout bar is copied back asynchronously a frame or two later purely for display.

Aside · Marching cubes

Step up to 3D and marching squares becomes marching cubes, jumping from 16 corner patterns to 256. William Lorensen and Harvey Cline introduced it at SIGGRAPH in 1987 to reconstruct surfaces from medical CT and MRI scans, and it quickly became one of the most cited papers in computer graphics. Because the original algorithm was patented until 2005, plenty of graphics programmers routed around it with marching tetrahedra instead, which is the first 3D extractor we'll build in Part 3.

The two saddle cases

Fourteen of the sixteen corner patterns have a single unambiguous wiring. The remaining two are diagonal saddles: two opposite corners sit inside the fluid while the other two sit outside. You can connect the four edge crossings either as one continuous neck or as two separate droplets barely missing each other, and the four corners alone cannot tell which is right. Our compute shader settles the tie by sampling the density field one more time at the exact center of the cell. Whenever a droplet pinches off a tall spike, that extra center sample decides the precise frame it breaks free.

Step 3 · AssemblyThe dish

Putting everything together gives us 20,000 particles integrated across five substeps per frame, the cursor dipole, and the live marching-squares surface, now filled and shaded. The particles splat into a half-resolution density texture that goes through two blurs. A tight blur irons out individual particle bumps so marching squares sees a clean liquid contour. A much wider blur acts like a smooth height field: near the boundary, its slope tilts the shading normal outward toward the viewer, giving the flat 2D slab a subtle rounded bevel instead of a paper-cutout edge.

Because real ferrofluid swallows nearly all light that enters it, the interior body color is almost pure black. To make it read as wet oil, the shader reflects an imaginary photo studio: a bright overhead softbox, a warm light strip off to the left, and a cool strip to the right, plus a tight key highlight. The extracted edge segments sample the same studio based on their outward normals, and the finished HDR image passes through subtle bloom and filmic tone mapping.

Hover above the liquid and a glossy hump rises to meet the glowing pole, leaning smoothly as you sweep across the tray. Dragging surface tension down toward zero lets the cone's flanks tear and ripple; pushing it higher pulls the surface taut and smooths out the silhouette. The magnet strength slider controls how far the dipole reaches, though the 90% gravity cap still keeps the peak from leaping across the gap onto the bar. Clicking view cycles through the whole pipeline, from the final shaded oil to the bare cyan mesh, the raw density field with its glowing threshold line, and the underlying particles colored by speed, while re-pour resets the tray.

Keep an eye on the segment counter at the bottom while you drag the magnet around. It hovers around 400 segments while the pool idles, then climbs as soon as a tall cone stretches out the perimeter. Every single segment is discarded and rebuilt from scratch in parallel on the next frame, because on a GPU regenerating a few hundred cells is vastly simpler and faster than trying to patch an existing polyline in place.

EpilogueFrom one spike to a crown

Building this piece reinforced two habits that pay off constantly in GPU simulation. On the physics side, dropping a dipole magnet into the force pass required zero changes to the sorting grid, density solver, or boundary springs; once a particle engine is in place, new physical forces stay cleanly self-contained. On the graphics side, converting particles to a field and a field to live geometry takes just a handful of parallel passes, and pairing an atomic counter with an indirect draw keeps the CPU completely out of the hot loop. And shading pitch-black liquid turns out to be mostly lighting design: the base color barely registers next to the studio reflections skimming its rim.

Still, one hovering bar magnet can only ever raise one local cone at a time. To grow an authentic Rosensweig crown out of a uniform field, the fluid itself has to become magnetic so particles can pull and push on their own neighbors. Part 2 adds mutual magnetization so a crown can sprout on its own, and Part 3 moves the whole dish into three dimensions to hunt for a surface extractor that keeps sharp ferrofluid tips intact rather than slicing them flat the way marching methods tend to do.

Next: Part two, The Crown. Every particle becomes a small magnet, and a field with no pull at all raises a row of evenly spaced spikes.
