Strange Matter · Part 3

Sharp Relief

26,000 magnetized particles in a dish, turned into a fresh triangle mesh every frame. Drag to orbit; hover to hold a magnet over the pool.

Scroll down to read ↓

Adding a third dimension to a fluid simulation sounds modest until you try to run it at sixty frames a second. The physics from Part 1 and Part 2 carries straight over, and the magnetic dipole formulas actually get cleaner in 3D than the 2D cross-section workaround. Almost everything surrounding the math gets harder: neighbor loops explode cubically, hardware alpha-blending disappears because GPUs cannot blend particle quads into a 3D volume texture, and turning that volume into glossy spikes forces a choice between competing surface extractors.

This final part lifts the entire magnetized pool into a 3D dish, splats its density into a 128³ voxel volume using fixed-point integer atomics, and pits three real-time meshing algorithms against one another on the exact same scalar field: marching tetrahedra, surface nets, and dual contouring. Swap between them on a live crown and you can watch triangle counts, surface smoothness, and spike tips change immediately.

Step 1 · Scaling costsOne more dimension

The first wall you hit in 3D is the neighbor search. The spatial sorting grid shifts from 256² cells to 64³, and checking a single cell radius now means visiting a 3 × 3 × 3 cube of 27 cells instead of a 3 × 3 square of 9. The GPU counting sort keeps the same histogram, prefix scan, and scatter layout from Sorting Water, scaled up so the scan folds 1,024 block sums instead of 256. Each particle record doubles to 64 bytes now that position, velocity, magnetic moment, and cached magnetic acceleration all carry three components. 

let center = cellOf(p.position);
for (var dz = -reach; dz <= reach; dz++) {
  for (var dy = -reach; dy <= reach; dy++) {
    for (var dx = -reach; dx <= reach; dx++) {
      let c = center + vec3i(dx, dy, dz);
      for (var k = cellStart[c]; k < cellStart[c] + cellCount[c]; k++) {
        accumulate(p, sorted[k]);
      }
    }
  }
}
// reach = 1 → 27 cells for pressure; reach = 3 → 343 cells for magnetics

Short-range SPH pressure stays manageable at 27 cells, whereas magnetics gets expensive in a hurry because dipole fields reach much farther. Part 2 summed magnetic pairs out to six kernel radii, which was a 13 × 13 square in 2D. Cube that reach in 3D and every particle would have to inspect 2,197 cells per pass; even four radii costs 729. To keep things interactive, the solver trims the magnetic reach to three radii: 343 cells and roughly 900 neighbors deep inside the bulk. Because the near-field attraction that pulls particles into vertical columns lives mostly inside two kernel radii, that cutoff preserves spike formation while discarding the long tail of side-by-side repulsion between distant peaks. It is a real compromise, and stretching the reach farther would likely nudge the crown's equilibrium spacing.

Amortizing magnetics across substeps matters even more here than it did in 2D. The moment relaxation sweep and pairwise magnetic force pass execute once per rendered frame on the first of four substeps, caching the resulting magnetic acceleration right on each particle for the remaining three substeps while pressure, cohesion, and dish collisions continue stepping.

Step 2 · Native dimensionMagnetics in 3D

For once, moving up a dimension simplifies the physics code. Huang, Hädrich, and Michels originally derived their smoothed dipole in three dimensions back in 2019, and Part 2 had to adapt it into 2D line dipoles for its vertical slice. In a full 3D dish, every particle carries a 3D magnetic moment and every neighbor contributes the original spherical solution: the SPH kernel W, its average W̄ over a ball of radius r, and the classic 1/3 self-field factor for a sphere.

With a full horizontal surface to play with, spikes can sprout anywhere across the floor rather than lining up in a single row. In a broad laboratory dish they settle into a tidy hexagonal pattern; in this compact, shallow dish you get several bustling rows of peaks constantly nudging each other for space. Just as in Part 2, the uniform vertical field has zero gradient and pulls on nothing by itself, doing its work strictly by magnetizing the particles so their mutual dipole forces organize the spikes. Hovering your cursor over the dish parks one large smoothed dipole above the oil, adding a real local gradient that draws a tall column straight underneath it.

Step 3 · A field with no pixelsSplatting a volume

In Part 1, building the 2D density field was effortless: draw a soft quad for each particle and let the GPU's fixed-function alpha blender sum the overlaps into a texture. WebGPU has no additive blending into 3D textures, so splatting becomes an explicit compute pass where each particle walks the small box of voxels within its kernel radius and adds its weight into every touched cell.

The density grid measures 128 × 128 × 128, roughly two million voxels occupying eight megabytes. Fitting that volume tightly around the dish instead of the entire simulation box shrinks each voxel down to 0.0075 world units, about half a particle spacing. Because a particle's splat radius spans roughly five voxels in each direction, every frame triggers around ten million concurrent voxel updates across thousands of GPU threads.

Letting thousands of threads write to shared voxels simultaneously requires atomic addition, and WGSL only provides atomics for 32-bit integers, never floats. The workaround is fixed-point math: multiply each particle's kernel weight by 256, round to an unsigned integer, and accumulate it with atomicAdd into a u32 storage buffer. When an extractor reads a voxel back, dividing by 256 recovers the floating-point density. Quantization error stays down around a few thousandths of a particle weight, well below anything visible in the final mesh, and integer addition buys a welcome bonus: unlike floating-point addition, integer sums do not depend on arrival order, so the density field comes out bit-for-bit deterministic no matter how the GPU schedules threads. 

// one thread per particle, over every voxel inside the splat radius
let q2 = dot(center - p, center - p) / (R * R);
let w = (1.0 - q2) * (1.0 - q2);
atomicAdd(&field[voxel], u32(round(w * 256.0)));

// when the surface extractors sample the volume
let density = f32(field[voxel]) / 256.0;

Step 4 · Triangles from a fieldMarching tetrahedra

At this point there is a scalar density sitting at every voxel corner, an invisible threshold surface threading between them, and a rasterizer waiting for triangles. Since 1987 the textbook tool has been marching cubes: inspect every 2 × 2 × 2 cube of corners, classify each of the eight corners as inside or outside, and index into a 256-entry lookup table emitting up to five triangles per cell. That table works well once debugged, though a single mistyped index leaves pinholes across every mesh you generate.

Marching tetrahedra skips the giant lookup table altogether. Slice each cubic cell into six tetrahedra fanned around its main diagonal. Each tetrahedron has just four corners and 16 sign configurations: either completely empty, completely full, one corner separated from the other three (producing one triangle), or two corners on each side (producing a quad, split into two triangles). Sixteen cases are small enough to write from scratch and verify by eye. The catch is geometry bloat: dicing every grid cell into six tetrahedra slices the surface much more finely than needed, leaving marching tetrahedra with roughly three times the triangle count of the dual extractors coming up next. 

for (var t = 0u; t < 6u; t++) {             // six tets around corner 0 → corner 6
  let mask = signs of the tet's 4 corners;   // 16 simple cases
  interpolate crossings along its edges;
  emit 0, 1, or 2 triangles;
}

Six tetrahedra, one diagonal, no winding

All six tetrahedra share the cube's main diagonal running from corner 0 to corner 6, arranged around it like orange segments. Interior faces meet cleanly inside the cell, and every outer square face splits along the exact same diagonal as the neighboring cell touching it, guaranteeing a watertight surface with no cracks. Vertex normals are sampled from the density field's gradient and interpolated to each edge crossing rather than derived from flat triangle faces, keeping reflections smooth. Because the tetrahedra emit triangles without a consistent clockwise winding, the render pipeline leaves backface culling off and the fragment shader flips any normal pointing away from the camera.

Step 5 · The dual ideaOne vertex per cell

Marching cubes and marching tetrahedra both plant their vertices directly on grid edges and process each cell in isolation. Dual extractors flip that geometry inside out. Place a single vertex inside every cell the surface passes through, positioned wherever it best represents the local patch of surface, and then stitch the vertices of neighboring cells into faces. Connectivity suddenly becomes trivial: every grid edge whose endpoints sit on opposite sides of the threshold is shared by four surrounding cells, and connecting those four cells' vertices yields one quad (two triangles). No lookup tables are needed, and the mesh comes out watertight by design.

The cleanest entry point into dual meshing is the surface net, introduced by Sarah Gibson in 1998 to smooth away the blocky staircase artifacts in segmented medical volumes. A surface net puts each cell's vertex at the simple average (the mass point) of all edge crossings belonging to that cell. Because averaging nudges every vertex inward toward the middle of its cell, surface nets produce silky, low-triangle meshes on smooth waves, at the expense of rounding down sharp corners and tall spike tips.

Step 6 · The sharp edgeDual contouring

Dual contouring (Ju, Losasso, Schaefer, and Warren, 2002) keeps the exact same one-vertex-per-cell connectivity as surface nets, while using one extra clue the density volume already holds: its gradient. Every edge crossing reveals both where the surface passes and which direction it faces, yielding a point and a normal, which together define a local tangent plane (often called Hermite data). Dual contouring moves the cell's vertex to the spot that minimizes squared distance to all of the cell's tangent planes at once. On a flat flank those planes all agree and the vertex stays near the mass point; inside a cell straddling a crease or a sharp spike tip, opposing tangent planes intersect right at the feature, pulling the vertex cleanly up into the point.

Finding the exact least-squares minimum solves a small 3 × 3 quadratic error function (QEF) per cell, typically via a singular value decomposition. In real-time GPU code, an iterative shortcut gets nearly all the way there with far less shader math: start at the mass point and take ten small gradient steps toward the tangent planes. On each step, project the current point onto every tangent plane in the cell, average the offsets, scale by a step factor, and clamp the result inside the cell bounds. That step factor is the sharpness slider in the final demo below. Set it to zero and the vertex never leaves the mass point, giving you pure surface nets; push it to one and it walks straight toward the plane intersection. 

var x = massPoint;
for (var it = 0u; it < 10u; it++) {
  var corr = vec3f(0.0);
  for (var k = 0u; k < count; k++) {
    corr += n[k] * dot(p[k] - x, n[k]);   // signed projection onto tangent plane k
  }
  x += corr * (sharpness / f32(count));
  x = clamp(x, cellMin, cellMax);
}
Aside · Why sharpness is a slider

Dual contouring has a famous Achilles' heel: when surface normals get noisy, nearly parallel tangent planes can intersect far outside their voxel, causing quads to pinch and fold over themselves. Because these normals come from a density grid re-splatted by thousands of moving particles every frame, they always carry a faint jitter. Clamping vertices to their cell prevents wild spikes, and backing sharpness slightly below 1 trades a sliver of tip crispness for a calmer reflection. Offline QEF solvers handle the same instability by blending a weak pull toward the mass point directly into the error matrix.

EpilogueWhat the series built

Next to the offline 2022 KAUST simulator, this 3D dish is still a lightweight sketch. The reference paper solves pressure implicitly, integrates overlapping dipole forces exactly, sums magnetic fields over all pairs rather than truncating at three kernel radii, and spends seconds rendering a single frame. The WebGPU version here keeps every stage resident on one GPU at interactive speed: grid sort, warm-started magnetization, current-loop pair forces, fixed-point volume splat, indirect-drawn surface extraction, and studio shading. The CPU writes a few hundred bytes of uniforms per frame and never waits on a single readback.

Across all three essays, a handful of engineering choices did most of the heavy lifting. Running particles into a scalar field and extracting live geometry via atomic counters and indirect draws worked identically for 2D segments and 3D triangles. Caching expensive long-range magnetic passes on the first substep kept cubic 3D neighbor loops inside a frame budget. Fixed-point integer atomics turned a missing WGSL feature into a race-free, bit-deterministic volume splat. And comparing marching tetrahedra against dual contouring made one lesson concrete: placing vertices intelligently matters far more than throwing extra triangles at a grid, cutting triangle counts by two-thirds while keeping sharper geometry. Pushing this further from here would mean replacing the three-radius cutoff with a fast multipole field solver, upgrading the ten-step relaxation to a regularized QEF solver, and scaling up to a deeper dish with hundreds of thousands of particles so the peaks taper to true needle points, maybe even watching ferrofluid climb a magnetized sphere along the way.
