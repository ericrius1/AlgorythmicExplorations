Strange Matter · Part 3

Sharp Relief

26,000 magnetized particles in a dish, turned into a fresh triangle mesh every frame. Drag to orbit. Hover to hold a magnet over the pool.

Scroll down to read ↓

Part one built a 2D pipeline from particles to a field to line segments. Part two made the particles magnetize each other and grew a self-spacing crown, still in cross-section. This part moves both halves into three dimensions: the simulation, and the step that turns it into a surface. Then it compares three ways of doing that second step: marching tetrahedra, surface nets, and dual contouring.

The physics carries over from part two: the same moment sweep, the same smoothed dipoles, the same current loop force pointing inward at the surface. Most of what's new is on the geometry side. Run three extractors over the same field and you get different triangle counts, different smoothness, and different spike tips.

Step 1 · Scaling costsOne more dimension

Adding a dimension changes what the neighbour search costs. The sort grid goes from 256² cells to 64³, and each particle's search now visits a 3 × 3 × 3 block, 27 cells instead of 9. The counting sort is the same histogram, prefix scan and scatter as in Sorting Water, except the scan now folds 1,024 block sums instead of 256. Each particle grows to 64 bytes, since position, velocity, magnetic moment and the cached magnetic acceleration all have three components now. 

let center = cellOf(p.position);
for (var dz = -reach; dz <= reach; dz++) {
  for (var dy = -reach; dy <= reach; dy++) {
    for (var dx = -reach; dx <= reach; dx++) {
      let c = center + vec3i(dx, dy, dz);
      for (var k = cellStart[c]; k < cellStart[c] + cellCount[c]; k++) {
        accumulate(p, sorted[k]);
      }
    }
  }
}
// reach = 1 → 27 cells for pressure; reach = 3 → 343 cells for magnetics

Magnetics is the expensive part, because it reaches much further than pressure. Part two summed pair magnetics out to six kernel radii, which in 2D is a 13 × 13 block of cells. In 3D that would be 2,197 cells per particle, and even four radii would be 729. Here I cut it at three radii: 343 cells and around 900 neighbours inside the bulk. The near field that gathers fluid into columns sits inside two radii, so the cutoff keeps that and drops the long tail of the side-by-side push between columns. That's a real approximation, and a longer reach would probably shift the spacing some.

The per-frame trick from part two matters even more here. The moment sweep and the pair forces run once per frame, on the first of four substeps, and the result is cached on each particle for the other three. Particles barely move in a sixtieth of a second, so the most expensive passes become a once-a-frame cost with no visible difference.

Step 2 · Native dimensionMagnetics in 3D

The magnetic maths gets simpler, if anything. The smoothed dipole was derived in 3D in the first place, by Huang and colleagues in 2019, and part two had to rework it for line dipoles in a cross-section. Here each particle carries a 3D moment and each neighbour's field is the original solution: the kernel W, its average W̄ over a ball, and a self-field factor of 1/3.

Spikes can now form anywhere on the surface instead of along a line. In a lab dish they settle into a roughly hexagonal pattern. This dish is small and shallow, so you get a few rows of them that keep jostling for position. As before, the uniform field has no gradient and pulls on nothing; it only magnetizes the particles, and their pair forces do the rest. The cursor adds one big smoothed dipole hovering over the dish, and that one does pull.

Step 3 · A field with no pixelsSplatting a volume

Part one built its field by drawing a small blurry quad per particle and letting the blending hardware add them up. There's no way to blend into a 3D texture like that, so the splat becomes a compute pass. Each particle walks the block of voxels within its radius and adds its kernel weight to each one.

The field is 128³, two million voxels and eight megabytes, fitted around the dish rather than the whole simulation box, so a voxel is 0.0075 units wide, about half a particle spacing. A particle's radius covers about five voxels each way, so a frame's worth of fluid makes roughly ten million additions, from thousands of threads at once.

Adding from many threads at once means atomics, and WGSL has no floating-point atomics. So the field is fixed point: scale each weight by 256, round to an integer, and atomicAdd it into a 32-bit voxel. Dividing by 256 on the way out gives the density back. The rounding error is a few thousandths of one particle's weight, far below anything the extractors can see. A bonus: integer addition doesn't care about order, unlike floating point, so the field comes out the same bit for bit no matter which thread gets there first. 

// one thread per particle, for every voxel within the splat radius
let q2 = dot(center - p, center - p) / (R * R);
let w = (1.0 - q2) * (1.0 - q2);
atomicAdd(&field[voxel], u32(round(w * 256.0)));

// in the extractors
let density = f32(field[voxel]) / 256.0;

Step 4 · Triangles from a fieldMarching tetrahedra

The field is a number at every voxel, the surface is where those numbers cross the threshold, and the renderer wants triangles. The standard answer since 1987 is marching cubes: visit every 2 × 2 × 2 cell, mark its eight corners inside or outside, and look up the result. That's 256 corner patterns, each with a recipe of up to five triangles, and one wrong entry in the table leaves holes in every mesh you make.

Marching tetrahedra avoids the big table. Split each cell into six tetrahedra around its main diagonal, and each tetrahedron has only four corners and sixteen sign patterns. Each is empty, full, one corner cut off (a triangle), or split down the middle (a quad, two triangles). Sixteen cases you can write by hand and check by eye. The cost is triangle count: the tetrahedra cut each cell up more finely than the surface needs, so the same fluid takes around three times the triangles of the dual methods below. 

for (var t = 0u; t < 6u; t++) {             // six tets around corner 0 → corner 6
  let mask = signs of the tet's 4 corners;   // 16 cases
  interpolate crossings along its edges;
  emit 0, 1 or 2 triangles;
}

Six tetrahedra, one diagonal, no winding

All six tetrahedra share the cell's main diagonal, corner 0 to corner 6, fanned around it like orange segments. Every face between two of them is inside the cell, and each square face of the cell is split into two triangles along a diagonal that matches the neighbouring cell's split. That's what makes the surface watertight from one cell to the next. Normals come from the field's gradient, interpolated to each crossing, instead of from the triangles, so the shading is smooth rather than faceted. The triangles come out with no particular winding, so the renderer doesn't cull back faces and the fragment shader flips any normal that faces away from the camera.

Step 5 · The dual ideaOne vertex per cell

Both marching methods put their vertices on cell edges, and handle each cell on its own. The dual methods turn that around. Put one vertex inside every cell the surface passes through, wherever best represents the surface in that cell, then join the vertices of neighbouring cells into faces. The connectivity becomes simple: every grid edge that crosses the threshold is shared by four cells, and those four cells' vertices make one quad. There's no lookup table at all, and the mesh is watertight by construction.

The simplest version is the surface net, from Sarah Gibson in 1998, made to smooth out the staircase look of segmented medical scans. It puts each cell's vertex at the average of the cell's edge crossings. Averaging pulls vertices toward the middle of their cells, so surface nets round off anything sharp. That's fine for a calm liquid and works against you on a spike.

Step 6 · The sharp edgeDual contouring

Dual contouring (Ju, Losasso, Schaefer and Warren, 2002) keeps the surface net's connectivity and places the vertex better, using one more thing the field can tell you: its gradient. At each edge crossing you know where the surface passes and which way it faces, a point and a normal, so a tangent plane. Dual contouring puts the vertex where it best fits all of the cell's tangent planes at once. Where the surface is flat, the planes agree and the answer is close to the average. Where it creases or comes to a point, the planes meet at the feature, and the vertex moves there.

The paper finds that point by minimizing a quadratic error function, which takes a small matrix decomposition per cell. I used a cheaper iteration that's common in real-time code: start at the average and walk. Each step measures the vertex's distance to every tangent plane and moves by the mean correction. Ten steps get close, and the vertex is clamped to its cell. The step size is the sharpness slider in the last demo. At zero the walk never starts and you have surface nets. At one it heads all the way to where the planes meet. 

var x = massPoint;
for (var it = 0u; it < 10u; it++) {
  var corr = vec3f(0.0);
  for (var k = 0u; k < count; k++) {
    corr += n[k] * dot(p[k] - x, n[k]);   // distance to tangent plane k
  }
  x += corr * (sharpness / f32(count));
  x = clamp(x, cellMin, cellMax);
}
Aside · Why sharpness is a slider

Dual contouring has a known weak spot. Noisy normals can put the best-fit point well outside its cell, and the mesh folds over itself. The normals here come from a field that thousands of particles re-deposit every frame, so they're never perfectly clean. Clamping to the cell stops the worst of it, and backing sharpness off from 1 trades a little tip sharpness for a calmer surface. Most production code does the same thing a different way, by mixing the average point into the error function.

The last demo has every control: χ, field strength, tension, sharpness, the surface level the extractors cut at, which extractor, and the view. The shading is the 3D twin of part one's. The fluid's own colour is almost black, and what you see is a small studio reflected in it, a softbox overhead and warm and cool strips on either side, plus one hard light for the pin highlight on every tip. Some things to try. Hover just above the pool and move slowly, and a column of fluid follows the magnet. Push χ up for a denser, more violent crown. Lower the surface level and the skin swells around the particles; raise it and the skin shrinks until it barely covers them, which is where the extractors disagree most. Then flip between surface nets and dual contouring on one tip and slide the sharpness. The crown in this sim has fairly round tips to begin with, so the difference is a matter of a voxel or so at each tip, but it's there. And switch to the particle view during a full crown to see the moments glowing brightest up the spike columns, the 3D version of part two's arrows.

EpilogueWhat the series built

Compared with the paper, this is still a sketch. The paper solves pressure implicitly, integrates the pair force instead of sampling it, sums the magnetic field over every pair instead of cutting it off at three radii, and takes seconds per frame. This version keeps the whole loop on one GPU: sort, magnetization, forces, splat, extraction and shading. The CPU writes a few hundred bytes of settings a frame and never waits on the GPU.

Over three parts, a few lessons kept coming back:

  • Particles, then a field, then geometry. The same three passes made segments in 2D and triangles in 3D, with an atomic counter and an indirect draw keeping the CPU out of the loop.
  • Spend the expensive part once a frame. The moment solve and the magnetic forces run on one substep and get reused on the rest.
  • Integers are the GPU's best atomics. Fixed point made a race-free, deterministic density volume out of an operation WGSL doesn't have.
  • Where a vertex goes matters as much as how many there are. The dual methods use a third of the triangles of marching tets and keep more of the shape.

Things I'd try next: a fast multipole method for the long-range field, so the cutoff could go; a proper QEF solve for dual contouring, regularized toward the mass point; and more particles in a deeper dish, which is what it would take for the tips to come to real points. The paper's other scenes, like ferrofluid climbing a magnetized sphere, would be good targets after that.
