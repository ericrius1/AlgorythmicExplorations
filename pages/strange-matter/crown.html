Strange Matter · Part 2

The Crown

The field over this pool is the same everywhere, so it puts no net pull on anything. The spikes still grow because every particle becomes a tiny magnet acting on its neighbors.

Scroll down to read ↓

In Part 1, a spike only rose because the bar magnet had a steep field gradient pulling oil up toward its pole. Remove that gradient by flooding the dish with a completely uniform vertical field, and Part 1's pool would sit dead flat: when ∇H = 0, an external field exerts zero net force anywhere. Yet a real dish of ferrofluid placed inside a uniform field still erupts into the classic Rosensweig crown of evenly spaced spikes. The missing ingredient is mutual magnetization. Inside a real pool, the magnetite grains line up with the applied field and start broadcasting magnetic fields of their own, so particles begin pulling and pushing directly on their neighbors.

To bring that behavior into a browser sim, this part follows Shao, Huang, and Michels' "A Current Loop Model for the Fast Simulation of Ferrofluids" (2022). Their formulation supplies the two pieces Part 1 lacked: a self-consistent way to compute every particle's magnetic moment, and a pairwise force model that pulls inward along the surface instead of blasting outer particles away the way the textbook Kelvin force does. The sim stays in a 2D cross-section for now, running 20,000 particles in the interactive figures below and 24,000 in the wide pool at the top (where moving your cursor drops an extra magnet over the crown).

Aside · Three papers from one group

This technique comes out of a trilogy of papers from Dominik Michels' group at KAUST. In 2019, Huang, Hädrich, and Michels simulated large-scale ferrofluid spikes from first principles using SPH particles and the Kelvin force. In 2020, Huang and Michels tried a completely different angle, tracking only the outer skin with a boundary element method. Their 2022 paper returned to particles and swapped in the current loop force model, making it stable enough to pair with fast, large-step incompressible fluid solvers.

Step 1 · Becoming a magnetThe moment problem

How eagerly a ferrofluid magnetizes boils down to a single material parameter, its magnetic susceptibility χ. Drop the liquid into an external field H and it develops magnetization M = χH. In particle terms, every SPH particle now carries a tiny arrow alongside its position and velocity: its magnetic moment m.

The twist is that a particle never sees the external field in isolation. It experiences the applied field plus the combined magnetic field generated by every other particle's moment, and all of those moments are unknown too. Each particle's answer depends on every neighbor around it, forming a coupled linear system with one unknown vector per particle: 

m_i = κ · B(r_i),   κ = χ / (1 + χ)      // each moment aligns with the local field…
B(r_i) = H_applied + Σ_j field(m_j)    // …which sums the fields of all other moments

Rather than treating each particle as a singular point dipole whose field blows up at zero distance, the solver smears each moment over the particle's SPH smoothing kernel to obtain a smoothed dipole. Beyond the kernel radius it behaves like an ordinary point dipole; up close, two overlapping particles feel a finite, well-behaved field with no arbitrary softening constant to hand-tune.

Above, every sixth particle renders as a tapered arrow aligned with its magnetic moment, growing longer and shifting from dim blue toward bright orange as its magnetization climbs. As the uniform field ramps up over the first second and a half, faint columns begin to glow inside the pool; move your cursor close to drop in a stronger smoothed dipole and watch the arrows beneath it bend toward the pole and flare brighter. Notice that arrows never point strictly at the cursor. Each one tracks the sum of the external field and its neighbors' dipoles, so vertically stacked particles amplify each other's field and shine hottest along the spine of every spike. Left unchecked, that mutual reinforcement would blow up to infinity; the demagnetization factor κ = χ / (1 + χ) keeps the feedback loop bounded.

The smoothed dipole, in 2D

The 2019 KAUST paper derives the smoothed dipole by solving the magnetostatic Poisson equation for a moment distributed across the SPH kernel W. At separation r, the exact field depends only on the kernel value W(r) and its average W̄(r) over the ball of radius r. Outside the kernel's support, the entire distribution looks like a point and the expression collapses to the textbook dipole formula.

That original derivation lives in 3D. Because the slice here is 2D, each particle really represents a long cylindrical rod extending into the screen, so its field is that of a line dipole falling off as 1/r² rather than 1/r³. Fortunately the algebra keeps the exact same structure: the spherical average becomes an average over a disk of radius r (a compact piecewise polynomial for the cubic spline), and the 3D self-field factor of 1/3 for a sphere becomes 1/2 for a round cylinder. In the compute shader, particle pairs sum magnetic fields out to six kernel radii, walking a 13 × 13 block of grid cells.

Step 2 · Iterative solutionOne sweep a frame

In an offline render, you solve that linear moment system all the way to convergence on every single time step. To hold 60 frames per second in a browser, this version cuts an aggressive corner: each rendered frame runs just one sweep of a dead-simple relaxation update, starting from last frame's moments as the initial guess. Every particle sums the field from its neighbors' current moments, then nudges its own moment 60% of the way toward the new target value: 

// one sweep per frame; mi is last frame's moment
var b = externalH(pi) + selfTerm(mi);
for (each neighbor j within 6h) { b += smoothedField(pi - pj, mj); }
var m = mix(mi, kappa * b, 0.6);   // under-relaxed so it can't ring

Technically the system never fully converges on any given frame, and visually you can hardly tell. A Rosensweig crown takes dozens of frames to rise and shift, whereas the moments chase the exact solution only a frame or two behind the particles. The magnetic forces strike the same bargain: both the moment sweep and the pairwise force pass run once per rendered frame on the first substep, and the remaining four substeps reuse that cached magnetic acceleration since particles barely budge in a sixtieth of a second.

Step 3 · The sign of the forceKelvin vs. current loop

Once every particle knows its magnetic moment, those moments have to become forces. Classical ferrohydrodynamics actually offers several competing force formulas that disagree about how stress is distributed inside the liquid. In 1977, J. V. Byrne proved that once you include the matching magnetic pressure jump across the free boundary, all of these formulations yield identical equilibrium shapes for a continuous fluid. The 2019 KAUST paper chose the classic Kelvin force, μ₀(M·∇)H. Their 2022 paper switched to the current loop model, which adds one bulk term to Kelvin: 

F_CL = F_Kelvin + μ₀(∇M)·M    // same continuum equilibrium, opposite sign at the surface

Once you discretize the liquid into SPH particles, that extra term makes a night-and-day difference. Across the outer skin of the fluid, magnetization drops sharply to zero, so the Kelvin force points outward and tries to peel surface particles straight off the spikes. An SPH pressure solver then has to fight continuously to drag them back in, forcing very small time steps. Adding the current loop's extra term flips the sign between aligned overlapping neighbors so they pull inward together, keeping every rising column cohesive even under larger, faster time steps.

Clicking force model swaps strictly between current loop and Kelvin while holding the moments, relaxation sweep, and every other knob fixed. Wait a couple of seconds for the initial ramp to raise a row of spikes under current loop, then flip to Kelvin: the tall columns slump almost right away, dissolving into a choppy fringe of stunted peaks that struggle to climb back up.

What the demo computes for each pair

The 2022 paper computes the exact force between two overlapping particles by integrating the smoothed force density across their overlap volume and approximating the integral with fitted polynomials. This sim takes a lighter shortcut and evaluates both force terms directly at the particle centers. The Kelvin piece takes the gradient of one smoothed dipole's field dotted with the other particle's moment, while the extra current-loop bulk piece acts inside two kernel radii and pulls aligned neighbors together. Evaluating at the centers preserves the key sign flip (aligned overlapping pairs repel under Kelvin alone and attract once the bulk term is added) while staying strictly equal and opposite for every pair so linear momentum is conserved.

Step 4 · AssemblyA crown that spaces itself

Turn the external field completely uniform and Part 1's gradient mechanism shuts off entirely. The applied field's sole job now is to magnetize the liquid. Everything that shapes the crown comes from particle-to-particle dipole interactions: aligned particles stacked head to tail attract, pulling oil upward into sharp columns, while aligned particles sitting side by side repel, shoving neighboring columns apart so they cannot merge into one wide lump. Gravity and surface tension step in to cap the height and pick the spacing.

Here the whole 2D system runs together: SPH pressure and cohesion, the single-sweep moment update, current-loop pair forces, and Part 1's marching-squares renderer. The uniform field ramps up smoothly over the first second and a half so you can watch spikes emerge straight out of a calm pool (re-pour resets the tray and replays the ramp). Below roughly χ = 1 the surface barely ripples; around the default susceptibility χ of 2 a steady crown forms, and cranking it toward 5 fractures the pool into violent lumps that flick stray drops into the air. Surface tension behaves a little counterintuitively at first: set it to zero and the spikes stay short while shedding particles off their tips, whereas dialing it up actually grows taller, cleaner cones because stronger cohesion binds each column together. You can also toggle the force model, cycle view across five stages (now including the moments arrows), or hover just above the surface to pull the spikes beneath your cursor taller.

EpilogueShortcuts and what comes next

Measured against the 2022 KAUST paper, this browser version is unapologetically scrappy. Their reference solver runs in full 3D, computes implicit incompressibility, integrates every overlapping pair force, and iterates the magnetic moments to tight convergence, spending seconds on a single frame where a browser gets sixteen milliseconds. Even so, a few core ideas survive the diet intact. When a coupled linear system evolves slowly from frame to frame, chasing it with one warm-started relaxation sweep gets surprisingly close for a fraction of the cost. Formulations that are provably equivalent in continuum equations can behave like opposites once discretized onto particles. And a uniform field with zero net pull can still carve crisp spatial structure out of a flat liquid purely through pairwise attraction and repulsion.

Pushing this 2D solver further would mean replacing the six-radius magnetic cutoff with a fast multipole method (which the paper also recommends) and testing whether a few conjugate-gradient iterations sharpen the crown noticeably over one Jacobi-style sweep. Before optimizing 2D further, though, there is a much bigger jump waiting: lifting the entire pool into three dimensions, where spikes pack across a square dish instead of a single line and marching squares has to grow up into real 3D surface extraction.
