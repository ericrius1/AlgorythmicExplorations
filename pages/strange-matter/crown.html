Strange Matter · Part 2

The Crown

The field over this pool is the same everywhere, so it doesn't pull on anything. The spikes grow because every particle is a small magnet acting on its neighbours.

Scroll down to read ↓

Part one grew one spike at a time under a moving magnet. It couldn't make the thing ferrofluid is famous for, the Rosensweig crown, where a whole surface breaks into evenly spaced spikes at once. Its particles were pushed around by the magnet but never became magnets themselves. In real ferrofluid the grains line up with the applied field and make fields of their own, and the crown comes from those grains acting on each other.

This article adds that, following Shao, Huang and Michels' "A Current Loop Model for the Fast Simulation of Ferrofluids" (2022). The paper gives me the two things I needed: a way to work out every particle's magnetic moment, and a pair force that pulls inward at the surface where the classic Kelvin force pushes outward. One of the demos below has a button that swaps between the two.

The simulation is still a 2D cross-section, 20,000 particles in the figures and 24,000 in the pool above. In the hero, moving your cursor over the pool adds one more magnet.

Aside · Three papers from one group

The method comes out of a run of papers from Dominik Michels' group at KAUST. In 2019 Huang, Hädrich and Michels simulated ferrofluid spikes from first principles with SPH particles and the Kelvin force. In 2020 Huang and Michels did it again with only the surface, using a boundary element method. The 2022 paper goes back to particles and changes the force model, which is what lets it use faster pressure solvers.

Step 1 · Becoming a magnetThe moment problem

How strongly a ferrofluid responds to a field comes down to one number, its susceptibility χ. Put it in a field H and it magnetizes to M = χH. In the simulation that means every particle carries a small arrow, its magnetic moment m, next to its position and velocity.

The catch is that the field a particle feels is the applied field plus the field of every other particle's moment, and those moments are unknown too. Each particle's answer depends on all the others. Written out, it's a linear system with one unknown vector per particle: 

m_i = κ · B(r_i),   κ = χ / (1 + χ)      // each moment follows the local field…
B(r_i) = H_applied + Σ_j field(m_j)    // …which is set by all the other moments

The field of one particle isn't the textbook point dipole, which blows up at zero distance. It's a smoothed dipole: the field of a moment spread over the particle's SPH kernel, the same smearing SPH applies to everything else. Far away it's the ordinary dipole. Up close it stays finite, so two overlapping particles feel a smooth, well-defined field and there's no softening constant to tune.

Each arrow is one particle's moment, drawn for every sixth particle so they don't pile up. Longer and more orange means stronger. Bring the cursor near and the arrows under it swing toward it and brighten. The cursor is one more smoothed dipole, a big one. The arrows don't point straight at it, though. Each one follows the sum of the applied field and its neighbours' fields, so particles stacked in a column reinforce each other and the column's arrows run brightest. That's a feedback loop: a column magnetizes itself more strongly, which pulls it together harder. The κ = χ/(1+χ) factor is what keeps it from running away.

The smoothed dipole, in 2D

The 2019 paper derives the smoothed dipole by solving the magnetostatic Poisson equation for a moment spread out as the kernel W. The answer depends on two things at distance r: the kernel value W(r) and W̄(r), the average of W over the ball of radius r. Outside the kernel's support the whole blur looks like a point and the formula turns into the exact dipole.

That derivation is for 3D. This page is a cross-section, so each particle really stands for a long column of fluid running into the screen, and the right fields are those of line dipoles, falling off as 1/r² instead of 1/r³. The 2D version has the same form. The ball average becomes an average over the disk of radius r, which for the cubic spline is a short piecewise polynomial, and the self-field factor of 1/3 for a sphere becomes 1/2 for a round column. Pairs interact out to six kernel radii, a 13 × 13 block of grid cells.

Step 2 · Iterative solutionOne sweep a frame

The paper solves the moment system to convergence, with an iterative solver, every time step. I went cheaper. Each frame does one sweep of the simplest possible update: work out the field at every particle from the current moments, then move each moment 60% of the way toward the value that field implies. Last frame's moments are the starting guess. 

// one sweep per frame; mi is last frame's moment
var b = externalH(pi) + selfTerm(mi);
for (each neighbour j within 6h) { b += smoothedField(pi - pj, mj); }
var m = mix(mi, kappa * b, 0.6);   // under-relaxed so it can't ring

This never fully converges, and it doesn't need to. The answer only changes as fast as the fluid moves, and a crown takes dozens of frames to grow, so the moments trail the exact solution by a frame or so and you can't see the difference. The same goes for the force: the moments and the magnetic forces are computed once per frame and reused across all five substeps, since particles barely move in a sixtieth of a second.

Step 3 · The sign of the forceKelvin vs. current loop

With the moments known, the next question is what force they produce. Classical ferrohydrodynamics has several force models that disagree about where force acts inside the fluid. J. V. Byrne showed in 1977 that, once each is paired with its own surface term, they predict the same jump in pressure across the surface, so a continuous fluid ends up in the same shape under any of them. The 2019 paper used the Kelvin force, μ₀(M·∇)H. The 2022 paper uses the current loop model, which is the Kelvin force plus one more term: 

F_CL = F_Kelvin + μ₀(∇M)·M    // same pressure jump, opposite sign at the surface

On particles, the choice matters. At the surface the Kelvin force points outward: the magnetization drops to zero across the boundary, and the surface particles get pushed off the fluid. A pressure solver has to keep hauling them back, which needs very small time steps. For aligned moments, the current loop's extra term pulls overlapping neighbours together, and the net force at the surface turns inward. The paper's point is that this lets it use modern incompressible solvers with much larger steps. The physics at equilibrium is the same, and the numerics are much better behaved.

The button swaps the force model and keeps everything else: the same moments, solver and settings. On current loop spikes rise and hold, because the near-field pull between aligned neighbours keeps each column together. Switch to Kelvin and give it a few seconds. The tall spikes slump, and the surface settles into a ragged fringe of short points that never grow up.

What the demo computes for each pair

The paper gets the force between two overlapping particles by integrating the smoothed force density over the overlap, and stores the result as fitted polynomials. I took a shortcut and evaluate both terms at the particle centres instead. The Kelvin term is the gradient of one smoothed dipole's field at the other particle's moment. The bulk term only acts within two kernel radii and is attractive for aligned pairs. That keeps the property that matters most: under Kelvin alone, two aligned, overlapping particles repel, and with the bulk term added they attract. Both terms are equal and opposite for each pair, so momentum is conserved.

Step 4 · AssemblyA crown that spaces itself

Now make the applied field the same strength and direction everywhere. A uniform field has no gradient, so it puts no net force on anything, and part one's whole mechanism is switched off. Spikes grow anyway. The field's only job now is to magnetize the particles. The forces come from the particles acting on each other: stacked neighbours attract, so columns gather and sharpen, and side-by-side neighbours repel, so columns keep their distance. That second part is the regular spacing part one never had. Gravity and surface tension set the scale.

EpilogueWhat's left out

Next to the paper, this page cuts a lot of corners. The paper is fully 3D. It solves pressure implicitly, integrates the pair force instead of sampling it, and solves the moments to convergence. It takes seconds per frame rather than a sixtieth of one. What carries over:

  • An unknown that depends on itself can be chased. One warm-started sweep a frame is enough when the answer moves slower than the frames.
  • Equivalent on paper isn't equivalent on particles. Two force models that agree in the continuum give opposite surface forces once the fluid is discrete.
  • A uniform field can still make structure. It has no pull of its own. The pattern comes from the pieces acting on each other.

Things I'd try next: sum the long-range field with a fast multipole method instead of cutting it off at six radii, which the paper also suggests; swap the relaxation for a few conjugate gradient steps and see how much it changes the crown; and go to 3D, where the spikes can pack in a hexagonal pattern instead of a row. Part three does that last one.
