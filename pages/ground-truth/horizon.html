Ground Truth · Part 6

The Horizon

A flight with no boundary: terrain ahead streams in a few milliseconds at a time while hills behind are discarded and forgotten.

Scroll down to read ↓

Every earlier demo in this series had walls around it: a 16-unit cutaway block, a 64-unit hillside, a 96-unit valley. Yet almost every architectural shortcut along the way was chosen for this moment. Part one kept the mountains stateless so any coordinate evaluates without a stored map, and part five tied every tree and rock to world-indexed grid integers so no tile ever needs to consult its neighbors. This final part cashes in both guarantees and drops the boundary completely.

Memory is finite, so an endless landscape has to behave like a sliding window centered on the viewer: generate the ground approaching the nose before it arrives, and free whatever drifts past the tail. Making that window run smoothly at 60 frames per second rests on three mechanisms working together: a coordinate grid of chunks tracking what exists, distance-based level of detail keeping vertex counts bounded, and a strict millisecond budget spreading builds cleanly across frames.

Overhead, the camera weaves gently toward a low sunset and never circles back. Everything visible is constructed live as it flies: a moving grid of terrain chunks surrounding the lens, instanced woods reaching three rings out, grass carpeting the closest tiles, and a single coarse backdrop mesh carrying distant ridges all the way into the horizon haze.

Step 1 · The ringChunk coordinates

Partition the world plane into square chunks 24 units wide, identified by integer grid coordinates (⌊x / 24⌋, ⌊z / 24⌋). The existence rule is a square radius around the camera's current chunk: keep every chunk within six steps in both axes, forming a 13 × 13 patch of 169 tiles. On every frame, the world manager diffs that desired set against what is already in memory. Missing tiles enter a build queue sorted closest first, while any chunk more than seven steps away is disposed, freeing its GPU buffers. Leaving a one-chunk hysteresis buffer between the load radius (6) and the eviction radius (7) stops a camera hovering near a tile border from thrashing, building and destroying the same edge rows back and forth.

Watch the moving window from above. As the camera glides forward, new chunks pop in along the leading edge (flashing white as they build) while trailing rows drop off behind, with the color key in the margin showing each resolution ring. Because the queue prioritizes tiles nearest the camera, high-detail ground right under the lens updates first while outer tiles catch up on later frames. In this top-down diagram, builds per frame caps work by tile count as a stand-in for the real engine's millisecond timer. Lower builds per frame to 1 and push flight speed up to see the queue back up and leave ragged gaps on the far edge. Evicting trailing tiles costs nothing permanently: fly back to those exact coordinates later and the hashes rebuild the identical hill, boulder, and tree leaning at the exact same angle.

Step 2 · Detail by distanceLevel of detail and skirts

A 24-unit tile five rings away shrinks to a sliver on screen, so giving it the same mesh density as the ground directly beneath the camera wastes both CPU build time and GPU vertices. Instead, mesh resolution steps down by ring distance: a 48 × 48 grid for the camera's own chunk and its eight immediate neighbors, 24 × 24 across the next two rings, and 12 × 12 out to the rim. At the default radius of six chunks, the readout shows how dramatically that pays off: roughly 80,000 live vertices across the entire window, compared with nearly half a million if every tile built at full resolution, and that footprint stays flat whether you fly one kilometer or a thousand. 

rings 0–1: 48² grid    rings 2–3: 24²    rings 4–6: 12²    // every chunk gets a skirt

Stepping mesh resolution across a shared border introduces T-junction cracks. Even though both adjacent tiles sample the exact same height function, the 48-segment edge follows an extra midpoint between every pair of 24-segment vertices, leaving thin slivers of sky peeking between the two polygons. Stitching edge indices across every possible neighbor combination works, at the cost of a tangle of special-case index buffers. A far simpler production trick is a skirt: extrude a narrow vertical curtain hanging four units straight down around all four borders of every chunk, carrying the same normals and paint as the surface edge. Whenever a crack opens between mismatched rings, your eye hits matching shaded terrain skirt instead of bright sky, and at two rings away the seam disappears completely.

Deep dive: matching normals and shadows across seams

Geometry cracks are only half of hiding tile edges; lighting mismatches can give away chunk borders just as easily. Surface normals come from elevation differences between adjacent vertices, and a vertex sitting on a tile edge has no neighbor inside its own mesh. Falling back to a one-sided difference there prints a faint lighting crease along every chunk boundary. Because the height function is stateless, the fix is almost free: evaluate one extra hidden row of heights outside all four borders so edge vertices run the exact same two-sided central difference as their neighbors.

Baked sun shadows run into the same boundary issue at longer range. Each chunk marches 40 world units toward the sun from every vertex to test whether distant peaks block the light, and most of those rays cross into neighboring chunks. Evaluating full multi-octave warped fBm along every march step outside the tile would cost more than building the terrain itself, so off-chunk heights come from a shared coarse height lattice sampled at 2-unit spacing and cached in 32-unit tiles. Adjacent chunks read identical distant peaks from that cache, so ridge shadows line up cleanly from tile to tile.

Step 3 · The budgetBuilding a few milliseconds at a time

Hitting 60 frames per second gives you 16.7 milliseconds for everything: simulation, mesh generation, and rendering. On a typical laptop, building one full-resolution chunk with its shadow bake takes roughly 4 ms, scattering its trees takes well under 1 ms, and planting a quarter-chunk of grass takes around 2 ms. Every time the camera crosses a chunk boundary, dozens of tiles simultaneously need creating or upgrading; rebuilding all of them on the spot would freeze the animation for tens of milliseconds at regular intervals. Instead, the manager works down the distance-sorted queue until 6 ms have elapsed, then yields the rest of the frame and resumes on the next tick: 

wanted.sort((a, b) => a.ring - b.ring);              // nearest first
const t0 = performance.now();
for (const w of wanted) {
  if (!needsWork(w)) continue;
  if (performance.now() - t0 > budgetMs) { queued++; continue; }   // next frame
  buildOneStep(w);                                   // terrain, then trees, then grass
}

Checking the clock before each unit of work means a frame can overshoot by at most one quick build step. Breaking each chunk into incremental stages (terrain mesh first, then trees and boulders, then grass in four quarter-chunk slices) guarantees no single step can stall a frame.

Foliage follows the same distance discipline. Trees scatter out to three rings around the camera using the lighter canopy meshes from part five (and an even sparser branch/leaf mesh beyond ring one), since distant silhouettes matter while inner twigs don't. Grass only populates the innermost rings at 24,000 candidates per chunk, where blades still span multiple pixels on screen; as tiles recede, their grass and trees are disposed until you circle back.

One last layer carries the land past the outermost chunk ring all the way to the horizon. Sitting beneath and around the chunk grid is a single 1,600-unit square backdrop mesh sampled every 10 units (25,921 vertices), with a square cutout in the center where the detailed chunks live. Whenever the camera drifts 24 units from the backdrop's center, a replacement builds quietly in slices of a couple of milliseconds per frame (evaluating a few rows of heights at a time, running its shadow march, assembling the mesh) and swaps in cleanly once ready. Nudging the backdrop slightly below the live chunk height where their edges overlap ensures detailed ground always wins the depth buffer.

Here the entire system runs live. Drag horizontally on the canvas to turn and vertically to climb or descend, heading anywhere you want across the world. Beneath the controls, the readout tracks live chunks, pending queue length, last build duration, total terrain vertices, and active tree count. Click freeze streaming while continuing to fly to peel back the stage illusion: you outrun the detailed 13 × 13 ring, glide out over the smooth coarse backdrop, and eventually sail past its rim into open sky. Unfreeze and watch the priority queue stitch the world back together around you, closest tiles first. Toggle LOD tint to color-code chunks as they stream in (cyan for full resolution, amber for half, red for quarter, violet for the far backdrop) and watch the queue jump and smoothly drain every time you cross a tile border.

Aside · Why this world isn't eroded

You might notice one ingredient missing from the flight above: part two's hydraulic erosion. Erosion has memory: every raindrop alters the slope seen by the next one, and a watershed depends on every ridge upstream, so you cannot carve an independent 24-unit tile without mismatches at its edges, nor fit a hundred thousand droplets inside a 6 ms streaming budget. To keep the world boundless and zero-download in a browser, this flight falls back on part one's shaping curve to suggest broad valleys while leaving particle rain for the bounded patches. Commercial open-world games usually solve that tension by eroding a huge finite map offline and shipping gigabytes of precomputed height tiles.

EpiloguePutting the pieces together

Looking back across all six parts of Ground Truth, the whole landscape grew out of one recurring engineering trade-off: deciding what can stay pure math and what genuinely needs state. Hashed gradient noise summed across rotated octaves, ridged, warped, and shaped yielded reproducible mountains at any coordinate. Where a scene could afford bounded memory, simulated raindrops carved connected drainage networks and handed over a soil ledger for free. Grass and trees pushed static placement and self-shadowing to build time so the GPU could animate hundreds of thousands of vertices in wind without asking the CPU for help. Climate noise and world-indexed jittered grids let biomes populate every hillside without consulting neighbors, and chunk rings, skirts, and a millisecond budget turned those stateless rules into a flight that never meets an edge.

Plenty of tempting upgrades still sit right around the corner: offloading height evaluation and chunk assembly into Web Workers or GPU compute shaders so the main thread never feels a build tick, tinting the coarse far backdrop with biomes and canopy shadows so the handoff past the outer chunk ring vanishes completely, and experimenting with stateless approximations of hydraulic erosion (such as derivative-damped noise or analytic gully ridges) that capture weathered drainage without breaking infinite tiling.
