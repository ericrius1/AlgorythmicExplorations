Ground Truth · Part 6

The Horizon

A flight that never reaches an edge. The land ahead is built a few milliseconds at a time, and the land behind is thrown away.

Scroll down to read ↓

Every scene in this series so far has had edges: a 64-unit eroded patch, a 96-unit valley, with plain land around them out to the haze. Meanwhile most of the design decisions were made with this part in mind. Part one made the ground a function with no stored map. Part five made every tree depend only on integers it can compute where it stands. This part uses those choices to build a world that doesn't run out.

You can't store an endless world, but if everything visible is a function of coordinates you don't have to. You only need to build the part around the camera, in time, and throw away what falls behind. The machine that does it has three parts: chunks decide what to build, level of detail decides how finely, and a budget decides when.

The flight at the top heads toward a low sun with a slow weave and never turns back. Everything in the view is generated as it goes: nearly 200 chunks of terrain around the camera, trees out to three chunks away, grass in the nearest few, and one coarse mesh beyond the last chunk that carries the land out to the haze.

Step 1 · The ringChunk coordinates

Cut the plane into square chunks 24 units on a side, and name each one by the integer pair (⌊x / 24⌋, ⌊z / 24⌋). The rule for existence: a chunk exists if it's within six chunks of the camera's chunk in both directions, a 13 × 13 square. Every frame, the manager compares the rule with what it has. Chunks that are wanted but missing go into a queue, nearest first. Chunks more than seven away are thrown out, geometry and all. The one-chunk gap between "wanted" and "thrown out" keeps a camera that wobbles on a boundary from building and destroying the same chunk over and over.

The white flashes are builds. They run ahead of the camera because the queue is sorted by distance and only a few builds happen per frame. In this diagram the limit is a fixed count you can set; the real world uses milliseconds, which comes up in step 3. Drop the builds per frame to one and raise the speed, and the queue falls behind and the far edge gets ragged. At the trailing edge, chunks disappear without losing anything, because every chunk can be rebuilt identically. Come back to the same coordinates and the hash gives you the same hill, the same boulder and the same tree leaning the same way. That's what all the determinism in the earlier parts was for.

Step 2 · Detail by distanceLevel of detail and skirts

A chunk five rings out covers a small patch of the screen, and building it at full resolution is wasted work. So resolution follows distance: a 48 × 48 grid for the camera's chunk and its neighbours, 24 × 24 for the next two rings, and 12 × 12 beyond that. The diagram's readout does the sum. At the default radius the live chunks hold about 85,000 vertices, against about half a million if every chunk were full resolution, and the total stays the same however far you fly. 

rings 0–1: 48² grid    rings 2–3: 24²    rings 4–6: 12²    // every chunk gets a skirt

Mixing resolutions creates seams. Where a 48-segment chunk meets a 24-segment one, both edges sample the same height function, but the coarse edge draws straight lines between its samples and the fine edge doesn't, so thin cracks of sky open between them. The exact fix is to stitch the edge vertices of every pair of resolutions together, which is a lot of bookkeeping. The cheap fix that many engines use is a skirt: every chunk gets a curtain of geometry hanging four units straight down from its border. A crack then shows skirt, which is shaded like the ground above it, and the cracks only happen far enough away that nobody can tell.

Deep dive: matching normals and shadows across seams

Cracks are the visible seam problem. Lighting is the quieter one. Normals come from differences between neighbouring heights, and a border vertex has no neighbour on one side. Use a one-sided difference there and every chunk border shows as a faint crease in the shading. Because the height function is pure, the fix is cheap: sample one extra row of heights beyond each edge, which the chunk never draws, and every border normal becomes the same two-sided difference its neighbour computes.

Shadows have the same problem at a larger scale. Each chunk bakes its own sun shadows by marching 40 units toward the sun from every vertex, and most of that march leaves the chunk. Evaluating the full height function out there would cost more than building the chunk, so heights beyond the edge come from a coarse lattice at 2-unit spacing, computed once per 32-unit tile and cached. Neighbouring chunks read the same lattice, so their shadows line up.

Step 3 · The budgetBuilding a few milliseconds at a time

At 60 frames a second there are 16.7 ms per frame for everything. In my measurements a full-resolution chunk with its shadow bake takes about 4 ms, a tree scatter well under one, and a quarter-chunk of grass about 2. Crossing a chunk boundary changes what several dozen chunks want, and building all of that at once would make the flight stutter every few seconds. So each frame, the manager walks the distance-sorted queue and builds until 6 ms have passed, then stops and leaves the rest for the next frame: 

wanted.sort((a, b) => a.ring - b.ring);              // nearest first
const t0 = performance.now();
for (const w of wanted) {
  if (!needsWork(w)) continue;
  if (performance.now() - t0 > budgetMs) { queued++; continue; }   // next frame
  buildOneStep(w);                                   // terrain, then trees, then grass
}

The check happens before each build, so a frame can overrun by one build, at most a few milliseconds. Each chunk does one step per turn (terrain first, then its trees and rocks, then a quarter of its grass) so no single step is large.

The same thrift decides what each chunk gets. Trees exist out to three rings, where a forest's silhouette still matters, using the lighter tree meshes from part five, and lighter still past the first ring. Grass only grows in the nearest rings, 24,000 candidate blades per chunk, because from a few chunks away a blade is less than a pixel. When a chunk falls far enough behind, its grass and trees are dropped and the rules will bring them back if you return.

Past the last ring there's one more piece. A 1,600-unit square of coarse terrain at 10-unit spacing, 25,921 vertices, lies under and around the chunks with a hole where they are. When the camera has moved 24 units from its centre, a new one is built in slices of a couple of milliseconds per frame (heights a few rows at a time, then the shadow march, then one assembly) and swapped in when it's done. It sits a little lower than the chunks where they overlap, so the detailed ground always wins.

This is the whole machine. Drag sideways to turn and up or down to climb or descend, and fly in any direction you like. The readout shows the live chunk count, the queue, the time the last build took, the terrain vertex total and the number of trees. Press freeze streaming and keep flying to see what's being hidden: you leave the detailed chunks behind and fly on over the bare, coarse backdrop, and if you keep going long enough you reach its edge. Resume and the queue rebuilds the world around you, nearest first. LOD tint repaints each chunk as it's rebuilt: cyan for full resolution, amber for half, red for quarter, and violet for the backdrop. Each time you cross a chunk boundary the queue count jumps and then drains over the next few frames.

Aside · Why this world isn't eroded

Part two's erosion is the one technique missing here. It's a history: every droplet changes the ground for the next one, and a river depends on everything upstream. It can't be split into chunks that agree at their borders, and a hundred thousand droplets don't fit in a 6 ms budget. So the endless world relies on the shaping curve from part one to suggest old terrain, and simulated rain stays in the bounded scenes. Large games often run erosion offline and ship the result as data. In a browser with no download, I chose the function.

EpilogueThe whole series

That's the end of Ground Truth. Here's what the pieces turned out to be:

  • A height function. Hashed gradient noise, summed over octaves, ridged and warped, gives mountains at any coordinate.
  • A history, where it's affordable. Simulated raindrops carve connected valleys and leave a soil map behind.
  • Decisions made once. Grass and trees are planted and baked into buffers, and only the wind moves them.
  • Rules instead of lists. Climate, biome and a world-indexed grid decide what grows where without remembering anything.
  • A budget. Build what's near first, build it finely only where it's close, and never spend more of a frame than you planned to.

Things I'd try next: move the height function and the chunk builds to the GPU or a worker so the main thread never pays for them; let the backdrop carry a coarse version of the biome tint and tree shadows so the transition at the last ring is invisible; and find a function-of-position approximation of erosion that's good enough to use everywhere.
