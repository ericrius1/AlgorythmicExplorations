Ground Truth · Part 2

The Rain

Ninety thousand simulated raindrops carve the terrain from part one while you watch. Each one only knows the slope under it.

Scroll down to read ↓

The mountains from part one have the right silhouette, but they've never been rained on. Noise has detail at every scale and no history. Real valleys are a record of water. They join each other downstream in branching networks, because water that finds a channel deepens it and the next drop finds it more easily. Valleys in fBm wander about and dead-end.

This part adds the history with hydraulic erosion. The method doesn't simulate the water as a fluid. Each raindrop is a particle that rolls over the heightfield and can do two things: pick up dirt and put it down. Run enough of them and the valleys connect.

The view at the top started out as plain part-one terrain. It gets 3,000 droplets a second until 90,000 have fallen, which takes about thirty seconds, and the camera keeps circling afterwards. The blue is where the droplets' paths collected: ponds in the basins, and the odd stream between them.

Step 1 · One dropletA particle that rolls downhill

A droplet is five numbers: a position on the map, a direction, a speed, an amount of water, and a load of sediment. Each step of its life looks like this: 

dir    = dir · inertia − gradient · (1 − inertia)   // downhill, with some momentum
move one cell along dir
speed  = √(speed² + drop · gravity)                 // falling makes it faster
water *= (1 − evaporation)                          // and it slowly dries up

Inertia keeps the droplet from turning sharply. With inertia 0 it follows the gradient exactly, turns at every cell, and gets caught in every small dent in the noise. A little momentum lets it cut smooth curves and coast across small flats. The default is 0.06. The height and gradient under the droplet come from bilinear interpolation of the four surrounding grid cells, so it moves over a continuous surface even though only a grid is stored.

Rain falls on this 220 × 220 map three droplets a frame, and each droplet's path is drawn as it runs downhill. Nobody routes them. Each droplet only sees the slope under it, and still the paths gather into the same lines, because downhill paths from a wide area all funnel into the same low ground. When a droplet goes six steps without reaching a new low point it's sloshing around in a pit, so the drawing stops and leaves a faint blue dot. The dots pile up where ponds would form. Push inertia up and the paths swing wide and overshoot the bends.

Step 2 · SedimentCarrying capacity

Tracing paths only finds the drainage. To change the ground, each droplet carries sediment, and it has a carrying capacity: 

capacity = max(drop, 0.01) · speed · water · C
if sediment > capacity: deposit some of the surplus   // slow, flat, or drying up
else:                   erode toward capacity          // fast and hungry

Most of the behaviour comes from that product. On a steep face the droplet is fast and drops a long way each step, so its capacity is high and it strips material. On the valley floor the slope goes away, the capacity goes with it, and the droplet sheds its load. That's why mountain streams run over bare rock while valley floors fill up with soft, flat sediment.

A droplet that would have to climb is at the bottom of a pit. It drops sediment there, up to the height of the climb, and over many droplets that fills basins with flat floors. In the view at the top those floors are the green flats around the ponds.

Two limits keep the digging stable. A droplet never erodes more than the height it's about to descend, or it digs a hole below its own path and the terrain fills up with needle-thin pits. And it never digs at a single point. The material comes from a soft brush, 21 cells within a radius of 2.5, weighted by 1 − distance / radius, so channels come out shaped like valleys instead of one cell wide.

There's a third limit near the edges of the map. Droplets that reach the edge leave and take their sediment with them, so a hole dug near the edge is never refilled, and every droplet that falls into it digs it deeper. An earlier version of this code let the brush reach the border, and after 100,000 droplets on a 256-cell grid one cell had been dug more than 19,000 units deep. Now nothing is eroded within the brush radius plus two cells of the edge. Deposits are still allowed there, since they can only return material that was dug up somewhere else.

This is a 16-unit block of terrain, 256 cells on a side. The storm starts on its own and gets 2.5 ms of every frame until 200,000 droplets have fallen. The readout shows how many droplets a second that works out to on your machine. Flip before / after to compare with the raw terrain: the ridgelines survive almost untouched (droplets start slow and empty up there), the slopes grow gullies that join on the way down, and the basins get flat floors. Sediment paint shows where the material went, darker where ground was cut away and pale where it was laid down. The blue is the flow record, the cells the most water passed through.

Aside · Earlier erosion models

Eroding fractal terrain is nearly as old as fractal terrain. Musgrave, Kolb and Mace's 1989 paper ran both hydraulic erosion and thermal weathering over noise. Thermal weathering uses no water at all: material slides downhill wherever a slope is steeper than some angle of repose, which rounds everything off. The droplet model on this page, with its inertia, capacity, and brush, follows Hans Beyer's 2015 thesis. Grid-based methods instead simulate shallow water flowing over every cell at once, and they map well onto GPUs.

Why it looks random, and what it costs

The only randomness is where each droplet lands, which comes from the same hash as the mountains. From then on a droplet is fully deterministic. The aggregate still looks varied because the terrain itself scrambles things: two droplets that land a fraction of a cell apart can end up in different watersheds. It's also reproducible. The same seed gives the same storm and the same valleys.

A droplet costs a few microseconds in JavaScript, and most of that is the brush, 21 cells touched on every eroding step. Running droplets in parallel on a GPU is harder than it sounds, because two droplets can write the same cell at once. Implementations that do it accept those collisions, since erosion is a statistical process and the errors mostly average out.

Step 3 · What comes outValleys, floors, and a soil map

The storm leaves more behind than new heights. The valleys now join downstream instead of wandering, which is one of the strongest cues that terrain has been weathered. The basins have flat floors, the only ground in the scene flat enough to grow a meadow on. The middle slopes are steeper and the crests are mostly untouched, so the relief is sharper than before.

And the simulation keeps a ledger. Every cell records its net change, negative where material was cut away and positive where it was laid down. That's a soil map for free: deep sediment in the valley floors, bare rock in the gullies. Part three plants grass from it.

EpilogueThe cost of history

Erosion breaks something part one was careful about. The height function was pure: ask for any coordinate and get the same answer, with nothing stored. The rain is a process. Each droplet changes the ground the next one sees, so the eroded terrain only exists as a stored grid that has to be simulated in order. That's fine for a patch of terrain, and a problem for a world with no edges. Part six comes back to it.

  • Local rules, global shapes. No droplet knows where the river is. The river is where the droplets agree.
  • One product does most of the geology. Slope × speed × water decides where ground is cut and where it's laid down.
  • Check the boundaries. The edge of the map broke the simulation in a way the interior never would.
  • Keep the ledger. Recording what changed costs one extra array and gives the next part its soil map.

Things I'd try next: run the droplets in a compute shader and accept the write collisions; add a thermal pass so the steepest faces settle into scree slopes; and erode at a coarse resolution, then add fine noise back on top, which gets most of the look for a fraction of the droplets.
