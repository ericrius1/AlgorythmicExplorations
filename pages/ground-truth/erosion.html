Ground Truth · Part 2

The Rain

Ninety thousand simulated raindrops carving valleys across the mountains from part one while the camera orbits overhead.

Scroll down to read ↓

Look closely at the noise mountains from part one and something quietly gives them away: their valleys go nowhere. Real mountain basins link together into branching drainage trees because water that cuts a slight groove draws the next storm into the same groove, deepening it over thousands of years. Fractal noise has roughness at every scale, yet it has no memory of water ever having moved across it, so its troughs meander and dead-end in isolated bowls.

To give the terrain that missing geological history, we run particle-based hydraulic erosion. Rather than solving shallow-water fluid equations across the entire map at once, we release individual raindrops onto the heightfield, let each one roll downhill while picking up and dropping dirt, and repeat the process tens of thousands of times until separate dents merge into connected channels.

The landscape at the top of the page begins as raw part-one terrain. Over roughly thirty seconds, 3,000 droplets a second rain down on it until 90,000 have traced their courses, carving gullies on the flanks and pooling into blue ponds and occasional connecting streams in the lower basins.

Step 1 · One dropletA particle that rolls downhill

Every droplet carries five pieces of state: a 2D position on the grid, a heading, a speed, a remaining volume of water, and the amount of sediment suspended inside it. At each step along its path it updates like this: 

dir    = dir · inertia − gradient · (1 − inertia)   // downhill, with some momentum
move one cell along dir
speed  = √(speed² + drop · gravity)                 // falling makes it faster
water *= (1 − evaporation)                          // and it slowly dries up

Inertia smooths out the steering. At zero inertia a droplet snaps straight down the local gradient at every step, zig-zagging sharply and snagging in the tiniest dimple of the noise. Giving it a small amount of momentum (0.06 by default) lets it glide through shallow bumps and sweep wide bends like water moving with real mass. Elevation and slope under the droplet come from bilinear interpolation of the four surrounding grid cells, so paths move smoothly across a continuous surface even though heights live on a discrete array.

Three droplets per frame land across this 220 × 220 relief map, leaving trails that shift from cyan (carrying little dirt) to amber (heavy with sediment). Each particle inspects only the slope directly beneath it, yet paths arriving from wide stretches of hillside converge into the same narrow drainage lines simply because gravity funnels them toward the lowest ground. Once a droplet goes six steps without hitting a new elevation minimum, it is sloshing back and forth inside a basin, so we stop tracing its trail and stamp a faint blue dot where water would collect into a pond. Push inertia up and watch droplets overshoot turns and climb partway up opposing slopes; hit downpour (500) to drop a burst at once.

Step 2 · SedimentCarrying capacity

Tracing downhill paths maps out the drainage, while actually reshaping the hills comes down to a single quantity called carrying capacity: how much dirt a droplet can hold in suspension right now. 

capacity = max(drop, 0.01) · speed · water · C
if sediment > capacity: deposit some of the surplus   // slow, flat, or drying up
else:                   erode toward capacity          // fast and hungry

Almost all of the recognizable geology falls out of that multiplication. Tumbling down a steep mountainside, the droplet moves fast and drops sharply on each step, so its carrying capacity jumps and it scours material off the slope. Reaching a flat basin floor kills both speed and vertical drop, collapsing capacity so the water dumps its load. Over time, upper slopes strip down to exposed rock while valley floors level out with washed-in silt.

Whenever a droplet's momentum carries it uphill out of a depression, it drops enough sediment behind it to fill the hole up to the height of the climb. Repeated over thousands of droplets, jagged pits gradually pave themselves over into flat green valley floors like the ones rimming the ponds in the hero view.

A couple of guardrails keep the math from tearing the heightfield apart. First, a droplet is never allowed to dig deeper in one step than the elevation it is about to descend; otherwise it excavates a pothole beneath its own feet and peppers the map with needle-thin spikes. Second, instead of subtracting material from a single grid point, erosion pulls from a soft circular brush (at radius 2.5, covering 21 cells weighted by 1 − distance / radius). That wide brush carves U-shaped gullies rather than one-pixel trenches.

A third guardrail came from a nasty edge bug. Droplets that roll off the border of the map disappear along with whatever dirt they were carrying, which means any gouge carved near the perimeter never gets refilled and traps subsequent droplets in a runaway feedback loop. An earlier version of this code let the brush touch the map edge, and after 100,000 droplets on a 256 × 256 grid one unlucky border cell had bored more than 19,000 units straight into the floor. Blocking erosion within the brush radius plus two cells of the boundary stopped the abyss completely, while still allowing deposition near the rim since deposits can only return soil lifted further uphill.

Aside · Earlier erosion models

Weathering fractal mountains is almost as old as generating them. Musgrave, Kolb, and Mace paired hydraulic erosion with thermal weathering in their landmark 1989 paper. Thermal weathering skips water altogether and slumps material downhill whenever a slope exceeds its angle of repose, softening sharp cliffs into talus aprons. The particle-droplet formulation here, with momentum, carrying capacity, and a radial brush, follows Hans Beyer's 2015 thesis. Grid-based alternatives track shallow-water depth and velocity across every cell simultaneously, making them a natural fit for GPU compute shaders.

Why it looks random, and what it costs

The sole random input is each droplet's landing spot, picked deterministically from the same integer hash that built the mountains. Once a droplet touches down, its trajectory is completely deterministic. Variety comes from chaotic sensitivity to the terrain itself: two drops landing a fraction of a cell apart near a saddle can peel off into completely different watersheds. Because the landing sequence is hashed from the seed, replaying the same seed reproduces every gully identically.

Each droplet takes only a few microseconds in JavaScript, most of it spent updating the 21 cells under the erosion brush. Parallelizing droplet erosion on a GPU is trickier than it looks because simultaneous threads collide when two droplets try to carve the same cell at once; GPU implementations usually live with those race conditions since erosion is statistical and occasional missed writes wash out over hundreds of thousands of drops.

Step 3 · What comes outValleys, floors, and a soil map

After a storm passes, the elevation grid reads like weathered rock rather than math. Tributaries join cleanly downhill instead of meandering into dead ends, mid-slopes sharpen beneath nearly untouched crests, and enclosed depressions flatten into level floors where a meadow could plausibly take root.

Just as useful is the side ledger maintained alongside the heights. Every cell keeps a running total of its net elevation change, negative where water stripped away rock and positive where silt settled out. Keeping that one extra array hands us a free soil map: bare scoured stone in steep chutes, deep alluvial soil across the valley bottoms. Part three will read straight from that ledger to decide where grass grows.

EpilogueThe cost of history

For all the realism it buys, hydraulic erosion breaks a guarantee part one worked hard to keep. A pure height function answers any (x, z) query in isolation with zero stored state. Erosion is inherently stateful and sequential: every droplet modifies the ground that later droplets roll across, so the carved result only exists inside a bounded grid simulated from start to finish. That works beautifully for a standalone patch of mountains, and becomes a real headache once we want an endless world with no edges. We will confront that trade-off directly in part six.

If I were extending this simulation next, I would port the droplets to a GPU compute shader and accept the occasional write collision for an order-of-magnitude speed boost, add a thermal weathering pass so sheer gully walls slump into realistic scree aprons, and test eroding at a coarse grid resolution before layering fine procedural detail back on top to get broad drainage networks at a fraction of the droplet count.
