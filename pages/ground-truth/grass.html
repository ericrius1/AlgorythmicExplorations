Ground Truth · Part 3

The Grass

Seventy-two thousand blades planted once on the CPU and animated across a sunlit valley entirely on the GPU.

Scroll down to read ↓

Drawing one mountain mesh is straightforward. Drawing 72,000 individual grass blades on top of it runs straight into a rendering wall if you treat each blade like its own 3D object or loop over them on the CPU every frame. Submit tens of thousands of separate draw calls and the driver chokes before the GPU even starts shading; update positions in JavaScript sixty times a second and your frame budget evaporates.

The way around both bottlenecks is to split static choices from live motion. At planting time, the CPU reads the eroded terrain from part two (built on part one's mountains), decides every blade's position, size, color, facing, and stiffness once, and packs the whole meadow into a single vertex buffer. From that moment on, the CPU never touches a blade again; all the swaying and shimmering happens in a handful of shader lines on the GPU.

At the top of the page, the camera crouches down inside a flat valley floor left by an erosion storm, peering toward a low sun. The 72,000 blades around it were filtered from 340,000 candidate spots sampled densely around the lens and thinning out across the meadow.

Step 1 · The bladeSeven vertices

Each blade starts as a narrow, tapered ribbon anchored at the ground and free to flex at the tip. Because its root is stiff while the upper half arches over, the sideways bend should start almost vertical and accelerate smoothly toward the top. The simplest function with that behavior is a parabola: for a vertex sitting at fraction t along the blade's length, horizontal displacement scales with t²: 

bendX = lean · t² · height                     // t = 0 at the root, 1 at the tip
y     = t · height − lean² · t² · 0.3 · height    // a bent blade gets shorter

That second term is easy to overlook until you animate a meadow without it. If a blade's tip slides sideways while staying at the same altitude, the blade visibly stretches like rubber whenever a gust hits. Lowering y in proportion to lean² keeps arc length nearly constant as the stalk bows over.

Above is a single blade widened 3× so you can see its mesh anatomy. White dots mark the vertices handed to the GPU, and the dashed line running up the middle is the smooth quadratic centerline they approximate. Each vertical segment needs a pair of edge vertices plus one shared point at the tip, giving 2n + 1 vertices and 2n − 1 triangles. Drag segments down to 1 and the blade turns into a rigid wedge; raise it toward 8 and the outline hugs the dashed parabola closely. Three segments (seven vertices, five triangles) hit the sweet spot: the upper segments bend cleanly where the eye notices silhouettes, while keeping vertex counts low enough for tens of thousands of blades. The live sway uses the exact wind formula from step 3, and the counter in the upper left tracks how far the tip drops as the stalk bends.

Step 2 · PlantingReading the soil map

Remember the sediment ledger kept at the end of part two? Positive values mark level valley floors where raindrops dumped washed-down silt, while negative values mark steep chutes scoured back to bare rock. Rather than hand-painting a density mask, the planter walks through hashed candidate coordinates across the terrain and computes each spot's odds of germinating straight from height, slope, and that erosion ledger: 

if (h01 < minH01 || h01 > maxH01 || slope > maxSlope) return 0;
let p = 0.55 + Math.min(0.45, Math.max(0, delta) * boost);   // silt raises the odds
if (delta < 0) p *= Math.max(0.15, 1 + delta * 8);            // cut ground lowers them
p *= 1 - smooth01((h01 - maxH01 * 0.7) / (maxH01 * 0.3));     // thinner toward the grass line
p *= 1 - smooth01((slope - maxSlope * 0.6) / (maxSlope * 0.4)); // and on steeper ground

A quick hash comparison against p decides whether a blade takes root. Surviving candidates draw the rest of their identity from the same hash: height, width, static lean, compass yaw, and a color blending deep green, young shoots, and drier straw tones near the upper grass line. Each blade also stores two wind parameters (a random phase offset so neighbors don't oscillate in unison, and a flexibility factor so tall stalks whip further than stubby tufts) plus the terrain's baked sunlight value at its root. All of those traits bake straight into vertex attributes and never need updating again.

Orbiting around the patch makes the soil ledger obvious: steep eroded walls and exposed ridgelines stay bald, while the flat silt-filled hollows grow thick with blades. Roughly one candidate in five survives. Raising candidates toward 400k adds tens of thousands of blades while barely nudging frame time, since every blade lives inside one static draw call. Underneath the geometry, the terrain material darkens toward a rich meadow green wherever planting odds are high, so distant patches still read as turf long after individual stalks shrink below a pixel.

Aside · One buffer, no instancing

GPU instancing is often the first reach for foliage: upload one canonical blade and stamp it out N times with a per-instance matrix. Here, baking every blade directly into one merged geometry buffer at planting time is simpler and actually uses less overhead. Instancing shines when each copy shares hundreds of vertices or moves independently on the CPU. A seven-vertex static blade carries less data than a 16-float transform matrix, so writing the final world positions and colors straight into one flat buffer stays compact and fast.

Step 3 · WindA gust field in two sines

Solving real aerodynamics over a grassy hillside would take a cluster of GPUs. Fortunately, human perception checks much simpler cues: gusts should arrive in coherent patches, those patches should sweep across the slope, and neighboring stalks should lean together while distant patches move out of phase. Two traveling sine waves at different spatial scales and speeds hit all three cues in five lines of vertex shader math: 

w1   = k·(x + 0.7z) + 1.7t + φ
w2   = k·(2.9x − 2.1z) + 3.4t + 1.7φ
gust = 0.7·sin(w1) + 0.3·sin(w2)
sway = (0.5·gust + 0.62) · strength · t² · flex      // never negative
move the vertex by (0.82·sway, −0.45·sway², 0.57·sway)

Because the same t² curve from step 1 multiplies the gust, roots stay planted in the dirt while tips whip across the wind direction. The per-blade phase φ breaks up artificial synchrony, and the −0.45·sway² drop prevents blades from stretching during heavy gusts. Adding 0.62 inside the parentheses keeps sway strictly positive, so stalks bow downwind and flutter around a steady lean instead of oscillating symmetrically back and forth like windshield wipers. In the meadow figure above, gust size sets k and displays 1/k, roughly the width of a single gust front in world units.

Aside · The first computer grass

The 1984 short film The Adventures of André & Wally B., produced by the Lucasfilm graphics team that soon became Pixar, rendered its grass and forest foliage with particle systems. Bill Reeves and Ricki Blau documented the approach at SIGGRAPH 1985: every stalk and twig was drawn as a particle trail, and lighting was approximated statistically because evaluating exact shading across millions of tiny strands was out of reach.

Step 4 · LightShading the whole field

Give a thin grass ribbon its true geometric normal (pointing straight out of its flat face) and lighting tens of thousands of them turns the hillside into noisy glitter, with individual blades flashing bright and dark as the camera moves. Instead, each blade stores a normal pointing almost straight up along the hillside, nudged only slightly toward the blade's facing direction for subtle variation. To the sun and sky, the meadow shades like a continuous velvety carpet whose silhouette breaks into crisp individual tips, matching how real turf reads from a few meters away. Those normals also skip rotating during wind gusts: once stalks are swaying, the eye tracks motion through changing silhouettes and ignores that the diffuse normals held still.

Three extra lighting terms sell the golden-hour look. First, direct sunlight tapers off toward the root, where blades are already tinted darker to mimic deep turf shadow. Second, looking back toward the sun adds a warm yellow-green translucency term weighted toward the upper half of each stalk, making backlit grass glow along its rim. Third, the fragment shader receives the vertex's live gust value and gently brightens bent blades, turning invisible wind waves into bright ripples rolling across the valley.

EpilogueScaling up to foliage

Packing 72,000 blades into a single draw call came down to deciding everything static once at planting time, handing wind motion over to a position-and-time vertex shader, and letting the erosion ledger dictate where meadows take root so grass naturally hugs silt-rich hollows and avoids rocky chutes. Shading the field's average surface normal instead of individual ribbon faces kept tens of thousands of tiny triangles visually calm at any distance.

To push a grass renderer further, the next steps would be generating blades on the GPU inside a hashed grid sliding under the camera, dropping faraway stalks down to single three-vertex triangles as a distance LOD, and writing footsteps or rolling objects into a small trail texture so grass parts around anything moving through it. Before worrying about endless fields, though, the valley needs taller company.
