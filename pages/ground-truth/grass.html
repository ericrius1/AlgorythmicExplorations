Ground Truth · Part 3

The Grass

A meadow of 72,000 blades, planted once on the CPU and moved by wind entirely on the GPU.

Scroll down to read ↓

So far this series has made ground: part one raised mountains out of noise, and part two rained on them until they had drainage and soil. This part puts something on that ground, and the first thing that grows there comes in large numbers.

Tens of thousands of anything changes the rules. One mesh per blade and the renderer drowns in draw calls. Any per-blade work on the CPU each frame and the frame budget is gone. So every decision about a blade gets made once, when it's planted: where it stands, how tall it is, its colour, how it moves in the wind. After that the CPU never touches it again. Everything that changes over time happens in a few lines of shader code.

The view at the top is a camera standing in a valley floor from a part-two storm, looking toward a low sun. The 72,000 blades around it were planted from 340,000 candidate spots, thickest near the camera and thinning with distance.

Step 1 · The bladeSeven vertices

A blade of grass is a tapered ribbon with a curve. The curve matters more than the vertex count. A blade is held at the root and free at the tip, so it bends very little near the ground and more and more toward the top. The cheapest curve with that shape is a parabola, so a vertex at fraction t of the way up leans sideways in proportion to t²: 

bendX = lean · t² · height                     // t = 0 at the root, 1 at the tip
y     = t · height − lean² · t² · 0.3 · height    // a bent blade gets shorter

The second line is easy to forget. If the tip moves sideways without dropping, the blade stretches like rubber as it bends. Taking a little height off in proportion to lean² keeps its length roughly constant.

The dots are the vertices the GPU gets, and the dashed line is the ideal curve they're approximating. Each segment is a pair of vertices, plus one at the tip, so three segments is seven vertices and five triangles. Slide segments down to one and the blade becomes a stiff triangle. Slide it up and the outline hugs the curve. I went with three: the bend shows where your eye checks it, at the tip, and nobody looks closely at the base. The wind in this figure uses the same formula as the shader in step 3, and the label at the top shows the tip dropping as the blade bends.

Step 2 · PlantingReading the soil map

Part two's storm left a ledger: how much material each cell lost or gained. The places where sediment piled up are the flat, wet valley floors where real meadows grow, so the planter uses that ledger. It walks through candidate points (hashed positions, like everything else in the series) and gives each one odds of taking root: 

if (h01 < minH01 || h01 > maxH01 || slope > maxSlope) return 0;
let p = 0.55 + Math.min(0.45, Math.max(0, delta) * boost);   // silt raises the odds
if (delta < 0) p *= Math.max(0.15, 1 + delta * 8);            // cut ground lowers them
p *= 1 - smooth01((h01 - maxH01 * 0.7) / (maxH01 * 0.3));     // thinner toward the grass line
p *= 1 - smooth01((slope - maxSlope * 0.6) / (maxSlope * 0.4)); // and on steeper ground

Then a hash of the candidate's index decides whether it beats those odds. Nobody paints a grass map. The storm already did.

Each blade that takes root rolls the rest of its character from the same hash: height, width, lean, facing, and colour (a random mix of greens and straw, getting drier toward the grass line). Two more numbers are for the wind: a phase, so the meadow doesn't move in lockstep, and a flexibility, since tall grass whips around while short tufts barely move. One more is for the light: how much sun reaches that spot, read from the terrain's baked shadows. All of it is written into vertex attributes and never changes again.

Look at where the grass isn't. The steep gully walls and the ridgelines stay bare, and the valley floors, where part two's droplets dropped their loads, are thick with it. About one candidate in five survives. Push the candidate count up and the frame rate barely changes, because a blade costs the CPU nothing after it's planted. The whole meadow is one draw call. The ground under it gets a darker green wherever the odds are high, so where the blades thin out with distance the field still reads as grass.

Aside · One buffer, no instancing

The usual tool for this is GPU instancing: upload one blade and stamp it out N times. This meadow does something simpler. Every blade is written into one big static buffer at planting time, already in its final position with its colour baked in. Instancing pays off when copies share a lot of geometry or move independently. A seven-vertex blade has little to share, and none of them ever move on the CPU, so the merged buffer costs nothing extra and lets every vertex carry its own data.

Step 3 · WindA gust field in two sines

Simulating real airflow over a meadow would be absurd. What the eye actually checks is much weaker: gusts should come in patches, the patches should travel, and neighbouring blades should agree while distant ones don't. Two travelling sine waves at different scales and speeds do that well enough: 

w1   = k·(x + 0.7z) + 1.7t + φ
w2   = k·(2.9x − 2.1z) + 3.4t + 1.7φ
gust = 0.7·sin(w1) + 0.3·sin(w2)
sway = (0.5·gust + 0.62) · strength · t² · flex      // never negative
move the vertex by (0.82·sway, −0.45·sway², 0.57·sway)

This runs per vertex, every frame, on the GPU. The same t² from step 1 scales the wind, so blades bend from the root the same way. The per-blade phase φ breaks up the lockstep, and the −0.45·sway² term shortens the arc as it bends, like the static lean. The 0.62 offset keeps the sway positive, so grass leans downwind and flutters around that lean instead of swinging back and forth like a metronome. The gust size slider sets k and shows it as 1/k, roughly the width of one gust in world units.

Aside · The first computer grass

The 1984 short The Adventures of André & Wally B., made by the Lucasfilm graphics group that later became Pixar, had a forest and grass drawn with particle systems. Bill Reeves and Ricki Blau described how in a 1985 SIGGRAPH paper: each blade and branch was a particle trail, and the shading was approximated statistically, since there were far too many to light one by one.

Step 4 · LightLighting a field instead of a blade

A real blade's normal points sideways, out of the flat of the ribbon. Light tens of thousands of ribbons that way and the meadow glitters, every blade flashing as it turns. So the normals here describe the field instead: each vertex claims a normal pointing almost straight up, tilted a little toward the blade's facing for variety. The lighting then sees a soft green surface that happens to have blade-shaped edges, which is what a meadow looks like from a few metres away. The normals don't follow the wind either. On moving grass you read the motion from the outline, and nobody notices that the shading stayed put.

Three more terms make it look like grass at golden hour. Sunlight mostly reaches the top of each blade, so the sun term fades toward the root, where the colour is already darkened. When you look toward the sun, light comes through the blades, strongest near the tips, which makes backlit grass glow yellow-green. And the gust value from the wind shader brightens the blades it's bending, so gusts show up as waves of light rolling across the field.

EpilogueWhat carries over

  • Decide once. Every per-blade choice is made at planting time and baked into vertices.
  • Move in the shader. The wind is a function of position and time, so it costs the CPU nothing and scales with the GPU.
  • Light the crowd, not the individual. Normals that describe the field hide the fact that it's made of flat ribbons.
  • Use what the last step left behind. The erosion ledger became a planting map without any extra work.

Things I'd try next: generate the blades on the GPU from a hashed grid around the camera, so the meadow follows you instead of being built once; add a distance-based level of detail that drops to one triangle per blade far away; and bend the grass away from objects moving through it with a small trail texture.
