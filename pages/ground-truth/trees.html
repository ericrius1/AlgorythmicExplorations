Ground Truth · Part 4

The Trees

Six trees grown from three rules about competing for space, planted a hundred times over on the hills from part two.

Scroll down to read ↓

Everything in this series so far has been a function of position. Ask the ground for its height at a point and you get an answer. Ask the soil whether grass grows there and you get another. Trees don't work that way. A branch exists because of the branch it grew from, and it goes where it goes because other branches already took the space next to it. A tree is a history, so it has to be grown.

The classic way to grow plants in a computer is the L-system, a grammar that rewrites each branch into smaller branches, generation after generation. It makes convincing botany, but a grammar works from the inside out and knows nothing about its surroundings. Real trees are shaped from the outside in, by where the light and open air are. So I went with an algorithm that models that directly, called space colonization.

The grove at the top is six trees grown that way, each a few hundred branch segments, instanced 104 times across a 64-unit patch of eroded hills with hashed sizes and rotations. The low sun throws each crown's shadow onto the grass, and the wind moves the trees and the blades together.

Step 1 · CompetitionBranches chase open space

Scatter a few hundred attraction points through the volume where leaves could live. They stand for light and open air. Plant a short trunk. Then repeat three rules until nothing changes: 

1. every attractor picks the nearest branch node within its influence radius
2. every node that was picked grows one step toward the average direction of its attractors
3. every attractor that a branch has come within the kill radius of is removed

That's the whole algorithm. Nothing in it says "branch here". A fork appears when one node's attractors pull in directions far enough apart that its new shoots end up closest to different groups, and from then on they grow apart. The crown's shape is just the shape of the attractor cloud. Branches don't collide, because an attractor near an existing branch has already been removed.

The cyan dots are the 240 attractors, in two hashed lobes. They go dark as branches reach them, and the faint circles show how far each growing tip can see. One round of the rules runs every five frames, and the readout says how many rounds it took and how many attractors were never reached. A finished tree stays up for four seconds and then grows again.

Influence is how far a tip can see. Small values mean each branch only feels the points right next to it, and growth stalls in gaps: lower it and watch attractors get stranded. Large values let distant points pull, so the tree reaches across the crown in long sweeping limbs. Kill radius is how close a branch has to get before a point counts as used up. Small values pack the crown with twigs; large values leave it airy, with fewer, straighter branches. Those two numbers and the shape of the attractor cloud account for most of the difference between one kind of tree and another.

Aside · The algorithm came from leaves

Adam Runions and colleagues first used this idea in 2005 to model the veins of a growing leaf, where the attractors are sources of the plant hormone auxin and the veins grow toward them. Two years later Runions, Lane and Prusinkiewicz turned it on whole trees, with open space playing the part of the hormone.

Step 2 · ThicknessThe pipe model

Colonization produces a skeleton of nodes with no widths. To give it a trunk, I use the pipe model: think of a tree as a bundle of pipes carrying water from the roots to the leaves, so a branch has to be thick enough to carry everything that grows beyond it. Leonardo da Vinci wrote down the version most people know: where a branch splits, the children's cross-sections add up to the parent's. As a recurrence, walking from the tips back to the root: 

radius(tip)    = 0.016
radius(node)ᴱ  = Σ radius(child)ᴱ      // E = 2 keeps area; I use 2.4

With E = 2, area is conserved at every fork. Murray's law for blood vessels says E = 3, and measured trees mostly land in between. I use 2.4 because it gave trunks that looked right to me next to the crowns. The nodes are stored in growth order, so every child comes after its parent, and one backward pass over the list computes every radius.

Step 3 · The meshBark rings and leaf cards

The 3D version is the same three rules with a third coordinate. The attractors fill two or three hashed ellipsoid lobes above a 1.5-unit trunk (one ellipsoid grows suspiciously tidy trees), every shoot gets a slight upward bias, and growth stops when no attractor is left or after 160 rounds. The default tree finishes in 24.

Each branch segment becomes a six-sided tube, a ring of vertices around the node joined to the ring around its parent, with normals pointing straight out from the branch so the bark shades smoothly. The tubes are written in growth order and not indexed, so drawing only the first part of the buffer shows the tree as it was a few rounds in. That's how the studio below animates growth without regrowing anything.

Leaves are diamond-shaped cards, two triangles each, 0.17 to 0.27 units long, scattered along the thin twigs: ten around each branch tip and five to eight around other twigs. Lighting a few thousand separately facing cards one by one would make the crown glitter, so their normals are mostly the direction outward from the crown's centre, with 30% of each card's own facing mixed in. The crown then shades like a rounded mass with a leafy edge.

Every slider change grows a new skeleton and replays the growth: the trunk shoots up over two and a half seconds and the leaves come out once it's mostly done. The default tree has 333 segments and 2,116 leaf cards, and the readout updates with each regrow. Push attractors up and the crown fills in; raise influence and the limbs get longer and straighter.

The light inside a crown would need ray tracing to do properly, so I estimate it once per vertex from where the point sits. Points on the side facing the sun and near the crown's surface get most of the sunlight; points deep inside or on the far side get less sun and less sky. When you look toward the sun, the leaves the sun reaches also let some light through, tinted yellow-green, the same way the grass in part three glows when it's backlit. That estimate is stored with the geometry, so it costs nothing per frame.

Step 4 · A groveSix trees, a hundred places

The grove at the top grows six trees with different seeds and 300 to 450 attractors, which takes a few milliseconds each. Each one is uploaded once as an instanced mesh for its bark and one for its leaves, and every copy is just a position, a rotation, and a scale between 0.8 and 1.3. You identify a tree mostly by its silhouette, and the same skeleton turned 90 degrees and scaled up on different ground reads as a different tree. The whole grove is twelve draw calls.

The spots come from hashed candidate points on the eroded terrain, kept on gentle, lowish ground and thinned by a slow sine pattern so the trees gather into groups. A candidate is rejected if an earlier tree already stands within 2.6 units. That's dart throwing, the simplest way to keep trees from crowding, and it's fine for one bounded patch. The next part explains why it won't work for a world that has to be built in pieces.

Two things make the trees sit in the scene instead of floating over it. Each crown stamps a soft shadow into the terrain's baked sunlight: an ellipse pushed away from the sun by the crown's height and stretched along the sun's direction, the way a low sun throws a long shadow. The grass reads the same baked light when it's planted, so blades in a tree's shadow are darker too. And the wind sways each tree in proportion to the square of the height, like the grass blades, with a phase taken from the instance number so a hundred copies of the same tree don't move in lockstep. The leaves add a faster flutter on top.

Deep dive: the cost of growing a tree

The inner loop is "every attractor finds its nearest node", which is attractors × nodes distance checks per round. For the default tree that's at most 320 attractors against a node count that grows to 333 over 24 rounds, a couple of million checks in all, and it finishes in a few milliseconds in plain JavaScript. The 2007 paper uses a spatial grid to find neighbours, which pays off with thousands of attractors. With a few hundred, the brute-force loop is simpler and fast enough to regrow on every slider move.

EpilogueWhat carries over

  • Model the competition. Three rules about space produce branching, crown shape and spacing without describing any of them.
  • One physical rule for proportion. The pipe model sets every radius in one backward pass.
  • Store things in the order they grew. The growth animation is a draw range, nothing more.
  • Uniqueness comes from placement. Six meshes, rotated, scaled and shadowed, make a grove.

Things I'd try next: shape the attractor cloud by species (a tall narrow cone for conifers, a wide flat one for an acacia); let the terrain's own shadows thin out the attractors on the shady side so trees lean toward the light; and bake a few views of each tree into impostor cards for the far distance, which part six could use.
