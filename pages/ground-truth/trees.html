Ground Truth · Part 4

The Trees

Six skeletons grown by competing for open space, then instanced over a hundred times across the eroded hillsides from part two.

Scroll down to read ↓

Up to now, almost every question the series asked about the landscape could be answered directly from coordinates: plug in (x, z) to get elevation, check the soil ledger to see if grass takes root. A branching tree resists that kind of local lookup. Every limb exists because an older limb reached that neighborhood first, and it swerves away from its siblings because they already claimed the light next door. To get believable silhouettes, you have to grow the skeleton step by step.

For decades, the standard way to synthesize plants in computer graphics was the L-system, a formal grammar that rewrites stem symbols into smaller twigs over successive generations. L-systems produce striking botanical symmetry, though pure grammars expand blindly from the inside out without sensing nearby branches or open sky. Real canopies are sculpted from the outside in by competition for light and room, so this part uses an algorithm built around that competition directly: space colonization.

The hillside grove overhead is built from just six grown skeletons, each a few hundred branch segments long, instanced 104 times across a 64-unit eroded patch with hashed rotations and scales. Every canopy stamps a soft shadow onto the ground and grass beneath it, and the whole forest bends together with the wind.

Step 1 · CompetitionBranches chase open space

Begin by scattering a cloud of attraction points throughout the volume where you want foliage to live, representing pockets of sunlight and open air. Plant a short starter trunk beneath the cloud, then loop through three simple rules until growth stops: 

1. every attractor picks the nearest branch node within its influence radius
2. every node that was picked grows one step toward the average direction of its attractors
3. every attractor that a branch has come within the kill radius of is removed

Nowhere in those three steps is there an explicit branching probability or fork angle. Splits emerge on their own whenever attractors tug a node from two wide directions so its newly added tips fall closest to separate clusters and pull apart on subsequent rounds. Limbs avoid colliding automatically as well, because once a branch claims a pocket of attractors and deletes them, nearby shoots have no reason to grow back into that occupied space.

Watch the rules play out in 2D above. The 240 cyan dots are attractors arranged into two hashed crown lobes; they dim once a tip comes within their kill radius, and the faint blue rings outline how far each active growing tip can sense. One iteration runs every five frames, pausing four seconds on the finished skeleton before growing again.

Two radii control almost the entire character of the branching. Influence sets each tip's search range: dial it down and tips only notice nearby dots, stalling out across gaps and stranding attractors; open it wide and distant clusters exert a strong pull, stretching the tree into long sweeping boughs. Kill radius sets how close a shoot must get to consume an attractor. Tight kill radii pack the crown with fine, gnarled twigs, while generous ones finish faster with an open, airy scaffold. Pairing those two parameters with different attractor cloud volumes accounts for much of the variety across tree species.

Aside · The algorithm came from leaves

Adam Runions and his co-authors originally introduced space colonization in 2005 to simulate vein networks inside growing leaves, where attractors represented microscopic sources of the growth hormone auxin. Two years later, Runions, Lane, and Prusinkiewicz scaled the same mechanism up to full 3D trees, casting open air in the role of auxin.

Step 2 · ThicknessThe pipe model

Space colonization leaves behind a wire skeleton of nodes with no thickness. To give each limb realistic taper, the generator borrows the botanical pipe model: picture a tree as a bundle of narrow straws hauling water from soil to foliage, so every parent limb must be wide enough to carry the combined flow of all the branches sprouting past it. Leonardo da Vinci noted an early version in his notebooks, observing that the cross-sectional area of a trunk roughly equals the summed cross-sections of its offshoots at every fork. Walking backward from twig tips down to the root gives a clean recurrence: 

radius(tip)    = 0.016
radius(node)ᴱ  = Σ radius(child)ᴱ      // E = 2 keeps area; 2.4 here

Setting the exponent E = 2 preserves exact cross-sectional area at every split. Murray's law for branching blood vessels predicts E = 3, and field measurements of real timber usually land between 2 and 3. Using 2.4 gave trunks sturdy enough to balance these crowns visually. Because nodes are appended to the array in growth order, every child sits after its parent, so a single reverse pass over the array assigns every radius in the tree.

Step 3 · The meshBark rings and leaf cards

Moving to 3D adds a third coordinate to the exact same loop. Attractors scatter through two or three overlapping hashed ellipsoid lobes perched above a 1.5-unit trunk (a single clean ellipsoid grows suspiciously manicured trees), each shoot gets a gentle upward phototropism bias, and iteration stops once attractors are spent or at 160 rounds. The default tree completes in 24 rounds.

Every parent-child edge is wrapped in a six-sided bark cylinder whose ring vertices share outward-pointing normals for smooth shading. Because all bark tubes go into an unindexed vertex buffer strictly in growth order, animating the tree growing from sapling to mature crown takes zero extra simulation: the renderer simply sweeps WebGPU's draw range forward through the existing vertex array.

Foliage consists of diamond-shaped leaf cards (two triangles each, 0.17 to 0.27 units across) clustered around slender twigs: ten per branch tip and five to eight along thin sub-branches. Shading thousands of tiny randomly angled diamonds using their literal flat face normals creates harsh sparkling highlights, so each leaf card borrows 70% of its normal pointing radially outward from the canopy's center and keeps only 30% of its own plane normal. That blend lets the crown shade as a cohesive sunlit volume while preserving a crisp, leafy silhouette.

The studio opens on the default tree (333 bark segments and 2,116 leaf cards, grown in 24 rounds). Hit replay growth, click new tree, or move any slider to watch the skeleton shoot up over two and a half seconds and unfurl its canopy once the outer twigs arrive. Cranking up attractors packs the crown denser, raising influence pulls out longer, straighter boughs, and tightening kill radius increases fine twig branching.

True light bounce inside a dense leafy canopy would call for path tracing, so the builder approximates self-shadowing once per vertex when building the mesh. Vertices on the sunward exterior of the crown receive full sunlight and open sky, while leaves tucked deep inside or on the shaded backside drop into cool ambient shade. When you orbit around to look back toward the sun, sunlit leaves also pick up a yellow-green backlit glow similar to part three's grass shader. Storing those values in vertex attributes makes canopy shading virtually free every frame.

Step 4 · A groveSix trees, a hundred places

For the hero hillside, the scene grows six distinct trees with varying seeds and 300 to 450 attractors (taking only a few milliseconds apiece), upload each variant as one instanced bark mesh and one instanced leaf mesh, and stamp out 104 instances with hashed yaws and scales between 0.8 and 1.3. Because human vision mostly reads trees by overall silhouette, turning the same skeleton 90 degrees, scaling it slightly, and planting it on a different slope makes repetitions vanish. All 104 trees render in twelve draw calls.

Tree positions come from hashed candidate points filtered for low, gentle terrain and modulated by a slow sine wave so woods cluster into natural groves with open clearings between them. Any candidate within 2.6 units of an already accepted tree is discarded. That greedy dart-throwing loop works cleanly on a single bounded hillside, though as part five shows, it breaks down the moment a world has to be generated piece by piece.

Two subtle touches anchor the grove into the landscape. First, every tree projects an elongated soft ellipse into the terrain's baked sunlight map, offset away from the sun in proportion to canopy height so low golden-hour rays throw long shadows; because the grass planter reads that exact sunlight grid, turf tucked beneath a canopy darkens automatically. Second, the vertex shader sways each tree in proportion to height squared, offset by its instance index so copies never lean in lockstep, while leaf cards add a quick high-frequency flutter on top.

Deep dive: the cost of growing a tree

The heaviest step is matching every living attractor to its nearest branch node, requiring attractors × nodes distance checks per round. With 320 attractors and a skeleton reaching 333 nodes over 24 rounds, that adds up to around two million simple arithmetic checks, finishing in a few milliseconds in plain JavaScript. Runions's 2007 paper accelerates neighbor lookup with a spatial hash grid, which pays off once you push into thousands of attractors; for a few hundred, the flat loop stays short and fast enough to regrow interactively on every slider tweak.

EpilogueFrom saplings to woods

Modeling competition for open space produced organic branching, realistic crown silhouettes, and collision-free limbs out of three lines of logic, while a single reverse pass of the pipe model handled trunk and branch proportions. Keeping vertex buffers ordered by birth round turned growth animation into a free draw-range slider, and instancing six varied skeletons with baked ground shadows turned them into a full grove for twelve draw calls.

Natural next steps: sculpt species-specific attractor clouds (narrow cones for spruces, flat umbrellas for acacias), thin out attractors inside terrain shadows so hillside trees naturally lean toward open sky, and render distant trees to a handful of billboard impostor cards for far-off ridges. Right now, though, the trees and meadows are still arranged by hand per scene; part five replaces that manual choreography with climate rules.
