Ground Truth · Part 1

The Heightfield

A whole range of peaks compressed into one pure function of (x, z), generated and shaded live in the browser.

Series overview · Ground Truth Ground Truth grows an entire living procedural landscape from pure mathematics and a single integer seed—no satellite heightmaps, no hand-sculpted meshes. Across six essays, the series shapes mountain ranges from layered gradient noise here, carves river valleys with simulated rainfall in Part 2, populates slopes with wind-swept grass in Part 3 and space-colonized trees in Part 4, sorts forests, meadows, and bare scree into biomes in Part 5, and streams chunks around a flying camera toward an endless horizon in Part 6.

Satellite elevation maps of a single mountain range take hundreds of megabytes of storage. Everything drifting past at the top of this page comes from a few dozen lines of math and one integer seed. Hand that function any (x, z) coordinate and it hands back an elevation, deterministically, with zero map data stored in memory. When this page opened, the browser sampled it across roughly 229,000 vertices, baked the sunlight, and set a slow camera circling on a nine-minute lap.

Step 1 · Smooth randomnessGradient noise

If you assign every point on a grid an independent random height, you get static rather than ground. Real terrain is spatially correlated: knowing the elevation under your boots tells you a lot about the elevation three steps away. What terrain needs is randomness with a smooth transition between samples and a controllable feature size.

Ken Perlin's gradient noise delivers both by hiding its randomness on the integer lattice and storing a random slope (a gradient vector) at each grid corner instead of a random altitude. At every integer corner the value is zero, leaving the corner tilted along its assigned slope. For a sample point anywhere inside a grid cell, each of the four surrounding corners projects its slope plane out to that point, and the four values are blended with a smooth interpolation curve: 

fade(t)      = 6t⁵ − 15t⁴ + 10t³          // flat 1st and 2nd derivative at 0 and 1
corner(c, p) = grad(hash(c)) · (p − c)     // that corner's slope, extended to p
noise(p)     = the four corners blended by fade(fx) and fade(fy)

That fifth-degree fade polynomial comes from Perlin's 2002 revision. His original 1985 noise used the cubic 3t² − 2t³, whose slope reaches zero smoothly at the cell borders while its curvature jumps sharply there. On a flat texture you rarely notice, yet once you compute surface normals and shine a low sun across the terrain, those curvature jumps catch the light as faint grid lines. The quintic curve flattens both the first and second derivatives at 0 and 1, erasing the lattice from the shading.

The hash function turns integer grid coordinates and a seed into a pseudo-random index with a few integer multiplies and xor-shifts borrowed from the MurmurHash3 finalizer, selecting one of 256 evenly spaced gradient directions. Because no lookup table of random floats sits in memory, asking for the elevation at (1402, 88) always returns the exact same answer. That guarantee pays off much later: part six throws chunks away as they pass behind the camera and rebuilds them if you turn around, which only holds together if every peak comes back identical.

Aside · Noise and the movies

Perlin invented gradient noise in 1983 after working on Disney's Tron at MAGI, frustrated by how sterile and machine-tooled early computer graphics looked. He published the technique at SIGGRAPH 1985 in "An Image Synthesizer", and in 1997 the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences awarded him a Technical Achievement Award for it.

Step 2 · The fractal sumOctaves

A single pass of gradient noise looks like soft rolling dough. Real mountains carry structure across orders of magnitude at once, from ridgelines stretching kilometers across a horizon down to ankle-high gullies. To reproduce that multi-scale roughness, the generator evaluates the noise repeatedly at finer and finer frequencies while shrinking its amplitude each time, then sum the layers together. Borrowing musical terminology, each layer is an octave, and the whole stack is fractional Brownian motion, or fBm.

Below, each octave sits in its own lane drawn at true scale, with its frequency multiplier (freq) and weight (amp) down the left edge and the combined mountain profile shaded underneath. Two sliders steer the texture of the sum. Lacunarity sets the frequency jump between lanes; at 2, each layer oscillates twice as fast as the previous one. Persistence scales each lane's height. At 0.5, high octaves roughen the silhouette while leaving the big peaks intact; drag it up toward 0.85 and the finest ripples grow nearly as tall as the base hills, turning the skyline into jagged rubble.

In 2D, stacking those layers takes a ten-line loop:

for (let o = 0; o < octaves; o++) {
  let n = perlin2(x, y, seed + o * 131);   // a different seed per octave
  sum += n * amp;
  norm += amp;
  amp *= gain;                             // persistence
  const nx = x * ROT_C - y * ROT_S;        // turn the grid 0.5 rad…
  y = (x * ROT_S + y * ROT_C) * lac;       // …and make it finer
  x = nx * lac;
}
return sum / norm;                         // roughly −1 to 1
Why each octave gets turned

Every octave samples the same integer lattice at a different scale. When lacunarity is an exact power of two, grid lines from every layer intersect at the origin and reinforce one another along the x and z axes, leaving subtle horizontal and vertical banding once you stack five or six octaves. Rotating the sampling coordinates by 0.5 radians (around 29°) on each pass ensures no two layers share an axis alignment, for the price of four multiplications, while bumping the seed per octave stops layers from echoing one another.

Aside · Coastlines and mountains

Benoit Mandelbrot championed this statistical view of natural shapes. His 1967 paper "How Long Is the Coast of Britain?" showed that natural boundaries keep revealing self-similar detail as your measuring stick shrinks, so their measured length diverges. He and John Van Ness formalized fractional Brownian motion in 1968, and The Fractal Geometry of Nature (1982) famously rendered synthetic mountain ranges with it. Even the word lacunarity is his, coined from the Latin for a gap and later adopted by graphics programmers for the frequency ratio between octaves.

Step 3 · Ridges and warpsBreaking the symmetry

Move that loop into two dimensions and you immediately get a recognizable heightmap, though two telltale artifacts still give away its mathematical origin. First, plain fBm is vertically symmetric: flip a peak upside down and it has the exact same round profile as a valley, whereas geological ranges tend to pair crisp, narrow crests with broader basins. Second, plain noise is isotropic, producing lumpy mounds with no sense of geological grain or flow.

Ridging breaks the vertical symmetry inside the octave loop. Taking 1 - 2 * Math.abs(n) folds each octave along its zero crossings, turning gentle sign changes into sharp upward creases while keeping valleys rounded. Blending between plain and folded noise dials the effect anywhere from soft hills to alpine spines: 

if (ridge > 0) n = lerp(n, 1 - 2 * Math.abs(n), ridge);

Directional sweep comes from bending the coordinate space itself with domain warping. Before sampling the main terrain fBm, the function evaluates two low-octave noise fields and uses them to nudge x and z: 

qx = fbm(x, z)                               // 3 octaves, its own seed
qz = fbm(x, z)                               // 3 octaves, another seed
h  = fbm(x + 4·warp·qx,  z + 4·warp·qz)      // the real terrain, read off-center

Because those two offset fields only use three octaves, neighboring points shift in almost the same direction. Instead of scrambling the terrain, the warp stretches slopes and ridges into long, sinuous sweeps reminiscent of folded strata. At the default warp of 0.9, a sample can slide up to about 3.6 world units sideways on hills roughly ten units across.

The relief map above shades higher elevations brighter and lights slopes from the upper left. Raising ridge pinches the bright crests into crisp spines while opening up the dark hollows between them; pushing warp shears the entire range into curving arcs. Every adjustment recomputes the height function across all 63,000 pixels on the CPU, with the timing printed underneath.

Aside · Where these came from

Domain warping goes all the way back to Perlin's 1985 marble shader, which added turbulence to the phase of a sine wave. Ridged multifractal terrain was popularized by Ken Musgrave in Texturing and Modeling: A Procedural Approach (1994), and Inigo Quilez's essays later turned warping fBm by another fBm into a go-to recipe for rock formations, coastlines, and clouds.

Step 4 · Standing it upFrom function to mesh

Turning the 2D field into a 3D surface starts with a regular grid of triangles whose y coordinates come from h(x, z). One extra shaping step sits right at the end of the height function: raw fBm hovers around zero with as much area above the midline as below, so it gets remapped into [0, 1] and raise it to the power 1.6. That power curve flattens out the lower third into broad basin floors while pinching the peaks upward. In the real world, valley floors grow wide and flat because washed-down sediment fills them in; part two will simulate that erosion directly, and this curve approximates the silhouette until then.

Surface normals come straight from neighboring grid samples via central differences, avoiding any extra calls into the noise function: 

normal(i, j) = normalize( (H[i−1, j] − H[i+1, j]) / 2Δ,  1,  (H[i, j−1] − H[i, j+1]) / 2Δ )

When building a mesh patch, the generator evaluates one extra margin row of heights just outside all four borders. That way edge vertices compute the exact same two-sided difference as interior ones. On an isolated square it barely matters, yet in part six it guarantees that two adjacent chunks compute identical normals along their shared seam, preventing shading creases across tile boundaries.

Step 5 · Paint and lightMaking it read as land

Even with accurate normals, an untextured gray heightfield reads like molded plastic. To make it read as earth and stone without loading image textures, the fragment shader blends four base materials from two numbers already sitting on every pixel: normalized elevation h01 and steepness 1 − normal.y (which runs from 0 on level ground to 1 on a vertical face): 

col  = mix(grass, scree, smoothstep(0.4, 0.7, h01));      // high ground goes stony
col  = mix(col, rock, smoothstep(0.25, 0.5, slope));      // steep ground loses its soil
snow = smoothstep(0.6, 0.72, h01) * (1 - smoothstep(0.3, 0.55, slope));
col  = mix(col, snowColor, snow);                         // snow, but not on cliffs

Requiring both altitude and gentle slope for snow lets white caps cling to saddles and ridgelines while shedding off sheer rock faces, instead of painting a flat bathtub ring at a single elevation. Perturbing each threshold slightly with noise breaks up any remaining contour-line look.

Because the sun stays fixed in these scenes, the heavier lighting can be baked once per vertex during mesh construction. From every vertex, the builder marches across the height grid toward the sun and checks how close intervening ridges come to blocking the ray, yielding soft terrain self-shadows without maintaining a real-time shadow map. A second quick pass compares each vertex against the average height of its neighborhood at two radii, darkening tucked-away gullies as cheap ambient occlusion. Finally, atmospheric haze pulls its color from the procedural sky directly behind each pixel, so distant ridges dissolve cleanly into the horizon glow.

Here the height function stands up as a 16-unit square of 201 × 201 vertices and 80,000 triangles, rebuilt and relit whenever you release a slider. Alongside octaves, ridge, and warp, a height slider scales the vertical relief. Clicking wireframe strips away the surface paint to show the uniform triangle grid underneath, and reroll picks a fresh seed while preserving your parameter choices.

The flyover at the top of the page runs the exact same function scaled way up: peak height 15 instead of 3.2, seven octaves, and three nested square rings of mesh centered on the camera. The inner ring places a vertex every quarter unit, while the outer ring stretches 540 units out with samples spaced 4.5 units apart. EpilogueWhere this leaves the terrain

One stateless function can summon reproducible mountains at any coordinate, stack octaves for multi-scale roughness, break fractal symmetry with ridging, domain warping, and a shaping curve, and bake shadows and occlusion once when the vertices are built. Everything downstream in this series leans on that contract: a mountain range that takes a few kilobytes of code and comes back bit-for-bit the same whenever you ask for it.

Pushing this stage further would mean moving height evaluation into a GPU compute shader to build meshes off the main thread, computing analytic noise derivatives instead of finite-difference normals, and trying Quilez's derivative-damped fBm, which quiets high-frequency octaves on steep slopes and fakes weathered rock surprisingly well without running a drop of water. First, though, the next part finds out what happens when real simulated rain gets a turn at these peaks.
