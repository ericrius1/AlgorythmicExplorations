Ground Truth · Part 1

The Heightfield

A landscape built from one function that returns a height for any point. The mountains above are generated and drawn live in your browser.

Scroll down to read ↓

The mountains at the top of this page come from one function. You give it an x and a z, and it gives back a height. There's no stored map behind it. The same inputs always produce the same answer, so any piece of the landscape can be rebuilt at any time from the function and a seed. The view above is about 229,000 vertices, built when the page loads, and the camera takes about nine minutes to fly one lap.

This series builds a whole landscape on top of that function. This part makes the function. Part two runs simulated rain over it until the valleys connect. Parts three and four add grass and trees, part five decides what grows where, and part six streams the world in around a moving camera so it never runs out.

Step 1 · Smooth randomnessGradient noise

Random numbers on their own make bad ground. Give every point an independent random height and you get static, because real elevation is correlated: the height here tells you a lot about the height a step away. Terrain needs randomness that changes smoothly and has a typical feature size.

Ken Perlin's gradient noise does this. It puts randomness only at the integer grid points, and what it puts there is a random slope (a gradient) instead of a random height. The noise is zero at every grid point and leaves it at that point's slope. In between, each surrounding corner extends its own slope to the sample point, and the four answers are blended with a smooth curve: 

fade(t)      = 6t⁵ − 15t⁴ + 10t³          // flat 1st and 2nd derivative at 0 and 1
corner(c, p) = grad(hash(c)) · (p − c)     // that corner's slope, extended to p
noise(p)     = the four corners blended by fade(fx) and fade(fy)

That fade curve is from Perlin's 2002 revision. His original 1985 noise used 3t² − 2t³, which matches slopes across grid lines but has a jump in curvature there, and once the noise is lit as a surface the jump shows up as faint grid lines. The quintic is flat in both the first and second derivative where it meets the grid, so the lighting can't find the seams.

The hash turns a grid point and a seed into a number that looks random. Mine is a few integer multiplies and xor-shifts borrowed from the finalizer of MurmurHash3, and the result picks one of 256 evenly spaced gradient directions. There's no table of random numbers anywhere. The mountain at (1402, 88) looks random, and it comes out the same every time anyone asks for it. The whole series depends on that. Part six streams terrain in and out of memory, which only works if a piece of ground comes back identical when you return to it.

Aside · Noise and the movies

Perlin came up with his noise in 1983, after working on Disney's Tron at the effects company MAGI, because he was tired of how clean and machine-made computer images looked. He published it at SIGGRAPH in 1985 in "An Image Synthesizer", and in 1997 the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences gave him a Technical Achievement Award for it.

Step 2 · The fractal sumOctaves

One layer of noise gives smooth rolling swells and nothing else. Real ground has detail at every scale at once, from ridgelines kilometres long down to gullies a metre wide. The usual fix is to add the noise to itself at higher frequencies and smaller amplitudes, over and over. Each layer is called an octave, and the sum is called fractional Brownian motion, or fBm.

The figure draws each octave in its own lane at its real size, labelled with its frequency and amplitude, and their sum underneath as a profile. Two numbers set the character. Lacunarity is the frequency step between octaves. At 2, each octave is twice as fine as the one before, which is where the musical name comes from. Persistence is the amplitude step. At 0.5 each octave is half as tall as the last, and the fine ones add texture without changing the shape. Push it up toward 0.85 and the fine octaves are nearly as tall as the coarse ones, and the profile turns to rubble.

In code it's a short loop:

for (let o = 0; o < octaves; o++) {
  let n = perlin2(x, y, seed + o * 131);   // a different seed per octave
  sum += n * amp;
  norm += amp;
  amp *= gain;                             // persistence
  const nx = x * ROT_C - y * ROT_S;        // turn the grid 0.5 rad…
  y = (x * ROT_S + y * ROT_C) * lac;       // …and make it finer
  x = nx * lac;
}
return sum / norm;                         // roughly −1 to 1
Why each octave gets turned

Every octave reads the same integer grid, just scaled. With lacunarity exactly 2, the grid lines of all the octaves pass through the origin and line up along the axes, and with enough octaves the agreement shows as faint horizontal and vertical structure. Rotating the coordinates by 0.5 radians (about 29°) between octaves means no two of them share a grid orientation, for the cost of four multiplies. The different seed per octave keeps them from repeating each other's pattern.

Aside · Coastlines and mountains

Benoit Mandelbrot made the case for modelling nature this way. His 1967 paper "How Long Is the Coast of Britain?" pointed out that a coastline looks statistically similar at different scales, so its measured length keeps growing as your ruler gets shorter. In 1968 he and John Van Ness described fractional Brownian motion, and The Fractal Geometry of Nature (1982) used it to draw imaginary mountain ranges. The word lacunarity is his too, from the Latin for a gap. The noise literature borrowed it for the frequency step.

Step 3 · Ridges and warpsBreaking the symmetry

In two dimensions the same sum makes a map. Plain fBm still has two giveaways. It's symmetric: valleys have the same shape as peaks turned upside down, while real mountains have sharp crests and broad valleys. And it has no direction. The features are round blobs that don't flow anywhere.

Ridging fixes the first. Fold each octave at zero with 1 − 2|n| and the smooth zero crossings become sharp creases pointing up. The ridge setting blends between the plain and folded versions, inside the octave loop: 

if (ridge > 0) n = lerp(n, 1 - 2 * Math.abs(n), ridge);

Domain warping fixes the second. Instead of reading the noise at the point you asked about, read it at a point that two smoother noise fields have pushed around: 

qx = fbm(x, z)                               // 3 octaves, its own seed
qz = fbm(x, z)                               // 3 octaves, another seed
h  = fbm(x + 4·warp·qx,  z + 4·warp·qz)      // the real terrain, read off-centre

The warp fields are smooth, so neighbouring points get pushed in nearly the same direction, and features get stretched into long curved flows instead of being scrambled. At the default warp of 0.9 a point can move by up to about 3.6 world units, where a typical hill is around ten across.

This is a relief map: brighter is higher, lit from the upper left. Slide ridge up and the crests tighten into bright creases while the valleys widen. Slide warp and the features shear into long sweeps, a bit like folded rock. Each redraw evaluates the height function at 63,000 points, and the readout shows how long that took.

Aside · Where these came from

Warping is almost as old as the noise. The marble in Perlin's 1985 paper is a sine wave with turbulence added to its input, which is a domain warp. Ridged noise was popularised by Ken Musgrave, whose "ridged multifractal" terrain appears in Texturing and Modeling: A Procedural Approach (1994). Inigo Quilez's articles later made fBm warped by fBm a standard tool for terrain and clouds.

Step 4 · Standing it upFrom function to mesh

The third dimension is a flat grid of vertices with every y set to h(x, z). Before that, the height gets shaped. Raw fBm is centred on zero and as likely to dip as to rise. The height function maps it to the range 0 to 1 and raises it to the power 1.6, which spreads out the low ground and keeps the peaks narrow. Real valleys are wide and flat because sediment fills them in. Part two simulates that; here the curve stands in for it.

Normals come from the same grid by central differences, with no extra calls to the noise: 

normal(i, j) = normalize( (H[i−1, j] − H[i+1, j]) / 2Δ,  1,  (H[i, j−1] − H[i, j+1]) / 2Δ )

The builder samples one extra row of heights beyond each edge, so the border vertices get the same two-sided difference as everyone else. That costs almost nothing here, and in part six it's what keeps neighbouring chunks from showing a crease of shading along every seam.

Step 5 · Paint and lightMaking it read as land

A lit grey mesh still doesn't look like land. The colour comes from two numbers per pixel: the height as a fraction of the maximum (h01), and the steepness, measured as 1 − normal.y, which is 0 on flat ground and 1 on a vertical wall. The shader blends four colours with a few smoothsteps: 

col  = mix(grass, scree, smoothstep(0.4, 0.7, h01));      // high ground goes stony
col  = mix(col, rock, smoothstep(0.25, 0.5, slope));      // steep ground loses its soil
snow = smoothstep(0.6, 0.72, h01) * (1 - smoothstep(0.3, 0.55, slope));
col  = mix(col, snowColor, snow);                         // snow, but not on cliffs

Snow needs height and also needs ground flat enough to sit on, so the snowline follows the shape of the crests instead of cutting through the mountains at one altitude. A little noise added to each threshold keeps the edges from looking like contour lines.

Most of the light is baked when the mesh is built. Every vertex marches toward the sun across the height grid and records how close the terrain came to blocking it, which gives soft shadows behind ridges without a shadow map. Each vertex also compares its height with the average of its surroundings at two scales, and points that sit below their neighbourhood get darker, a cheap kind of ambient occlusion. The sky is a function of view direction, and the haze takes its colour from the sky behind each pixel, so distant ridges fade into the horizon instead of into grey.

The same four dials, now in 3D: a 16-unit square with 201 × 201 vertices and 80,000 triangles, rebuilt and relit whenever a slider comes to rest. The readout shows how long that took. Turn on the wireframe to see how plain the geometry is. Reroll changes the seed and keeps the dials, because the seed decides which world you get and the dials decide what kind of world it is.

The view at the top of the page is the same function at a larger scale: height 15 instead of 3.2, seven octaves, and three square rings of mesh around the camera. The inner ring has a vertex every quarter unit, and the outer ring reaches 540 units out with one every 4.5. Part six builds rings like these on the fly as the camera moves.

EpilogueWhat carries over

  • A function beats a map. Nothing is stored, so nothing runs out, and the same seed always gives back the same mountain.
  • Detail comes from sums. One octave is a swell. Six of them at persistence 0.5 are a mountain range.
  • Shape the statistics. Ridging, warping, and the power curve each fix one specific way raw noise doesn't look like land.
  • Bake what doesn't move. The sun stays put, so shadows and occlusion are worked out once per vertex instead of every frame.

Things I'd try next: evaluate the height function in a compute shader so the mesh builds on the GPU; use the noise's analytic derivatives for normals instead of finite differences; and try Quilez's derivative-damped fBm, which suppresses fine detail on steep slopes and looks surprisingly eroded without any simulation.
