Ground Truth · Part 5

The Biomes

Forest in the wet hollows, meadow in between, scree on the dry heights. Nobody placed any of the 243 trees in this valley.

Scroll down to read ↓

The series has its pieces now: mountains, drainage, grass and trees. But every scene so far was put together by hand: this grove here, that meadow there. In a real landscape the pattern carries information. Where the trees stop tells you how cold it gets, and where green turns to tan tells you how much rain falls. Plants spread evenly over everything are one of the quickest ways to make generated terrain look fake.

This part does two things. First it decides what grows where, from altitude, slope and a climate field. Then it places the individual trees and rocks in a way that any region of an endless world could reproduce on its own, without knowing about its neighbours. The second problem shapes the design more than the first, because part six builds the world in chunks.

The valley at the top is 96 units across, and everything in it comes from those rules: 243 trees, 89 rocks and about 126,000 grass blades. Past the valley the ground keeps going out to the haze, painted by the same rules, so far-off woods still read as forest even where no trees are drawn.

Step 1 · ClimateA moisture field

Ecologists summarize where biomes occur with Whittaker's diagram, which plots mean temperature against rainfall and divides the plane into tundra, grassland, desert, forest and so on. I built a small version. Altitude stands in for temperature, and slope gets a veto, since nothing much grows on a cliff. Rainfall needs a field of its own, so I used the same fractal noise that made the mountains, at a broader scale: three octaves at 0.035 cycles per unit, about 29 units per wave against the terrain's 11. Weather patterns are bigger than hills, and a moisture field as busy as the terrain looks like camouflage. 

m = clamp(0.5 · fbm(x · k, z · k) + 0.5 + offset, 0, 1)    // offset is the wet/dry dial

if (slope > 0.55)            return cliff;
if (h01 > treeLine + 0.17)   return alpine;
if (h01 > treeLine)          return m > 0.5 ? meadow : scree;
if (m > 0.5)                 return forest;
if (m > 0.36)                return meadow;
return scrub;

This is a 64-unit stretch of the part-one terrain, coloured by class and lightly hillshaded so the relief shows through. The readout tallies how much of the map each biome covers. Show moisture swaps in the raw field, and its blobs are clearly broader than the hills. Wet ↔ dry shifts every point's moisture by the same amount: slide toward dry and the forests shrink back to the wettest patches, then disappear. Tree line moves the altitude, as a fraction of peak height, above which forest gives way to meadow and scree. Real tree lines work this way, a temperature contour you can see from a distance. Climate scale changes the moisture field's wavelength, and a short one shows why it has to be broad.

Aside · The missing river

A more physical moisture model would use drainage. Water runs downhill and collects, so valley floors are wet because of the shape of the land, and part two computed that. But erosion's output is a grid built by a long simulation, and here moisture has to be a function of position so that part six can evaluate it anywhere. The noise is a stand-in, and the compromise shows if you look for it: this climate doesn't follow the valleys.

Step 2 · PlacementA grid in world coordinates

Knowing that a point is forest doesn't say where each tree stands. Natural spacing is scattered but never clumped, and the usual answer in graphics is Poisson-disk sampling: random points, none closer than a minimum distance. The grove in part four used the simplest version, throwing darts and rejecting any that land too close to an earlier tree. The trouble is that it's a process. Whether a tree survives depends on the trees placed before it, so a chunk can't place its trees without knowing its neighbour's, and their neighbours', and so on.

The alternative that works in pieces is a jittered grid. Divide the world into cells of 2.6 units, numbered from the world origin with floor(x / cell), not from any chunk's corner. Each cell holds at most one candidate tree, moved somewhere in the middle 76% of the cell by the cell's own hash. Then the climate gets a vote and the hash decides: 

x = (ci + 0.12 + 0.76 · hash(ci, cj)) · cell          // same for z with another hash
p = forest ? 0.92 : meadow ? 0.1 : scrub ? 0.05 : 0
p *= 1 − smooth((h01 − (treeLine − 0.12)) / 0.12)       // thin out below the tree line
keep the tree if hash(ci, cj, salt) < p

A tree's existence and position depend only on its cell's two integers, so any query over any region returns the same trees. Spacing comes for free, since two candidates can't share a cell. The grid is a little more regular than true Poisson-disk sampling, but on hilly ground with hashed sizes and rotations I can't see it. Near the tree line the odds fade out, and the trees that survive are scaled down by up to 45%. Real tree lines end in stunted trees, not a fence.

Every tree, rock and grass blade on this 40-unit block was accepted or rejected by those rules, and the readout counts them. Slide toward dry and the forest retreats into the wettest hollow; slide toward wet and it spreads over the shoulders of the hill. Raise the tree line and trees climb higher. Rocks use their own grid, with 5-unit cells: they're likely on scree, less likely on alpine ground and cliffs, occasional in scrub and rare elsewhere, and their size is the square of a hash, so there are many more small ones than large. Grass is planted from hashed candidate points with odds by biome: thick in meadows, half as dense under trees, sparse in scrub, nearly absent on scree. The ground itself is tinted toward each biome's colour, with forest pulled hardest toward a dark canopy green.

Deep dive: six trees playing a forest

The valley at the top has 243 trees but only six tree meshes, the same six skeletons the part-four grove uses, each instanced with hashed rotation and scale. At this distance I use a lighter mesh per tree: a third of the leaf cards, each one scaled up so the crown covers the same area, and no twigs thinner than 0.022 units, since the leaves hide them anyway. That takes a tree from about 1,800 leaves to about 600.

Each variant is one instanced draw call for bark and one for leaves, so the trees cost twelve draw calls however many there are. The grass goes the part-three way instead, one merged buffer, because a seven-vertex blade isn't worth a 16-float matrix of its own. Roughly: instance when one copy has more data than its matrix, merge when it has less.

EpilogueWhat carries over

  • Let the pattern carry information. Altitude, slope and moisture are enough to make a landscape that reads as a climate.
  • Number things from the world, not the region. Cells indexed in world coordinates make any query reproducible.
  • Prefer rules that don't need neighbours. A jittered grid looks almost as good as Poisson-disk sampling and works in pieces.
  • Paint the ground with the same rules. Where the plants stop being drawn, the tint still says forest or scree.

Things I'd try next: add a temperature field so north-facing slopes get a lower tree line; vary the tree species by biome instead of using one kind everywhere; and replace the moisture noise with a cheap function-of-position stand-in for drainage, such as how far below its surroundings a point sits, so the wet ground follows the valleys.
