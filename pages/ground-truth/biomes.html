Ground Truth · Part 5

The Biomes

Forest in the damp hollows, open meadow across the shoulders, scree on the dry heights: 243 trees placed purely by rules.

Scroll down to read ↓

Real landscapes telegraph their climate from miles away. An abrupt contour across a mountainside tells you where winter gets too cold for timber, and bands of green trace where moisture lingers. Spreading grass and trees uniformly across generated hills breaks that illusion almost instantly. Up to this point we have built mountains, erosion, grass, and trees, yet each scene still stitched them together by hand for one bounded patch.

Here we turn placement over to rules, with a strict engineering constraint hanging over everything. First, we classify any point on the ground into a biome from its elevation, slope, and a broad moisture field. Second, we scatter individual trees and boulders in a way that any slice of an infinite world can compute in complete isolation, without ever asking neighboring chunks what they planted. That second requirement ends up steering the whole architecture, because part six will stream the world in piece by piece.

The 96-unit valley circling overhead holds 243 trees, 89 rocks, and roughly 126,000 grass blades, every one accepted or rejected by the same set of rules. Beyond the valley rim, bare ground stretches out into the haze wearing the exact same biome ground tints, so distant hillsides still read as timberland even where no tree geometry is drawn.

Step 1 · ClimateA moisture field

Ecologists often map terrestrial biomes on a Whittaker diagram, plotting annual precipitation against average temperature to carve out regions of tundra, desert, grassland, and forest. We can build a compact procedural version with three inputs available at every (x, z). Altitude stands in for temperature, steep slope gets a veto so sheer rock faces stay bare, and rainfall comes from a second fractal noise field tuned to a much broader wavelength than the hills: three octaves at 0.035 cycles per unit (roughly 29 world units per wave, compared with 11 for the terrain). Weather systems span whole valleys, and a moisture field as busy as the terrain makes the map look like mottled camouflage. 

m = clamp(0.5 · fbm(x · k, z · k) + 0.5 + offset, 0, 1)    // offset is the wet/dry dial

if (slope > 0.55)            return cliff;
if (h01 > treeLine + 0.17)   return alpine;
if (h01 > treeLine)          return m > 0.5 ? meadow : scree;
if (m > 0.5)                 return forest;
if (m > 0.36)                return meadow;
return scrub;

This 64-unit slice of part-one terrain is classified pixel by pixel, lightly hillshaded so you can still read the ridges, with the color key tucked into the bottom-left corner and area percentages along the readout below. Clicking show moisture swaps in the raw blue rainfall field so you can see how wide its weather bands are compared to individual hills. Shifting wet ↔ dry adds a uniform offset across the whole map, pulling forests back into the dampest pockets until drought clears them out. Moving tree line raises or lowers the elevation threshold (as a percentage of peak height) where woods give way to high meadow and bare scree, and shrinking climate scale shows how quickly a high-frequency moisture field breaks up into noisy patches.

Aside · The missing river

In a fully physical landscape, soil moisture follows drainage: rain collects in troughs, keeping valley bottoms lush while exposed spurs dry out. Part two computed exactly that flow map, yet erosion only runs on a stored grid over a finite patch. Because part six needs to evaluate biomes anywhere in an open world on demand, broad fractal noise stands in for rainfall here, a compromise you can spot if you look closely, since damp patches cross over ridgelines instead of hugging the valley floors.

Step 2 · PlacementA grid in world coordinates

Labeling a hillside as forest still leaves the problem of deciding where individual trunks stand. Real woods look organic when trees are scattered randomly while respecting a minimum elbow room. The classic graphics algorithm for that is Poisson-disk sampling, and part four's hillside grove used its simplest variant: throw random darts and reject any spot that lands within 2.6 units of an earlier tree. That works fine inside one fixed boundary, yet it fails immediately in a chunked world: accepting or rejecting a tree near a chunk border depends on whatever trees the neighboring chunk happened to place first, which depends on its neighbors, rippling outward without end.

A jittered grid breaks that dependency chain cleanly. Slice the entire world plane into 2.6-unit squares indexed from the global origin via floor(x / cell) and floor(z / cell) so cell boundaries never care where a chunk begins or ends. Each square gets at most one candidate tree, nudged inside the middle 76% of the cell by hashing the cell's integer pair. Then we query the biome at that candidate point and let a final hash decide whether a tree takes root: 

x = (ci + 0.12 + 0.76 · hash(ci, cj)) · cell          // same for z with another hash
p = forest ? 0.92 : meadow ? 0.1 : scrub ? 0.05 : 0
p *= 1 − smooth((h01 − (treeLine − 0.12)) / 0.12)       // thin out below the tree line
keep the tree if hash(ci, cj, salt) < p

Because every tree's existence, position, variant, and yaw depend strictly on its own (ci, cj) integers, any two chunks querying overlapping bounds agree on every trunk without ever communicating. Minimum spacing is built into the cell size, since two trees can never share a square. On flat paper a jittered grid looks slightly more regular than true Poisson-disk sampling, yet once draped over rolling hills with varied trunk sizes and rotations the underlying lattice disappears. Near the tree line, survival odds taper off smoothly and surviving trees shrink by up to 45%, so timberland frays naturally into stunted stragglers rather than ending along a sharp fence.

Every tree, boulder, and blade on this 40-unit diorama passed through those stateless rules, with live counts shown beneath the controls. Slide wet ↔ dry toward dry to watch trees retreat into the dampest hollow, or toward wet to see canopy creep across the slopes; slide tree line to push timber higher up the ridge. Boulders run on a coarser 5-unit jittered grid (common on scree, occasional on cliffs, alpine ridges, and scrub, rare in meadows), with their scale squared from a uniform hash so small stones far outnumber massive boulders. Grass uses hashed candidate spots weighted by biome (dense in meadows, halved beneath shaded canopy, sparse in scrub, almost gone on scree), while the terrain mesh itself receives a per-vertex tint pulled strongly toward deep canopy green wherever forest belongs.

Deep dive: six trees playing a forest

The 243 trees in the hero valley reuse the exact six space-colonized skeletons from part four, stamped out with hashed scales and rotations. Because we view them from further back here, each variant builds a lighter level-of-detail mesh: one-third as many leaf cards (scaled slightly larger to keep crown volume intact) and twigs thinner than 0.022 units skipped since foliage conceals them anyway. That trims a tree from around 1,800 leaves down to roughly 600.

Each variant needs one instanced draw call for bark and one for foliage, keeping the entire forest at twelve draw calls regardless of tree count. Grass sticks with part three's merged static buffer instead, because a seven-vertex blade is lighter than a 16-float transform matrix. As a rule of thumb: instance objects whose geometry outweighs a transform matrix, and merge anything smaller straight into one buffer.

EpilogueRules without memory

Three local measurements (altitude, slope, and a broad moisture field) proved enough to organize bare hills into recognizable climate zones, while indexing jittered placement grids from the world origin made every tree and boulder completely reproducible without checking neighbors. Painting the terrain mesh with the same biome rules bridges the gap into the distance so wooded ridges still look wooded past the draw range of individual crowns.

With more time, I would add a slope-aspect term so shaded north-facing flanks carry a lower tree line than sunny south slopes, grow distinct tree species per biome instead of sharing one hardwood shape everywhere, and replace pure noise moisture with a stateless approximation of local concavity so damp forests hug valleys without needing an erosion pass. Now that every mountain, tree, rock, and grass blade can be reconstructed from coordinates alone, we are finally ready to take down the walls around the world.

Next: Part 6, The Horizon. Chunks, distance-based level of detail, and a strict millisecond budget turn these stateless rules into a landscape that never ends.
