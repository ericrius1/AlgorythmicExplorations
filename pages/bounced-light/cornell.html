Bounced Light · Part 5

The Cornell Box

Every pixel above is an average of random light paths, and it gets more accurate with every frame you leave it running. This is a path tracer, aimed at the most famous box in computer graphics.

Scroll down to read ↓

The box at the top is being rendered as you read. Each frame, every pixel sends a few random paths into the room, follows each one through its bounces, and adds what it found to a running sum. The counter in the corner is how many paths each pixel has averaged so far. It stops at 16,384. The hero also uses a better way of picking its random numbers and a light filter for its first second or so; Step 4 explains both, and the figures about noise below use neither.

Part four faked its soft shadows, skylight and contact darkness with local approximations built on the distance function. Those were all stand-ins for one equation, which James Kajiya wrote down in 1986: 

L(x, ω) = Le(x, ω) + ∫ f(x, ω', ω) · L(x', −ω') · cos θ' dω'
// light leaving x = light it emits + light arriving from every direction ω', scattered by the surface

The light arriving at x from some direction is the light leaving whatever point x' it came from, so the equation contains itself. Unroll it and it becomes an integral over every path light could take through the scene: every number of bounces, every route, every detour off the red wall. Parts one to three solved the 2D version with a hierarchy of probes. In 3D the integral has too many dimensions for that, and the tool that still works is Monte Carlo integration.

Step 1 · Monte CarloIntegrating with random samples

To integrate a function you can't afford to evaluate everywhere, evaluate it at random points, weight each answer by how likely that point was to be picked, and average. The average converges to the true integral. For the rendering equation, one random evaluation means following one random path of light through all its bounces and reporting what it brought back.

In the shader, a diffuse bounce is three lines. The new direction is picked with probability proportional to cos θ, which is the same shape as the Lambert surface's own weighting, so the two cancel and the throughput only picks up the surface colour: 

throughput *= h.albedo;         // 0.73 for the white walls
ro = p + h.n * 1e-4;
rd = cosineDir(h.n);            // random, weighted toward the normal

After three bounces, Russian roulette starts ending paths at random, with the survivors boosted to make up for the ones it kills. That keeps long paths cheap without biasing the average.

The catch is the noise. The variance of a Monte Carlo average falls as 1/N, so the error, its square root, falls as 1/√N. Four times the samples buys half the grain. The figure below lets you watch that happen, and it measures the noise as it goes: the readout is the estimated standard error of the average, relative to its brightness, over a grid of pixels.

It starts at one sample per pixel per frame, so you can catch it early. On my machine the readout says about 12% at 16 samples, 3% at 250 and 1.5% at 1,000: every factor of four in samples halves it. Pause in the first second to see what a handful of random paths looks like. Nothing about the algorithm changes from the first sample to the ten-thousandth. It's the same random walk, averaged more times. This figure and the next one use plain random numbers and show the raw average, so the readout means what it says.

Aside · The box itself

The Cornell box comes from Cornell's Program of Computer Graphics. Cindy Goral, Kenneth Torrance, Donald Greenberg and Bennett Battaile used it in their 1984 radiosity paper, and they built a physical box to compare the rendering against. Coloured side walls and white everywhere else make the light bounced off the walls easy to see, and the lab kept measuring and publishing data for it. It became the standard test scene for global illumination.

Step 2 · Light samplingNext-event estimation

Plain random bouncing has a problem you can work out on paper. The lamp in the next figure is 0.24 units across, in a box two units on a side. From the middle of the floor, a cosine-weighted bounce points at it about one time in 220. The other 219 paths keep wandering, and most of them leave through the open front or die in roulette with nothing. The image is a few bright speckles from the lucky paths and darkness everywhere else, and it takes a very long time to average out.

The fix is to ask the lamp directly. At every diffuse bounce, pick a random point on the lamp, send a shadow ray toward it, and if nothing is in the way, add its contribution, weighted by how big the lamp looks from here: 

// a random point on the lamp, seen from p: pdf is 1/area, so convert to solid angle
let sh = intersect(p + h.n * 1e-4, ld);
if (sh.mat == 3) {
  radiance += throughput * (h.albedo / PI) * lightLe() * (cosS * cosL * area / d2);
}

The random walk carries on as before. When a later bounce happens to hit the lamp, that hit is thrown away, because the shadow ray already counted it. Otherwise the light would be counted twice.

It starts with light sampling off. After about 900 samples per pixel the readout is still around 45%, and the room is grainy. Switch it on and it's under 5% within about 150 samples. Same scene, same lamp, same average in the end. Only the way the samples are chosen changed. The general idea is called importance sampling: spend samples where the integrand is large. Most of what makes a production path tracer fast is more of it.

Step 3 · Indirect lightOne bounce at a time

In part four, soft fill light and dark corners were each their own approximation. Here they come out of the same loop, one bounce at a time. The slider caps how many surfaces a path can touch.

At one bounce you only see direct light. Everything the lamp can't see is black, including the whole ceiling, because the lamp only shines downward. At two, which is where the figure starts, the ceiling lights up from the floor, the shadows fill in, and the walls start to tint their neighbours: pink on the left side of the tall sphere, green on the right side of the small one. By four or five the corners settle into the soft contact darkness that ambient occlusion was imitating, here because little light makes it into a crevice and back out. Past that the image barely changes, since a path that has bounced six times off 73% white walls carries less than a sixth of its original weight.

Step 4 · AssemblyThe full renderer

Here's the whole thing with the knobs exposed. Press and drag on the image to move the lamp across the ceiling. Anything you change makes the accumulated samples wrong for the new scene, so the sum restarts.

This figure, the bounce figure and the hero do three things the noise figures don't, so that a restart doesn't look like a snowstorm:

  • Better random numbers for the first bounce. The point on the lamp and the bounce direction come from a Halton sequence, which spreads a pixel's samples evenly instead of letting them clump. Each pixel shifts that sequence by a value from a 64×64 blue-noise tile, so neighbouring pixels make different mistakes and the leftover grain is fine and even.
  • A cap on one kind of path. Light that bounces off a wall, then off the metal sphere, then reaches the lamp can't be found by light sampling, because a shadow ray can't aim through a mirror. Those paths are rare and very bright, so they're capped. That trims the faint caustic the sphere throws on the floor.
  • An edge-aware filter, only while the sum is young. Each pixel is averaged with neighbours that see the same surface, facing the same way, at the same depth, and whose brightness agrees to within the pixel's own measured noise. Its radius shrinks as 1/√N and it switches off at about 1,200 samples, so the image it settles on is the plain average. The denoise button turns it off if you want to see the raw sum.

Lamp size changes the shadows and nothing else. The lamp's total power stays the same as it grows, so the room keeps its brightness. Shrink it to 0.08 wide and the shadows go sharp; stretch it to 1.4 and they spread out. That's the soft shadow part four approximated with k · h / t, now coming from a real area light.

The front sphere starts as a gold mirror. Its reflection shows the red wall, the lamp and the other sphere, plus a black hole where it sees out through the open front of the box. A mirror bounce is deterministic, so the reflection is as sharp as the camera can resolve. Switch it to glossy and roughness scatters each reflected ray inside a small sphere around the mirror direction. Raise the roughness and the reflection blurs the same way the shadow blurred when the lamp grew, for the same reason: the pixel is now averaging over a spread of directions instead of one.

EpilogueWhat the box teaches

This is the algorithm behind most film rendering today: Kajiya's equation, estimated with random paths, plus decades of better ways to choose them. Production renderers add denoisers, sample reuse and much smarter importance sampling, but they're estimating the same average this tab is.

  • Random sampling handles high dimensions. A path with eight bounces is a point in a space with over a dozen dimensions, and Monte Carlo doesn't care.
  • Noise falls slowly. 1/√N means each halving of the grain costs four times the work, so better samples beat more samples.
  • Count every path once. Light sampling and the random walk can both find the lamp. Getting the bookkeeping right is most of the work.
  • Every blur is an average. Soft shadows, glossy reflections and colour bleeding are all the same integral over a wider set of directions.

Things I'd try next: multiple importance sampling (Eric Veach's 1995 method for mixing light sampling and BRDF sampling), so a tiny lamp seen in a glossy sphere stops being noisy; temporal reprojection, so dragging the lamp could reuse the old samples instead of restarting; and a BVH so the box could hold a real mesh instead of two spheres.

Everything in this box is opaque. Nothing in it bends light: no glass, no water, no lens. Refraction is where path tracing produces caustics and focus, and it's where colour stops being three numbers, because how much glass bends light depends on the wavelength.
