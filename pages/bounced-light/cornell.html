Part fourStepping into 3D in part four came with an awkward trade-off: it gained crisprsquo;s step into 3D came with an awkward trade-off: it gained crisp three-dimensional forms, yet fell back on fake local approximations for soft shadows, skylight, and contact darkening. Every one of those stand-ins tries to imitate a single physical balance that James Kajiya published in 1986 as the rendering equation: 

L(x, ω) = Le(x, ω) + ∫ f(x, ω', ω) · L(x', −ω') · cos θ' dω'
// light leaving x = light it emits + light arriving from every direction ω', scattered by the surface

Notice the recursion buried on the right side: the radiance arriving at point x from direction ω' equals the radiance leaving whatever surface x' sits down that ray. Expanding that recursion turns a single pixel's brightness into a high-dimensional integral over every possible multi-bounce path light can travel through the room, including every detour off the red and green walls. Radiance cascades solved the 2D version by building spatial probe grids. In three dimensions, a path with eight bounces lives in a space with more than a dozen dimensions, far too vast for any grid, so this part turns to Monte Carlo integration instead.

While you have been reading these two paragraphs, the Cornell box at the top of the page has been tracing fresh random paths on every animation frame and adding them into a running average (the counter beneath it tracks samples per pixel up to 16,384). That hero render also turns on stratified blue-noise sampling and a light temporary denoiser during its first second or two; Step 4 unpacks both, while the noise experiments coming up first disable them so you can watch raw Monte Carlo convergence unadorned.

Step 1 · Monte CarloIntegrating with random samples

Whenever an integral spans too many dimensions to evaluate on a regular grid, Monte Carlo estimates it by picking random points in the domain, dividing each sample by the probability of picking it, and averaging the results. Applied to Kajiya's equation, a single sample means firing a ray from the camera, letting it bounce randomly around the box, and recording whatever light it eventually brings home.

In WGSL, a diffuse matte bounce reduces to three lines. Choosing the random outgoing direction with probability proportional to cos θ matches a Lambertian surface's cosine weighting exactly, so the probability density cancels out of the math and leaves only the wall's albedo: 

throughput *= h.albedo;         // 0.73 for the matte white walls
ro = p + h.n * 1e-4;
rd = cosineDir(h.n);            // random bounce, weighted toward the normal

Once a path survives three bounces, Russian roulette starts terminating weak paths at random while boosting the weight of survivors by the reciprocal survival probability. That keeps long bounce chains cheap without introducing any systematic bias into the final average.

The price of Monte Carlo simplicity is noise. The variance of a random average shrinks as 1/N, meaning its standard error (the visible grain) falls only as 1/√N: cutting the grain in half demands four times as many samples. Below, you can watch that curve unfold live while a compute shader tracks the estimated relative standard error across a grid of pixels.

The demo starts slowly at 1 sample per pixel per frame so you can hit pause in the first second and inspect what a handful of random walks looks like. Let it accumulate (or crank up samples per frame) and watch the measured noise drop roughly from 12% around 16 samples to 3% around 250 samples and 1.5% near 1,000, halving every time sample count quadruples. Because this figure and the next one use plain pseudo-random numbers with zero filtering, both the grain and the percentage readout reflect pure textbook Monte Carlo.

Aside · The box itself

The Cornell box originated at Cornell University's Program of Computer Graphics. In 1984, Cindy Goral, Kenneth Torrance, Donald Greenberg, and Bennett Battaile constructed a physical box with one red wall, one green wall, and neutral white surfaces elsewhere so they could photograph real color bleeding and compare their radiosity renders side by side. The lab went on to publish precise spectral reflectance and geometry measurements, turning the box into the benchmark scene for global illumination.

Step 2 · Light samplingNext-event estimation

Blind random bouncing falls apart quickly as soon as the light source gets small. In the figure below, the ceiling lamp is just 0.24 units square inside a 2-unit room. Standing in the middle of the floor, a cosine-weighted random ray has roughly a 1-in-220 chance of stumbling directly onto that patch of ceiling. The other 219 paths keep ricocheting around the walls, and most escape through the open front or end in Russian roulette carrying zero light. The raw average turns into harsh bright fireflies scattered over dark salt-and-pepper grain.

Rather than waiting for blind bounces to get lucky, next-event estimation checks the lamp explicitly at every diffuse hit. Each bounce picks a random point on the ceiling emitter, shoots a shadow ray straight toward it, and if nothing blocks the line of sight, adds the lamp's direct illumination scaled by the solid angle the lamp subtends from that spot: 

// random point on the lamp seen from p: pdf is 1/area, converted to solid angle
let sh = intersect(p + h.n * 1e-4, ld);
if (sh.mat == 3) {
  radiance += throughput * (h.albedo / PI) * lightLe() * (cosS * cosL * area / d2);
}

The random walk still bounces onward to gather indirect illumination from the walls and spheres. The only bookkeeping rule is avoiding double-counting: if a diffuse bounce later lands on the lamp by accident, its emission gets discarded because the explicit shadow ray at the previous vertex already accounted for direct lighting.

With light sampling: off, even 900 samples per pixel leave the noise readout hovering around 45% in a storm of speckle. Click the button to turn light sampling: on and watch the error plunge below 5% within about 150 samples. Both modes converge to the exact same mathematical image; concentrating samples where the integrand is actually bright (known broadly as importance sampling) simply gets there orders of magnitude faster.

Step 3 · Indirect lightOne bounce at a time

Part four needed separate chunks of shader code for soft shadows, fill lighting, and ambient occlusion. Inside a path tracer, every one of those phenomena falls out of the exact same loop as you allow paths to take more bounces. Drag max bounces from 1 direct up to 8 to add each layer of light transport one hop at a time.

At 1 direct, only surfaces with an unobstructed view of the downward-facing lamp get lit, leaving every shadow pitch-black and plunging the entire ceiling into darkness. Stepping up to 2 (the starting state) bounces light off the floor onto the ceiling, lifts the shadows out of black, and starts tinting the matte spheres with pink from the red wall on the left and green from the right. By four or five bounces, soft darkening settles naturally into the tight corners where the spheres touch the floor, because few multi-bounce paths manage to squeeze in and escape back out. Beyond six bounces, changes become nearly invisible: six matte reflections off 73% white walls leave a path carrying less than a sixth of its starting energy.

Step 4 · AssemblyThe full renderer

Putting everything together opens up interactive control of the room. Drag anywhere across the box to slide the ceiling lamp to a new position, toggle the small front sphere between matte, mirror, and glossy, and tweak the lighting geometry. Whenever you change the scene, the accumulated samples no longer match, so the buffer resets and begins converging anew.

To keep those restarts from flashing into ugly white snow, this figure (along with the hero and bounce demo) adds three variance-reduction tricks that were switched off in the noise experiments. First, the primary bounce draws its lamp point and scatter angle from a low-discrepancy Halton sequence shifted per pixel by a 64×64 void-and-cluster blue-noise tile (Cranley–Patterson rotation), preventing samples from clumping inside a pixel while turning residual error into fine, even grain across neighbors. Second, paths that bounce off a matte wall, hit the reflective metal sphere, and then stumble onto the lamp cannot be sampled via shadow rays through the mirror; capping those rare, ultra-bright spikes suppresses fireflies (at the small cost of trimming the faint floor caustic under the gold sphere). Third, while sample counts are low, an edge-aware bilateral filter averages pixels that share the same surface normal, depth, and material within each pixel's measured variance; its filter radius shrinks as 1/√N and shuts off completely around 1,200 samples so the settled render is pure unfiltered path tracing. Click denoise: on to turn the filter off whenever you want to inspect the raw accumulation.

Adjusting lamp width isolates shadow softness cleanly because the shader scales the lamp's radiance inversely with its area, keeping total emitted power constant. Shrink the lamp down to 0.08 and shadows snap crisp; widen it toward 1.40 and shadows melt smoothly across the floor and spheres, reproducing physically what part four's k · h / t formula could only approximate.

The front sphere begins as a polished gold mirror. Because ideal reflection bounces every ray along a single deterministic angle, its curved surface reflects razor-sharp images of the red wall, green wall, white sphere, ceiling lamp, and the black rectangle of the box's open front. Click sphere: mirror to cycle to glossy, then raise glossy roughness: scattering each reflected ray across a small cone around the mirror direction blurs the reflection in the exact same mathematical way widening the lamp blurs shadows, by averaging over a broader slice of incoming directions.

EpilogueWhat the box teaches

Strip away the production engineering around modern animated films and architectural renderers, and this loop is still what sits underneath: Kajiya's rendering equation estimated with random walks, paired with decades of smarter ways to draw samples. Monte Carlo handles high-dimensional bounce integrals without breaking down, yet its 1/√N decay punishes brute force, which is why tricks like next-event estimation, low-discrepancy sequences, and careful single-counting bookkeeping buy far more clarity than raw sample counts ever could. Along the way, soft shadows, glossy reflections, and multi-bounce color bleeding all reveal themselves as variations on one theme: averaging light over wider bundles of directions.

There is plenty of room to push a browser path tracer further: Eric Veach's multiple importance sampling (1995) to blend shadow-ray and glossy-BRDF sampling cleanly when a tiny bright lamp reflects in a rough sphere, temporal reprojection so sliding the lamp could reuse older samples instead of clearing the buffer, and a bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) to trade two analytic spheres for arbitrary triangle meshes.

Before going there, though, one whole half of optics is still missing from this room. Everything in the Cornell box is opaque: light reflects off walls and metal without ever passing inside them. Transparent glass brings refraction, focused caustics, and camera lenses, and it finally breaks the habit of representing color as three RGB numbers, because glass bends every wavelength of light at a slightly different angle.
