Bounced Light · Part 4

Leaving Flatland

A copper sculpture with no mesh, no vertices and no model file. One function says how far away the nearest surface is, and every pixel marches a ray through it. Drag to walk around it.

Scroll down to read ↓

The first three parts of this series were flat. Light moved around a 2D canvas, and the thing that made it fast was a distance field: a texture where every pixel stores how far it is to the nearest surface, rebuilt every frame with the jump flood. Rays used it to leap across empty space instead of crawling.

Moving that plan to 3D means a 3D texture, and memory and rebuild time both grow with the cube of the resolution. So for this part I kept the idea and dropped the storage. A distance field is a function of position. For shapes made of math it doesn't need to be sampled and stored, because you can write the distance down directly: 

// negative inside, zero on the surface, positive outside
fn sdSphere(p: vec3f, r: f32) -> f32 { return length(p) - r; }

fn sdTorus(p: vec3f, t: vec2f) -> f32 {
  let q = vec2f(length(p.xz) - t.x, p.y);
  return length(q) - t.y;
}

A whole scene is one function, map(p), that returns the smallest of its shapes' distances: the nearest surface in the world is the nearest surface of whichever shape is closest. The sculpture at the top is a pedestal, a tilted torus and three wandering spheres, and that's the entire scene description. There's no geometry stored anywhere. Each pixel calls map a few dozen times to find its surface, then up to fifty more to shade it.

Step 1 · SculptingBlending shapes together

Combining shapes with min() welds them like machine parts. Where two surfaces overlap they meet in a sharp crease. Swap min for a smooth minimum and any two surfaces within k of each other blend into one: 

// within k of each other, two surfaces blend instead of creasing
fn smin(a: f32, b: f32, k: f32) -> f32 {
  let h = clamp(0.5 + 0.5 * (b - a) / k, 0.0, 1.0);
  return mix(b, a, h) - k * h * (1.0 - h);
}

Where one distance is much smaller than the other, h pins to 0 or 1 and this is a plain min. Where they're close, the last term subtracts up to k/4, which pulls the surface outward and fills the corner. The metaballs and lava-lamp wax earlier in the series merged the same way, but through a texture. Here it's one formula, and it's most of what SDF modelling is: build a shape from primitives, then choose a k for every joint.

The sphere and the box sit 0.2 apart, and a small sphere orbits through both of them. At k = 0 it's a plain min, and the small sphere cuts into the others with hard creases. Around 0.2 the creases fill in and the small sphere pulls a smooth neck out of whatever it passes. The big sphere and the box stay apart until k goes past about 0.4, because the blend has to pull each surface 0.1 outward to close the gap and the most it can pull is k/4. The shapes never change. Only the rule for combining their distances does.

Step 2 · Sphere tracingStepping by the distance

Every pixel gets a ray, and each ray has to find the first surface in its way. The brute force is to step forward a fixed amount and check whether you're inside something yet. That takes thousands of steps, and it can still step clean through a thin wall.

The distance function gives you something better. If map(p) says the nearest surface is d away, then a sphere of radius d around p is empty, and the ray can move d forward without missing anything. Then it asks again.

This is sphere tracing. John Hart named it and worked out when it's safe in a 1996 paper. The whole loop in the shader is a few lines: 

for (var i = 0; i < MAX_STEPS; i++) {
  let h = map(ro + rd * tt);
  if (h.x < 0.001 * (1.0 + tt)) { hit = true; break; }  // close enough: a hit
  tt += h.x;                                            // the safe step
  if (tt > TMAX) { break; }                             // nothing out there
}

The tolerance grows with distance, so far-away surfaces don't need sub-pixel precision. Out in the open the ray takes big steps, and near a surface it slows down until it's within tolerance. That means the cost depends on what each pixel is looking at, and you can see it:

This is the hero scene again, coloured by how many steps each pixel's ray took. Surfaces that face the camera are cheap and dark. Silhouettes run warmer, because a ray that skims past an edge keeps getting small answers. The most expensive part of the image is the floor near the horizon, where rays run almost parallel to it. At the default budget of 48 steps, those rays give up before reaching anything and show up teal. Drag the budget down and the teal spreads inward from the horizon and the silhouettes; push it to 128 and almost everything finishes. Every sphere tracer has a budget like this and some policy for what to draw when a ray runs out.

Aside · Demoscene roots

Sphere tracing caught on in the demoscene, where whole animated films have to fit in 4 or 64 kilobytes of code. There's no room for meshes, but a distance function is a few lines. Íñigo Quílez's 2008 talk "Rendering Worlds with Two Triangles" made the approach popular, and his articles on distance functions, smooth minimum and soft shadows are where most people learn it. They're why so many Shadertoy shaders start with a function called map.

Step 3 · ShadingNormals, shadows and occlusion

Shading needs a surface normal, and the distance function provides that too. The gradient of a distance field points in the direction distance grows fastest, which is straight out of the surface. Four extra calls to map, at the corners of a tiny tetrahedron, estimate it.

Shadows come next. March a second ray from the surface toward the light, and if it hits anything the point is in shadow. That gives hard shadows. For soft ones, look at how close the shadow ray came to hitting something on the way: 

// h: distance to the nearest surface here · t: how far along the shadow ray
res = min(res, k * h / t);
t += clamp(h, 0.012, 0.3);

A shadow ray that passed close to an edge was nearly blocked, so it should be nearly dark. The ratio h/t is roughly the angle by which it missed, as seen from the shaded point, and the smallest one along the march sets how much light gets through. k is sharpness: a big k only darkens very near misses, so the shadow edge is hard, and a small k darkens wide misses too. It behaves a lot like a bigger or smaller light, though no light is ever sampled.

Ambient occlusion uses the distance function the same way. Step out along the normal five times. If a probe 0.27 units out finds the nearest surface only 0.1 away, something is hanging over this point, and it should get less light from the sky: 

for (var i = 1; i <= 5; i++) {
  let h = 0.03 + 0.12 * f32(i);            // how far out this probe is
  occ += (h - map(p + n * h).x) * sca;     // how much less room there is than that
  sca *= 0.7;                              // nearer probes count more
}
return clamp(1.0 - 1.6 * occ, 0.0, 1.0);

Drag shadow sharpness down to 2 and the sculpture's shadow spreads into a broad smudge; push it to 64 and the edge goes crisp. Then turn ambient occlusion off. The light and shadows stay the same, but the inside of the torus, the joins between the blobs and the foot of the pedestal all lighten, and the sculpture looks pasted onto the floor. Five calls to map per pixel put that contact back.

EpilogueWhat got left behind

Going to 3D cost the thing parts one to three were about. Radiance cascades answered "what does every point see?", so light really arrived, bounced and scattered. Sphere tracing only answers "what does the camera see?", and everything else became a heuristic: shadows from near misses, skylight from a dot product, contact darkness from five probes up the normal.

  • A distance is a safe step. Knowing how far the nearest surface is turns ray marching from a guess into a guarantee.
  • Cost follows the picture. Silhouettes and grazing floors are where a sphere tracer spends its time, so that's where it fails first.
  • One function can answer many questions. The same map gives the hit, the normal, the soft shadow and the occlusion.
  • Local stand-ins add up to less than the real thing. Each approximation looks fine alone. Put them in one scene and the missing bounce light shows.

Things I'd try next: domain repetition, which tiles a shape across space for the cost of a mod; cone tracing, so one ray serves a few pixels at once; and over-relaxed stepping (Keinert and others, 2014), which steps a bit further than the distance allows and backs up when it overshoots, to save steps on grazing rays.

There's an equation for what all these heuristics are approximating. James Kajiya published it in 1986 as the rendering equation: the light leaving a point is what it emits, plus the light arriving from every direction, scattered by the surface. There's no cascade for it in 3D, but you can estimate it with random sampling.
