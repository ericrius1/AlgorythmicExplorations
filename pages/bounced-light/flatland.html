Bounced Light · Part 4

Leaving Flatland

A floating copper sculpture built without a single triangle, vertex buffer, or 3D model file. One mathematical function reports the distance to the nearest surface, and every pixel marches a ray through it. Drag sideways to walk around the plinth.

Scroll down to read ↓

Everything across the first three parts lived in a 2D slice, sped up by a distance field: a texture storing how far each pixel sat from the nearest wall, rebuilt every frame with a jump flood so rays could leap across open space rather than crawling pixel by pixel. Taking that texture-backed plan straight into three dimensions runs headfirst into a wall of cubic growth, because a 3D voxel grid multiplies both memory and rebuild work by an entire extra axis.

There is a clean way around storing a 3D grid at all. A distance field is simply a function mapping any point in space to a scalar distance. For primitives built out of algebra, you can evaluate that exact distance right inside the fragment shader with zero memory overhead: 

// negative inside, zero on the surface, positive outside
fn sdSphere(p: vec3f, r: f32) -> f32 { return length(p) - r; }

fn sdTorus(p: vec3f, t: vec2f) -> f32 {
  let q = vec2f(length(p.xz) - t.x, p.y);
  return length(q) - t.y;
}

Combining primitives into an entire scene takes one function, map(p), that evaluates every object and returns the smallest distance (the nearest surface in the world is simply whichever shape sits closest). The floating sculpture above consists of one flat floor plane, a rounded pedestal box, a slowly rotating torus, and three drifting spheres. No mesh sits in GPU memory; each pixel calls map a few dozen times to locate its surface hit and up to fifty more times to figure out its shading.

Step 1 · SculptingBlending shapes together

Taking the plain min() of two distance fields joins shapes like rigid machined parts, leaving a sharp crease wherever they intersect. Swapping min for a polynomial smooth minimum softens any pair of surfaces that come within a radius k of each other so they flow together like molten metal: 

// within k of each other, two surfaces blend instead of creasing
fn smin(a: f32, b: f32, k: f32) -> f32 {
  let h = clamp(0.5 + 0.5 * (b - a) / k, 0.0, 1.0);
  return mix(b, a, h) - k * h * (1.0 - h);
}

Whenever one shape sits much closer than the other, h clamps to 0 or 1 and the formula reduces to ordinary min. Where both distances are nearly equal (h ≈ 0.5), the quadratic correction subtracts up to k/4, pulling the zero-isosurface outward to fill the fillet between them. The metaballs in part one achieved the same liquid merging by summing blobs into a blurred texture; here, a three-line formula handles it analytically at infinite resolution. Most signed-distance-field modeling comes down to assembling simple primitives and choosing the right blend radius k for every joint.

Here a stationary sphere and a rounded box stand 0.20 units apart while a smaller sphere orbits through both. Drag blend radius k down to 0 (min) and the orbiting sphere slices clean creases into the shapes. Around 0.22, it pulls smooth liquid necks out of whichever solid it grazes. Notice that the big sphere and the box remain separate until k crosses 0.40: bridging their 0.20 gap requires pulling each surface outward by 0.10, which only happens once the maximum pull k/4 exceeds 0.10.

Step 2 · Sphere tracingStepping by the distance

To render a pixel, the shader fires a camera ray into the scene and searches for its first intersection with map(p) = 0. Stepping forward by a tiny constant increment would take thousands of evaluations per ray and could still jump right through a thin feature. Knowing the true distance to the nearest surface gives a huge speedup: if map(p) returns d, then an open sphere of radius d around p is guaranteed free of geometry, so the ray can jump straight forward by d and query again.

John Hart formalized this algorithm as sphere tracing in 1996, proving the Lipschitz condition that guarantees safe steps. In WGSL, the entire intersection loop fits in five lines: 

for (var i = 0; i < MAX_STEPS; i++) {
  let h = map(ro + rd * tt);
  if (h.x < 0.001 * (1.0 + tt)) { hit = true; break; }  // close enough: a hit
  tt += h.x;                                            // jump by the safe radius
  if (tt > TMAX) { break; }                             // escaped into the sky
}

Scaling the hit threshold by 1.0 + tt relaxes precision far from the camera so distant rays stop once their error drops below a pixel. Out in open air the steps stay huge, then shrink rapidly as the ray closes in on a surface. Because step count tracks local geometry, rendering cost varies wildly across the image:

This heatmap colors every pixel of the hero scene by how many iterations its ray needed. Broad faces aimed toward the camera finish in a handful of steps (deep violet), while silhouette rims glow bright orange because rays skimming past an edge repeatedly receive tiny distances. The costliest strip in the whole frame is the floor near the horizon, where rays fly nearly parallel to the ground plane; at the default step budget of 48, those rays run out of iterations before touching the floor and get flagged in teal. Drag the budget lower to watch the teal band creep inward from the horizon and silhouettes, or crank it up toward 128 so nearly every ray finishes. Every production sphere tracer sets a cap like this along with a fallback (in the shaded modes here, a ray that misses the march falls back to an exact ray-plane intersection with the floor).

Aside · Demoscene roots

Sphere tracing flourished in the demoscene, where entire real-time animated shorts have to fit inside 4 or 64 kilobytes of executable code. Polygon meshes eat that budget immediately, whereas an analytic distance function takes a handful of bytes. Íñigo Quílez's 2008 talk "Rendering Worlds with Two Triangles" and his companion essays on SDF primitives, smooth minimums, and soft shadows popularized the craft worldwide, which is why so many Shadertoy shaders still name their scene function map.

Step 3 · ShadingNormals, shadows, and occlusion

Once a ray hits a surface, lighting requires a surface normal. Conveniently, the gradient of a distance field points straight along the direction of fastest distance increase, which is perpendicular to the surface. Evaluating map at four tiny offsets arranged on the vertices of a tetrahedron around the hit point gives an accurate normal with just four extra calls.

Casting a second ray from the hit point toward the key light handles shadows. Checking only whether the shadow ray collides with geometry gives crisp hard-edged shadows. To turn them soft without tracing a bundle of rays across an area light, you can track the tightest near miss along the shadow march: 

// h: distance to nearest surface · t: distance marched toward the light
res = min(res, k * h / t);
t += clamp(h, 0.012, 0.3);

If a shadow ray passes very close to an edge (small h) at distance t, the ratio h/t approximates the narrow angular clearance past the blocker as seen from the shaded point. Taking the minimum along the march darkens points sitting inside the penumbra, while the sharpness factor k tunes how tight that clearance needs to be before darkening kicks in: large k produces razor-sharp shadows, and small k spreads out a wide, cloudy penumbra mimicking a broad light source.

Ambient occlusion borrows the exact same distance trick to darken tucked-away crevices. Stepping outward along the surface normal five times and comparing how far we stepped (h) against what map reports as the true nearest distance reveals whether nearby geometry is crowding overhead: 

for (var i = 1; i <= 5; i++) {
  let h = 0.03 + 0.12 * f32(i);            // probe distance along the normal
  occ += (h - map(p + n * h).x) * sca;     // shortfall reveals nearby geometry
  sca *= 0.7;                              // weight closer probes more heavily
}
return clamp(1.0 - 1.6 * occ, 0.0, 1.0);

Pull shadow sharpness k down toward 2 to wash the sculpture's shadow into a soft twilight smudge, then push it up to 64 to snap the edge razor-sharp. Next, drag ambient occlusion down to off: even though the direct light and shadows stay untouched, the crease where the pedestal meets the floor and the inner folds of the copper blobs suddenly brighten until the sculpture looks pasted onto the concrete. Five quick calls to map per pixel restore all of that grounding contact.

Bright metallic copper hovers right above a pale stone pedestal, yet the stone never picks up a warm copper tint from it; the subtle warmth on downward-facing surfaces is just a constant fake bounce term I typed into the shader. EpilogueWhat got left behind

Analytic distance functions get a surprising amount of mileage out of a single function: one map(p) yields exact safe steps for ray intersections, four-tap surface normals, near-miss penumbras, and five-tap ambient occlusion, all running smoothly inside a fragment shader with zero geometry buffers. If I wanted to push sphere tracing further, I would look at domain repetition (tiling infinite geometry for the cost of a modulo), cone tracing to anti-alias edges, and over-relaxed stepping (Keinert et al., 2014), which dares to step slightly past the safe radius and backs up on overshoot to cut down those expensive grazing silhouette steps.

Stepping into 3D this way, however, quietly traded away the heart of the first three parts. Radiance cascades asked what every point in the scene could see from every direction, giving true bounced color and volumetric light. Sphere tracing only asks what the camera sees along one primary ray, forcing everything else (soft shadows, skylight, ambient fill, and contact darkness) back into clever local heuristics. Each approximation looks convincing on its own, yet side by side you can feel the missing indirect light.

All of those heuristics are trying to imitate a single physical law that James Kajiya wrote down in 1986 as the rendering equation: the light leaving any surface point equals what it emits plus all light arriving from every direction, scattered according to the material. In 3D we cannot easily tile the whole volume with radiance cascades, but we can solve Kajiya's integral directly by averaging random light paths.
