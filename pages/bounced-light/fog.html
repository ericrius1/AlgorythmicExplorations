Bounced Light · Part 3

The Fog

A low morning sun breaking through pines and ground mist. Those shafts between the trunks emerge wherever sunlit fog borders shadowed fog. Drag across the forest to puff extra smoke into the beams.

Scroll down to read ↓

In part two, every pixel in the lighting pass fell into one of two camps: either completely empty vacuum or an opaque wall. The smoke curling above the flames was painted on afterward during the display pass, so cascade rays flew straight through it without dimming or lighting it up. Real air sits in the messy middle ground. Suspended water droplets, dust, and smoke constantly siphon a small fraction of light out of every passing beam and deflect part of it off in new directions. Graphics papers call that a participating medium, meaning the space between objects actively modifies the light traveling through it.

Adding fog to our 2D radiance cascades keeps the jump-flood distance field, the probe hierarchy, and the top-down merge completely intact. All it takes is one extra texture describing the mist and a couple of bookkeeping lines tucked inside the ray march itself.

Step 1 · ExtinctionFog takes a fraction

Picture a ray slicing through mist of local density ρ. Over any short stretch Δs, droplets remove a fixed percentage of whatever radiance remains in the beam, either absorbing it as heat or knocking photons sideways out of the ray. Compounding a fixed percentage loss step after step produces an exponential decay known as the Beer–Lambert law: 

T *= exp(−σ · ρ · Δs)   // transmittance shrinks across each marched step

Because the cascade shader already marches rays forward in leaps governed by the distance field, tracking extinction only takes a running transmittance variable T initialized to 1. One small tweak keeps large open rooms accurate: since fog density can vary across empty air where the distance field would otherwise permit a giant leap, each step is capped at roughly a twelfth of its cascade interval so the march always samples the fog texture along the way. Whatever a ray ultimately gathers (a glowing lamp, a bounced wall, the cascade merge above it, or the open sky) gets multiplied by T. Better still, the cascade hierarchy composes transmittance automatically: each level dims only its own ring of distance, and multiplying by the parent level's transmittance during the merge accounts for everything farther out.

Pull the scatter slider all the way down to 0 to isolate pure extinction. Thickening the fog now darkens the entire room and softens the lamp's reach like peering through smoked glass, because light is being removed from rays without putting any of it back into the air. Dragging scatter back up brings out the bright glowing halo around the bulb.

Step 2 · In-scatteringWhere the light goes

When a beam loses energy inside mist, only a sliver turns into heat; most of those photons bounce off water droplets into fresh directions and join other rays crossing that same spot. From any passing ray's point of view, every illuminated patch of fog acts like a soft volumetric glow proportional to the light bathing it: 

J      = fluence_last_frame(x)            // incoming light bathing this spot in the fog
a      = 1 − exp(−σ · ρ · Δs)             // fraction extinguished across this step
inscat += T · J · scatter · a               // fraction scattered back along the ray
T      *= 1 − a

Conveniently, we already have an answer for J: the cascade-0 fluence computed one frame earlier. It is the exact same temporal feedback loop that gave us multi-bounce wall color in part two, now applied to the air itself. On frame one, direct sunlight illuminates the mist; on frame two, that glowing mist radiates light into neighboring pockets of fog, giving second-order scattering; frame three adds the third order, and so on. Keeping the scatter albedo at or below 1 guarantees the medium never invents energy, so the sum converges cleanly.

Glowing air does make every sampling imperfection far easier to spot than diffuse walls did. Because cascade 0 only fires four coarse directions per probe, a tiny emitter can leave subtle radial spokes in the halo, and reading last frame's fluence causes a rapidly whipped lamp to drag a faint glowing comet tail behind it. Rotating the ray angles each frame, blending over a short temporal history, and keeping the lamp generously sized keep the halo smooth and round.

Aside · Crepuscular rays

Those dramatic beams slicing through clouds or forest canopies are called crepuscular rays, from the Latin word for twilight. Nothing physically shoots extra light along the bright bands: sunlight floods all the mist it can reach, tree crowns or clouds carve dark shadow tunnels through that mist, and from the side you read the sharp contrast between lit and shadowed air as glowing shafts.

Here, the same directional sky from part two shines through two openings in the left wall. No geometry draws the beams; the rays passing through the slits light up the fog in their path while the wall shields the air above and below them. Lowering sun height swings the shafts steeper across the floor and warms the solar tint. Raising fog brightens the illuminated beams right up to the point where extinction starts dimming the far corner of the room, since the same density both scatters light toward you and blocks light behind it. Sweep your cursor across the dark room to inject puffs of smoke: each plume stays dark in shadow and flares warm gold the moment it drifts across a sunlit shaft.

Deep dive: the phase function I left out

The equations above assume isotropic scattering, sending deflected light out equally in all directions. Real mist droplets strongly favor forward scattering (deflecting most photons by just a few degrees), which is why staring toward a low sun through morning fog creates a blinding silver glare while looking away from the sun reveals dull gray mist. Graphics renderers typically model this with the Henyey–Greenstein phase function (published in 1941 for interstellar dust), controlled by a single asymmetry parameter g. Supporting directional scattering inside radiance cascades would require storing angular moments in the media texture rather than a single RGB brightness. In a 2D slice viewed orthogonally from outside the plane, forward glare matters far less than in 3D, so keeping scattering isotropic buys clean single-lookup performance; the extra halo around the sun disk in the hero backdrop is painted directly into the sky layer.

Step 3 · AssemblyThe forest

Putting the directional morning sky, solid pine silhouettes, and volumetric mist together yields the forest below. Ground mist falls off exponentially with altitude just like a thin real atmosphere, topped with lighter upper haze so the gaps between tree crowns catch beams from the low sun, while slow-drifting procedural noise keeps the density uneven. The pale row of distant trees is purely a backdrop layer behind the lighting plane.

Adjusting mist height lifts or lowers the dense ground bank, while view: final cycles through the diagnostic buffers including a new fog density view showing the raw media texture (the stratified mist bank, drifting noise, and any smoke puffs you breathe in with your cursor). Switching back and forth between fog density and final separates what I authored from what the cascades solved: a single uniform band of mist turns bright gold where sunlight slips between two pines and drops to cool gray a trunk width away.

Wrap-upLeaving the plane

Our 2D cascade renderer now handles arbitrary numbers of moving emitters, colored multi-bounce walls, an environment sky, and air that simultaneously absorbs and scatters light, all built by adding a couple of lines inside the ray march and reusing last frame's fluence one more time. If I kept extending this 2D world, I would store a few directional coefficients in the fog texture for forward-peaked glare, give the mist wavelength-dependent absorption so thick fog shifts hue with distance, and hook real light-blocking smoke onto part two's bonfire.

At this point, though, the real bottleneck is living inside a two-dimensional slice. Every scene across these first three parts has been a flat floor plan or vertical cross-section viewed from outside. Porting this exact texture-based strategy to 3D means replacing 2D textures with 3D voxel volumes, cubing memory consumption and jump-flood rebuild costs overnight. So in part four I am stepping into 3D by dropping the stored distance texture altogether and writing the distance field as a pure math function evaluated directly by every ray.
