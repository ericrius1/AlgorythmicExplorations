Bounced Light · Part 3

The Fog

A low sun, a stand of pines, and mist. Nothing draws the shafts of light between the trees: they're fog the sun reaches, next to fog it doesn't. Move your cursor through the scene to breathe in some smoke.

Scroll down to read ↓

In part two a ray either travelled freely or stopped dead. There was nothing in between: no haze, no glow caught in smoke, no shafts through the trees. Real air is the in-between. Every metre of fog takes a little of the light passing through it, and everything in this part follows from modelling that. The rendering term is participating media: any volume that does something to the light going through it.

The pipeline stays the same as in parts one and two: distance field, cascades, merge. Fog arrives as one new texture and a few lines inside the ray march: absorption and scattering become two new terms in the sum every ray was already doing.

Step 1 · ExtinctionFog takes a fraction

Send a ray through fog of density ρ. Over each short stretch Δs, a fixed fraction of whatever light is left gets absorbed or knocked off course. A fixed fraction per step adds up to a decaying exponential, which is the Beer–Lambert law: 

T *= exp(−σ · ρ · Δs)   // each segment keeps a fraction of the light

The march from part one needs very little else. Each ray already moves through the scene in leaps sized by the distance field. Now every leap also samples the fog texture and multiplies a running transmittance T down. Fog varies smoothly, so I cap each leap at about a twelfth of the cascade's interval, which keeps the fog sampled even across open space. Whatever the ray ends up with (a hot surface, the merge with the cascade above, the sky) arrives dimmed by the fog it crossed. The hierarchy composes on its own: each cascade dims only its own interval, and the merge multiplies in the rest.

Slide scatter to zero first. That's extinction alone. Thicken the fog and the room just gets darker, and the lamp's shadow goes soft and murky. It looks like smoked glass. Real fog around a lamp glows. The light taken out of a beam has to go somewhere, and so far I've only removed it.

Step 2 · In-scatteringWhere the light goes

Light that fog takes out of a beam meets one of two fates. Some is absorbed and becomes heat. In fog, most of it is scattered: bounced off a droplet into a new direction, where it becomes part of some other ray. So every bit of lit fog is a faint light, giving back a share of the light it sits in. To make fog glow, a ray has to pick up that share from every bit of fog it crosses: 

J      = fluence_last_frame(x)            // how much light this bit of fog sits in
a      = 1 − exp(−σ · ρ · Δs)             // the fraction this segment takes out
inscat += T · J · scatter · a               // …and the same fraction of J it gives back
T      *= 1 − a

The first line is the question the cascades already answer: how much light reaches this point? They answered it one frame ago. That's part two's frame loop again, aimed at the air instead of the walls. The fog's glow is last frame's light, the cascades carry that glow around, and next frame the fog glows with the result. Surfaces that re-emit light gave multiple bounces for one texture read. Fog that re-emits light gives multiple scattering at the same price, one more order per frame. Scatter is the fraction of the stolen light that comes back out, so at 1 or below the fog never makes light out of nothing.

Look closely at the halo with scatter turned up and you can see the bookkeeping. A small bright source is hard for the coarse cascade-0 directions, and the glow is last frame's light, so a lamp that moves fast leaves a faint tail. Glowing fog magnifies details that were invisible when only walls were lit. Rotating the ray directions every frame and averaging the last few frames is what keeps the halo round, and the lamp stays fat on purpose.

Aside · Crepuscular rays

Shafts like these are called crepuscular rays, from the Latin for twilight. Nothing is emitted along the beam. The sun lights all the fog it can reach, something in the way keeps it from some of the fog, and what you see is the boundary between lit air and unlit air. A light shaft is a shadow seen from the side.

Sunlight through two slits in a wall. Nothing here draws the shafts. The sky from part two comes in through the gaps, the fog it reaches glows by the formula above, and the fog in the wall's shadow stays dark. Lower the sun and the shafts swing down and turn warmer. Thicken the fog and they get brighter while the room behind them gets darker: the same σ does both, because the fog that scatters light toward you is also dimming everything else. Blow smoke with your cursor and a puff lights up only while it's inside a shaft.

Deep dive: the phase function I left out

This fog scatters equally in all directions, an isotropic phase function. Real droplets mostly deflect light by a few degrees, which is why looking toward a low sun through mist is blinding and looking away is just grey. The standard model is the Henyey–Greenstein function, published in 1941 for dust between the stars, with one parameter g for how strongly scattering favours the forward direction. The cascades could carry it, but the glow texture would have to store direction as well as brightness. In a flat world seen from outside the plane it buys very little, and the isotropic version keeps the glow to one texture read. The glare around the sun in the hero is painted into the sky backdrop for that reason.

Step 3 · AssemblyThe forest

Everything together. The morning sky is the top cascade's merge, as in part two. The mist is densest at the ground and thins with height (one exponential, as in a real atmosphere), with a thinner haze above it so the gaps between the crowns have something for the sun to light. A slowly drifting noise field keeps it patchy. The far row of pines is backdrop only: it's drawn, but the light never sees it.

The views have one new entry. Fog density shows the media texture itself: the mist bank, your smoke puffs, the clearer air above. Flip between it and final and you can see who does what. The density is made up by me, the glow is computed, and the same bank of mist is gold where the sun reaches it and grey a couple of trunks over.

Wrap-upLeaving the plane

The renderer now has lights in any number, things that block them, coloured multi-bounce, a sky, and air that absorbs and scatters. In two dimensions, that's most of what light does.

  • Fog is a new term, not a new pass. Extinction and in-scattering are two more lines in the march every ray was already doing.
  • The frame loop keeps paying off. Last frame's light gave bounce in part two and gives multiple scattering here, for one texture read each.
  • Light shafts are shadows. They come from lit fog next to unlit fog, so they appear wherever something blocks the sun.

Things I'd try next: a phase function stored in a few directional terms, so mist toward the sun glares and mist away from it doesn't; coloured fog that absorbs some wavelengths more than others; and smoke from part two's fire that blocks its own light. The bigger limit is the plane itself. Every scene so far is a floor plan, and the camera floats outside it. Doing 3D the obvious way, with a voxel distance field and cascades over a volume, multiplies every texture in this series by a third axis. Part four takes a different route: the distance field stops being something stored in a texture and becomes a function the rays evaluate.
