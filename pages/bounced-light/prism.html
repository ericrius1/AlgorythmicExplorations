Bounced Light · Part 6

The Prism

A narrow slit of white light entering a clear glass prism. There are no colored materials anywhere in this scene: every hue on the floor emerges because each traced path picks a single wavelength and bends by that wavelength's refractive index.

Scroll down to read ↓

Hold a clear triangular prism in a beam of white sunlight and a bright rainbow fans out across the floor, even though both the light source and the glass are completely colorless. Up through part five, every surface in this series was opaque, and every ray carried color as three numbers (red, green, and blue). Transparent glass breaks that shortcut immediately: light slows down inside glass, turns sharply at each boundary, and turns by a slightly different angle for every wavelength.

In the hero scene above, a white beam spreads by 0.27° (roughly the angular radius of the sun) through lightly hazy air onto a tall glass prism standing on a dark floor. Adding refraction, wavelength-dependent dispersion, light-traced caustics, and a finite camera lens to part five's path tracer is enough to produce the visible beam, the internal reflections, and the full spectrum painted across the ground.

Step 1 · BendingSnell's law, per wavelength

When light crosses from air into glass at an angle, the drop in speed bends its trajectory according to Snell's law, n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, where n is the refractive index of each medium. Real glass has a higher index for shorter wavelengths than for longer ones, so violet light bends more steeply than red. Augustin-Louis Cauchy wrote down a tidy empirical fit for this in 1836 (with wavelength λ in micrometers): 

n(λ) = A + B / λ²      // A = 1.5 here · B controls how strongly colors fan out

Typical window or crown glass sits around B ≈ 0.004 µm², while dense flint glass made for optical prisms runs closer to 0.013–0.016. Because white light contains every visible wavelength simultaneously, passing it through a prism separates each wavelength along its own exit angle. Below is a 2D optics bench tracing 64 distinct wavelengths from 380 to 720 nm through an equilateral prism, each bent by its own Cauchy index.

Drag or hover over the canvas to move the incoming beam (vertically to shift it, horizontally to tilt its entry angle). Pull dispersion B down to 0.000 and the prism still deflects the beam cleanly, yet every wavelength turns identically so the beam stays white; slide it back up and the exit ray opens into a spectrum, while the readout below reports the glass class, middle deflection angle, and full red-to-violet spread directly from the traced rays.

Then rotate prism rotation into negative angles. Inside the glass, the rays strike the far face at a shallower grazing angle until Snell's law runs out of real solutions and the glass face turns into a perfect mirror, trapping the light inside. This is total internal reflection, the same principle that guides pulses down fiber-optic cables. Because violet light has the highest refractive index, it hits the critical angle first: from the starting beam position at B = 0.016, rotating to −2.0° traps 11% of the spectrum, −6.0° traps 75%, and by −7.0° every wavelength bounces off the second face and exits elsewhere.

Aside · Two prisms

In 1672 (and later in Opticks, 1704), Isaac Newton settled an old debate about whether glass dyed white light by adding a second prism behind a slotted board. Letting only a single narrow color from the first fan pass into the second prism bent that ray again without splitting or recoloring it, demonstrating that the colors were already present in white light and that the prism merely sorted them by refrangibility.

Although the sine law of refraction bears Willebrord Snellius's name (1621) and appeared in print under René Descartes in 1637, the Persian mathematician Ibn Sahl had already stated the geometric law centuries earlier, around 984, in a treatise on burning mirrors and lenses.

Step 2 · Glass in the tracerReflect or refract

Whenever a ray hits glass in 3D, part of its energy reflects off the surface and the remainder refracts inside. The Fresnel equations give the exact split, and graphics shaders usually compute Christophe Schlick's 1994 approximation: roughly 4% reflected head-on, climbing smoothly to 100% at grazing angles. Splitting every ray into both reflected and refracted children would double the work at each glass face, so a Monte Carlo path tracer rolls the dice, follows a single branch, and scales the path weight to keep the average unbiased: 

let F = schlick(cosI, etaDecide, iorDecide);          // physical reflection fraction
let P = select(F, clamp(F, 0.25, 0.75), camPath && F < 1.0);
if (rnd() < P) {
  (*sp).weight *= F / P;                              // chose reflection
  return reflect(rd, n);
}
(*sp).weight *= (1.0 - F) / (1.0 - P);                // chose refraction

Sampling reflection with probability F directly leaves head-on studio reflections noisy for thousands of frames because only 4% of camera rays take that branch. Clamping the camera branch probability to at least 25% and weighting by F / P trades a tiny bit of refraction speed for much cleaner surface highlights.

Drop a clear glass sphere into a tight beam of light and it acts as a thick lens, concentrating rays into a blazing bright caustic inside its own shadow. Ordinary camera-driven path tracing struggles terribly to render that spot: a ray starting from the camera has to hit the floor, bounce randomly upward into the glass ball, refract twice along just the right curve, and emerge aimed back into the tiny 0.25°–1.5° cone of the light source. Because almost no camera paths succeed, the caustic dissolves into sparse bright fireflies.

With light tracing: off, you see the bright elliptical beam on the floor, the sphere's dark shadow inside it, and a dusting of lucky camera-path sparks right where the focused spot belongs. Click the button to turn light tracing: on and a dedicated compute shader starts launching between 65,000 and one million rays per frame from the light source instead, steering them through the glass ball onto the floor and atomically splatting their radiance straight into whichever camera pixel sees that floor point. Starting from the light end makes finding the caustic trivial, so the focal ring fills in smoothly within a couple of seconds. This demo leaves denoising off so both techniques show up raw, while carefully partitioning path types (light tracing handles beam-through-glass-to-floor paths, camera tracing handles everything else) so nothing gets double-counted.

Widening beam spread softens the caustic just as widening the ceiling lamp softened shadows in part five, because the sphere is projecting an image of a broader light cone; with light tracing off, a wider beam also makes lucky camera hits more frequent. Turning up dispersion B tints the caustic rim subtly where different wavelengths focus at slightly different depths, though most colors still overlap across the bright core.

Aside · Tracing from the light

James Arvo introduced tracing paths forward from light sources to capture caustics in 1986, calling it backward ray tracing. In the early 1990s, Eric Lafortune and Yves Willems (1993) and Eric Veach and Leonidas Guibas (1994) generalized this into bidirectional path tracing, joining subpaths grown from both the camera and the lights; Henrik Wann Jensen's photon mapping (1996) took another route by caching light-traced hits in a spatial structure queried later by camera rays. The light tracer here is the stripped-down core of that idea, linking floor hits directly back to the camera lens.

Step 3 · One wavelength per pathSpectral sampling

Even with refraction and light tracing working, an RGB path tracer still hits a wall the moment dispersion enters the picture: a single ray carrying white RGB has no well-defined refractive index because red, green, and blue all need to head in different directions.

Monte Carlo offers a clean escape. A pixel's visible color is already an integral over the visible spectrum, so wavelength can be sampled alongside bounce directions. Whenever a path hits dispersive glass for the first time, it locks onto a single wavelength λ between 380 and 720 nm, refracts strictly by n(λ) from that point onward, and converts its final accumulated energy back into linear RGB using the human eye's color-matching response: 

if (!(*sp).locked && PP.optics.x > 0.0) {     // first encounter with dispersive glass
  (*sp).locked = true;
  (*sp).lambda = 380.0 + (*sp).u * 340.0;       // sample one wavelength, 380–720 nm
  ior = iorAt((*sp).lambda);                    // Cauchy index for this λ
}
...
return wavelengthRGB(sp.lambda) * sp.weight;    // convert single λ back to RGB

Inside wavelengthRGB, the shader evaluates the piecewise-Gaussian fits to the CIE 1931 color-matching functions published by Chris Wyman, Peter-Pike Sloan, and Peter Shirley in 2013, then transforms CIE XYZ into linear sRGB. Pure monochromatic wavelengths sit outside the sRGB gamut triangle, producing negative channels; rather than hard-clipping those negatives to zero (which shifts cyan and yellow hues), it desaturates out-of-gamut colors by adding just enough white to lift the smallest channel to zero, then normalizes the curves so a flat spectrum from 380 to 720 nm averages cleanly to (1, 1, 1). Paths that never touch dispersive glass bypass spectral locking altogether and stay plain white, adding zero overhead to opaque reflections.

Left to raw pseudo-random rolls, single-wavelength sampling suffers from stubborn chromatic speckle. To tame it, each pixel advances its wavelength parameter u across frames in golden-ratio steps offset by a 64×64 blue-noise tile, and each frame's light-tracing batch sweeps across the spectrum the same way. Even the first dozen samples per pixel span violet through red fairly evenly.

The three plotted curves show the linear RGB weights produced by wavelengthRGB across 380–720 nm, while each vertical line marks one randomly drawn wavelength and the right-hand swatch displays their mean. Drag wavelengths averaged from a handful up to 4,096 (or click roll again): at 16 samples the swatch tilts strongly toward whichever hues got lucky, at 100 it still carries a subtle cast, and by 4,096 every channel lands within a few hundredths of 1 · 1 · 1 white, obeying the same 1/√N law from part five. Every pixel in the spectral path tracer is running that exact average while sending each wavelength along its own bent route through the prism: where all wavelengths recombine, the floor averages back to white, and where the glass fans them apart, each spot receives only a slice of the spectrum and glows with pure color.

Step 4 · AssemblyPrism, ball, and lens

The final scene lines up both the prism and the glass ball inside one beam and swaps the pinhole camera for a thin lens. A pinhole shoots every pixel's rays from a single point, keeping the entire world sharply in focus at once. Give the lens a finite aperture, start each camera ray from a random point across that disk, and steer it toward the focal plane at focus distance: points resting on the focal plane still converge cleanly, while anything closer or farther spreads out into soft optical bokeh. Rob Cook, Thomas Porter, and Loren Carpenter unified depth of field, glossy blur, and soft area shadows with this exact distributed sampling idea in 1984.

Drag horizontally across the canvas to sweep the beam around the prism, or vertically to tilt its elevation. Steering the dispersed fan through the glass ball splits its bright caustic into overlapping colored arcs across the floor. The camera starts focused on the prism's top face; widen aperture (or drop it to pinhole) to control depth of field, then push focus distance roughly one unit farther back to bring the glass ball into crisp focus while the prism softens in the foreground. Widening beam spread softens the spectral bands from the illumination side instead, overlapping neighboring wavelengths across the floor.

Two extra touches polish this view and the hero render above. First, to reveal the beams slicing through air, every straight segment of a light-traced ray scatters a small fraction of its energy toward the camera from one random point along its flight (a lightweight single-scattering cousin of part three's volumetric fog, without attenuation). Second, because a single dispersive sample hitting glass arrives as an intense monochromatic spike, three temporary guardrails quiet early frames without corrupting the final answer: bright single-wavelength samples are capped by a ceiling that grows with √N so it relaxes completely as samples accumulate; a neighborhood clamp pulls lone outlier pixels into the YCoCg luminance and chroma bounds of their eight neighbors until roughly 1,000 samples per pixel; and the same edge-aware bilateral filter from part five (extended with a gentle hue guard so adjacent rainbow bands never bleed together) tapers away and shuts off at 1,200 samples.

EpilogueSix ways of moving light

Across all six essays, the real lesson was how many optical phenomena collapse into a few clean sampling patterns. Every tricky fork in the road (reflect versus refract, which wavelength to trace, where on the camera aperture to launch a ray) turns into one random draw paired with a weight. When a narrow path is nearly impossible to discover from the camera, launching rays from the light end turns a storm of sparks into a crisp caustic. And treating color itself as another sampled dimension lets a prism split white sunlight into a full spectrum without slowing down opaque paths at all. Pushing this spectral engine further would mean upgrading to hero-wavelength sampling (carrying four wavelengths per ray until dispersive glass forces them to branch), swapping Cauchy's fit for Sellmeier glass tables, and animating a rippling water surface to cast dancing pool caustics.

That brings Bounced Light full circle. The series began inside a 2D lava lamp where radiance cascades let every pixel gather global illumination by matching spatial and angular resolution to distance, then stretched that 2D hierarchy to thousands of bonfire embers, multi-bounce rooms, and sunlit volumetric fog. Stepping out of flatland in part four traded cascades for analytic signed distance functions and fast local shading heuristics, part five restored physically accurate 3D bounces through Monte Carlo path tracing, and here glass, caustics, and wavelength joined the same loop. Six distinct algorithms, all answering one underlying question (what light reaches this spot?), and all running live inside your browser tab.
