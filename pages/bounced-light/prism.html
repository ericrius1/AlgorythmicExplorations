Bounced Light · Part 6

The Prism

A slit of white light through a glass prism. Nothing in the scene has a colour. The rainbow on the floor comes from tracing each path at one random wavelength and bending it by that wavelength's index.

Scroll down to read ↓

Everything in this series so far has been opaque. Light bounced off things, but it never went into them. Rainbows, the bright wobbling lines on the bottom of a pool and a magnifying glass focusing the sun all happen inside transparent material. This last part adds one new event to part five's path tracer, refraction, and then deals with something every earlier part simplified away: light has a wavelength, and glass bends each wavelength by a different amount.

The scene at the top is a prism standing on a dark floor, with a narrow slot of white light aimed at it. The beam's direction spreads by 0.27°, about the angular radius of the sun. The air has a thin haze in it, so you can see the white beam coming in from the left and the fan of colour leaving the prism, and where the fan comes down it paints a rainbow on the floor. Every colour in the image comes out of the glass.

Step 1 · BendingSnell's law, per wavelength

Light slows down in glass, and when it crosses the surface at an angle it turns. The ratio of speeds is the refractive index n, and the turn follows Snell's law, n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. For the rest of this page the important part is that n isn't one number. It's larger for short wavelengths, so blue bends more than red. A simple fit for that is Cauchy's from 1836, with λ in micrometres: 

n(λ) = A + B / λ²      // A = 1.5 here · B sets how much the colours separate

Real crown glass has B around 0.004 µm², and dense flint glass, the kind used for prisms, around 0.013 to 0.016. White light is every visible wavelength at once, so a prism sends each one off at a slightly different angle. The figure below traces 64 wavelengths from 380 to 720 nm through a 2D prism, each with its own n.

Move the pointer over the figure, or touch it, to move the beam: up and down shifts it, left and right tilts it. Drag dispersion to zero and the prism still bends the beam, but every wavelength bends alike and it stays white. Raise it and the beam opens into a fan. The readout measures both from the traced rays: how far the middle of the fan turned, and the angle between its red and violet edges.

Now rotate the prism toward negative angles. The light inside starts meeting the second face at a steeper angle, and past a limit it can't get out. Snell's law has no solution there, so the light reflects back into the glass instead. That's total internal reflection, the same effect that keeps light inside an optical fibre. The violet end goes first, since it has the highest index: at −2° the readout says 11% of the beam is trapped, at −6° it's 75%, and by −7° nothing gets out of the second face at all.

Aside · Two prisms

Newton's famous prism experiment used a second prism. He let the fan from the first one fall on a board with a small hole, so only one colour got through, and sent that colour through another prism. It bent again but didn't spread or change colour. So the glass wasn't adding colour to white light. The colours were already in it, and the prism only separated them. He described this in a 1672 letter to the Royal Society and again in Opticks in 1704.

The law of refraction is usually credited to Willebrord Snellius in 1621, and Descartes published it in 1637. The earliest known statement of it is older: the Persian mathematician Ibn Sahl described it around 984 in a treatise on burning mirrors and lenses.

Step 2 · Glass in the tracerReflect or refract

At a glass surface some light reflects and the rest goes through. The split comes from the Fresnel equations. Graphics usually uses Christophe Schlick's 1994 approximation, which is about 4% reflected head-on, rising to all of it at grazing angles. A renderer that traced both branches every time would double its work at every surface. A Monte Carlo path tracer picks one branch at random and weights the result so the average comes out right: 

let F = schlick(cosI, etaDecide, iorDecide);          // the fraction that reflects
let P = select(F, clamp(F, 0.25, 0.75), camPath && F < 1.0);
if (rnd() < P) {
  (*sp).weight *= F / P;                              // picked reflection
  return reflect(rd, n);
}
(*sp).weight *= (1.0 - F) / (1.0 - P);                // picked refraction

The obvious choice is to reflect with probability F. Camera paths reflect at least a quarter of the time instead, and the weights correct for it. The faint reflection of the studio lights in the glass is only 4% of the light, and picked 4% of the time it stayed grainy for thousands of samples.

A glass ball in a beam of light makes a caustic: the ball acts as a lens and focuses the beam into a bright spot on the floor. Part five's tracer can find it in principle. A camera path has to land on the floor, happen to bounce up into the ball, refract twice, and come out pointing back along the beam to within its spread. That's rare, so the caustic shows up as scattered sparks that take a very long time to average out.

The bright ellipse is the beam landing on the floor, the dark hole in it is the ball's shadow, and the sparks inside the shadow are the camera paths that found the caustic. Turn on light tracing and a compute pass starts paths from the light instead, from 65,000 to a million per frame depending on how fast your GPU is. Each one goes through the ball, lands on the floor and is projected straight into the pixel that sees that spot. From that end the caustic is easy, and it fills in within a second or two. This figure shows the raw average with no filtering, so you're seeing both methods as they are. The camera keeps everything else, so no light is counted twice.

Beam spread works like the lamp size in part five. A wider beam blurs the caustic, because the ball is focusing an image of a bigger source. With light tracing off, a wider beam is also easier for camera paths to find, so the sparks get denser. Raising the dispersion only adds a faint coloured fringe. Each wavelength focuses at a slightly different depth, but on the floor the colours still overlap almost everywhere.

Aside · Tracing from the light

James Arvo proposed tracing rays from the light to catch caustics in 1986, calling it backward ray tracing. Bidirectional path tracing (Eric Lafortune and Yves Willems in 1993, Eric Veach and Leonidas Guibas in 1994) connects paths started from both ends, and Henrik Wann Jensen's photon mapping (1996) stores light paths in a map that the camera looks up later. The light tracing here is the simplest version: light paths that end on the floor connect straight to the camera.

Step 3 · One wavelength per pathSpectral sampling

Every renderer in this series has carried colour as three numbers, red, green and blue. Glass with dispersion breaks that. A path carrying "white" has no single direction to refract in, since every wavelength in it wants to go a different way.

The fix is more Monte Carlo. A pixel's colour is an integral over wavelengths as well as over paths, so sample that dimension too. When a path first refracts through dispersive glass, it picks one wavelength, bends by that wavelength's index from then on, and at the end it's turned into a colour by the eye's response to that wavelength: 

if (!(*sp).locked && PP.optics.x > 0.0) {     // the first dispersive refraction
  (*sp).locked = true;
  (*sp).lambda = 380.0 + (*sp).u * 340.0;       // one wavelength, 380–720 nm
  ior = iorAt((*sp).lambda);                    // Cauchy, for this wavelength
}
...
return wavelengthRGB(sp.lambda) * sp.weight;    // the eye's response to it

wavelengthRGB is the CIE 1931 colour matching functions, using the piecewise Gaussian fits Chris Wyman, Peter-Pike Sloan and Peter Shirley published in 2013, converted to linear sRGB. Pure spectral colours are more saturated than a screen can show, so where a channel comes out negative I add white until it doesn't, instead of cutting it off at zero; that keeps the cyan and yellow in the rainbow. Then I scaled it so a flat spectrum from 380 to 720 nm averages to white. Paths that never touch dispersive glass skip all this and stay white. That means floors and walls cost nothing extra, and only light that went through the glass picks up colour.

u is the random number that picks the wavelength, and it isn't fully random. A pixel's samples step through the spectrum by the golden ratio, starting from a blue-noise value for that pixel, so even its first dozen samples cover red to violet fairly evenly. Each frame's batch of light paths sweeps the spectrum the same way. Colour noise is the slowest kind to average out in a spectral renderer, and this removes most of it.

The curves are how much red, green and blue each wavelength contributes. Each vertical line is one random wavelength, and the swatch is their average. With a handful of samples the swatch is some random tint. At a hundred it's still visibly off, and at 4,096 the readout is within a few hundredths of 1 · 1 · 1 on every channel. It's the same 1/√N convergence as part five's noise figure. That's what every pixel in the hero is doing, except each sample also took its own path through the glass. Where all the wavelengths land in the same place they average back to white. Where the prism has sent them to different places, each spot only gets some of the wavelengths, and you see colour.

Step 4 · AssemblyPrism, ball and lens

The last figure puts the prism and a glass ball in one beam and adds a camera lens. A pinhole camera sends every ray from one point. A real lens has an area: rays start from random points on the lens and are aimed so they all meet at the focus distance. Things at that distance come out sharp and everything nearer or farther blurs. It's the same move that turned a point light into an area light in part five, applied to the camera. Rob Cook, Thomas Porter and Loren Carpenter added soft shadows, glossy blur and depth of field to ray tracing this way in 1984.

Drag sideways to swing the beam around the prism, and up and down to steepen or flatten it. The fan swings across the floor, and where it passes through the ball you get a coloured caustic. The default focus is on the top of the prism. Open the aperture wider and the ball and the rainbow behind it blur. Push the focus about one unit further and the ball sharpens while the prism blurs. The floor caustic sits at its own depth, so it comes into focus at its own setting. Beam spread blurs the rainbow from the other end, the same way it blurred the caustic.

Every blur in this image is an average over something with a size: the beam's spread, the lens's area, the range of wavelengths. Part five made the same point about lamps and rough mirrors.

Two more things are going on here and in the hero. The haze is drawn by the light tracer: every straight piece of a light path also scatters a little light toward the camera from one random point along it, with no dimming, which is a single-scattering version of part three's fog. And while the sum is young, three things keep the glass from sparkling. Light from the studio or the beam that reaches the camera through dispersive glass arrives at one wavelength, so a single sample can be a bright pure red or green; those samples are capped, and the cap grows with the square root of the samples already in the sum, so it lets go as the image converges. Then any pixel that falls outside the range of its eight neighbours is clamped into that range, with brightness and hue (in YCoCg) clamped separately, so a lone coloured pixel takes its neighbours' colour. Last comes the same edge-aware filter as part five's hero, with a loose hue test so neighbouring bands of the rainbow never blend. The clamp fades out by about 1,000 samples per pixel and the filter switches off at 1,200.

EpilogueSix ways of moving light

  • Random choices can replace branching. Reflect or refract, which wavelength, where on the lens: each is one random number and a weight.
  • Some light is easy from one end and hard from the other. The caustic that a camera path almost never finds is easy for a path that starts at the light.
  • Colour is an integral too. Treating wavelength as one more sampled dimension costs almost nothing for paths that never meet dispersive glass.

Things I'd try next: more than one wavelength per path (hero wavelength sampling, which traces a few at once and only splits them at the glass), Sellmeier's dispersion formula with real glass data instead of Cauchy's, and water with a moving surface for the pool-floor caustics.

That's the end of Bounced Light. It started with a lava lamp in 2D, lit by radiance cascades: a hierarchy of probes that let every pixel see the whole scene by being careful about what it didn't need to resolve. The bonfire and the fog pushed that hierarchy to thousands of lights and to light scattering through a medium. Part four left 2D and traded the hierarchy for distance-field heuristics, which made 3D cheap but gave up bounced light. Part five brought bounced light back with random sampling, paid for in noise and patience, and this part added glass and wavelength to the same loop. Each of them is a different way of answering "what does this point see?", and all of them ran in this browser tab.
