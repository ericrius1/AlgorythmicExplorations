Bounced Light · Part 1

The Lava Lamp

Wax that heats, rises, cools and sinks, lit only by itself. The lamp above is a particle fluid with a temperature on every particle, and its glow is real 2D light transport. Stir it with your cursor.

Scroll down to read ↓

In the Particle Worlds series I simulated a lot of matter: gravity, sand, water, dust, a slice of the cosmic web, and a fluid you can play like an instrument. I never simulated light. Every pixel in those six parts was a particle carrying its own colour, added on top of its neighbours and then colour graded. No particle ever lit another one. The core of the galaxy in part one cast no glow on its own arms.

This series is about light, and I wanted to start small: one lamp, one room, two dimensions. A lava lamp is a good first subject because it's two problems stuck together. How the wax moves is a heat problem: heat goes in at the coil, comes out near the cap, and buoyancy does the rest. How it looks is a light problem. The glow around a rising blob, the warm wash on the cold wax above it, and the shadow a sinking blob drags across the glass all come from light leaving one place and landing somewhere else. So the demo simulates both.

Step 1 · One number per particleWax with a temperature

The fluid is the same smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH) I built for water in Sorting Water. Particles are sorted into a grid, each one sums up its neighbours to get a density, and pressure pushes back wherever the crowd is denser than it wants to be. The one addition is a temperature on every particle. The neighbour loop that already sums density also lets heat flow between particles that touch: 

rho   += w·w              // density, as before
tFlux += (T_j − T_i)·w    // conduction: heat flows along the same kernel

Particles near the coil warm up, hottest over the middle of it. Everything loses a little heat all the time, and a lot of it near the top of the vessel. What makes this a lava lamp and not a pot of soup is that temperature feeds back into the motion in two places: 

rest_i = ρ₀ · (1 − β·T_i)        // thermal expansion: hot wax wants more room
acc.y += buoyancy·T_i − gravity   // the water I don't simulate lifts what expanded

The first line is a cheap stand-in for thermal expansion. A warmer particle is happy with fewer neighbours, so a warm patch pushes outward and takes up more room. Blobs swell as they heat, as real wax does. The second line is a bigger shortcut. In a real lamp the wax floats in water, and buoyancy is the water reacting to the wax's density. I don't simulate the water, so a force proportional to temperature does its job. Cold wax sinks and hot wax rises. The water's other job, drag, is a damping term on every particle's velocity, and it's what keeps the motion slow.

One more force keeps the wax in one piece. Pressure only cares about crowding, not about whether the fluid stays together, so a plume driven by pressure and buoyancy alone tears into ragged streamers. Real wax holds together because it's one liquid sitting in another, and the boundary between them costs energy. I copy that with a cohesion force between pairs of particles: weakly attractive at middle distances, repulsive up close. That's surface tension in particle form. Necks pinch off, small blobs get absorbed by big ones, and what's left is round. The lamp near the end of this page has a slider for it, and at zero the blobs fray.

Give it a few seconds. Heat builds up in the pool on the floor until a patch has expanded enough to lift off. It cools on the way up, and once its buoyancy drops below gravity it turns around and sinks. Nothing in the code says "circulate". The cycle comes out of four numbers, one per slider: heat in, lift per degree, swelling per degree, and heat out.

Step 2 · Particles become a surfaceMetaballs

Particles are a good way for wax to move and a bad way for it to look. Wax is one continuous thing. The standard fix is an implicit surface: every particle stamps a smooth round bump into a low-resolution field texture, the bumps add up, and everything above a threshold counts as inside. Two particles drifting toward each other merge into one rounded shape before they touch, which is the look real wax has and dots never will.

9,000 small bumps added together are lumpy at the scale of one particle, and a hard threshold turns every lump into a scallop on the edge of the wax. So before anything reads the field, I blur it with a small Gaussian (two passes, nine taps each). Without it the wax looks like cotton wool.

Aside · Blobby molecules

Jim Blinn introduced the idea in 1982 to draw electron-density surfaces around molecules, and called them blobby molecules. Everyone since has called them metaballs.

The splat carries temperature too, as a second channel, so the surface pass knows the average temperature of the wax at every point. That number decides what the wax is, as far as light is concerned: 

emission  = palette(T) · luminance(T)  // cold: near-black plum … hot: bright amber
occlusion = 1 inside the wax           // hot or cold, wax blocks light

Cold wax is an occluder. It's almost black, and you see it by what it blocks. Hot wax is a light. The same blob is both at different points in its life, so the lights in this scene move, split in two, and cast shadows on each other. A glow sprite pasted on each blob can't do that. For that I need to move light around the scene, which nothing in the earlier series did.

Step 3 · Light transportRadiance cascades

Here's the problem. For every pixel I want the light arriving from every direction, with shadows, in real time. The brute-force answer is to march a few hundred rays out of every pixel, every frame. That's the n² problem of light transport, and it's far too slow. One observation about how light behaves makes it cheap.

It's called the penumbra condition. The further away a light is, the more slowly its contribution changes as you move around, but the more finely you need to know which direction it's in. Nearby light needs probes packed close together and only a few directions each. Distant light needs lots of directions and hardly any probes. One grid of probes can't do both, so radiance cascades use a stack of them: 

cascade 0:  probes every 2 px,   4 rays each,  covering   0 →   4 px
cascade 1:  probes every 4 px,  16 rays each,  covering   4 →  20 px
cascade 2:  probes every 8 px,  64 rays each,  covering  20 →  84 px
// each level: ¼ as many probes × 4 times the rays, so every cascade costs the same

Each cascade only traces a ring: short ray segments that start where the level below gave up. Scroll through one probe doing it:

The levels are then merged from the top down. When one of a cascade's rays hits nothing, it takes the light the next level up found in the same direction, interpolated between the four nearest coarser probes. Everything ends up in cascade 0, which holds, for every 2×2 block of the light grid, the light arriving from the whole canvas. There's no random sampling anywhere, so there's no speckle to average away.

Three refinements are worth having, the first from people who have worked on this since Sannikov's write-up. Plain interpolation draws faint rings around small bright lights, because the four coarser probes see a nearby light from slightly different angles. The bilinear fix traces a separate short ray from each probe to each of its four parents and merges with each parent on its own, which gets rid of the rings and stops light leaking through thin walls, for four times the rays. And the 4 directions of cascade 0 are coarse enough that a small light can slip between two of them, so every frame I rotate the whole set of directions by a new angle and keep a short running average of the result. Last, interpolating between coarse probes leaves a faint pattern tied to where those probes sit: soft blotches the size of the coarse spacing, and a cross around small lights. So every frame, each cascade above 0 also shifts its whole probe grid by a new offset inside one probe cell. The pattern moves with the grid, and the same running average smooths most of it out. With all of that, every cascade below the top traces four short rays per pixel of the light grid, so the lamp below (five cascades on a desktop screen) comes to about 17. Brute force at the same quality would be thousands.

Aside · Shipped in a game first

Alexander Sannikov developed radiance cascades for the lighting in Path of Exile 2 and wrote them up in 2023. If the merge looks familiar, coarse levels answering for whatever the fine levels can't see, it's the same idea as the Barnes–Hut tree in Two Falling Dots.

Deep dive: distance fields and the jump flood

Two supporting pieces make it fast. The rays sphere-march against a distance field: at every pixel, the distance to the nearest solid thing, so each step along a ray can jump that far without missing anything. The field is built by the jump flood algorithm (Rong & Tan, 2006). Every pixel repeatedly asks neighbours at halving offsets which surface pixel is the nearest one they know about. On the roughly 400×260 light grid of the lamp below, that's nine passes, and afterwards every pixel knows its distance to all the geometry. It's the same halving rhythm as the scan in part three and the FFT in part five.

This sandbox is the algorithm with nothing else in front of it. Paint lights, paint walls, erase, and the shadows follow whatever you draw. The slider cuts the stack of cascades short. With one cascade, light travels four pixels and stops. Each level you add lets it travel four times further, for the same cost per pixel.

Step 4 · AssemblyThe lamp

Everything runs in one direction, with no loops: particles → temperature → metaball field → emission and occlusion → distance field → cascades → pixels. The coil is painted into the same scene texture as the wax, so it isn't a special light, just a hot object. The metal base, the cap and the table are dark occluders. The glass is drawn afterwards and doesn't block anything.

The light is computed at a fraction of the canvas resolution, because light is smooth. The final pass re-reads the blurred field at every canvas pixel, so the edges of the wax stay sharp, and shades each pixel with the light from the cascades. It writes into a high-range buffer, and the page's bloom and tone mapping take it from there.

The view button shows each stage of the renderer. Scene is what the rays see: a few glowing shapes on black, which is the entire input to the lighting. Distance field is the jump flood's contour map, rippling as blobs split and merge. Light only leaves out the wax's own emission and shows just the light that travelled: everything in that view arrived at its pixel from somewhere else. Back on final, watch a cold blob drift past a hot one and pick up a warm rim on one side. Nothing in the code draws that rim. It's the light transport.

Wrap-upWhat I took from it

A lot carried over from the matter series. The grid sort under the wax is the one from part three, unchanged except that each particle grew a temperature. Splatting particles into a field and reading it back is the same move as part five's mass deposit onto a grid. The cascade merge is the Barnes–Hut idea from part one, applied to light.

  • A matter sim asks where things go. A light sim asks what things see. The particle code barely changed. Everything new on this page is about the second question.
  • Match the sampling to how the signal changes. Near light varies in space and far light varies in angle. Giving each level only the detail it needs is what makes the cascades cheap.
  • Light can run coarse if the display pass doesn't. The cascades run at a fraction of the canvas resolution, and nobody can tell, because the edges are drawn at full resolution.

Things I'd try next: give the water a little colour so the light picks up a tint on its way through, let the glass refract, and replace the buoyancy shortcut with a real second fluid. Part two keeps the cascades and changes the scene: thousands of moving lights, light that bounces, and a sky.
