Bounced Light · Part 1

The Lava Lamp

Wax that heats, swells, rises, cools, and sinks, lit entirely by its own glow. Every blob comes from a 2D particle fluid carrying temperature, and all the illumination and shadows come from real-time light transport. Drag through the glass to stir it.

Series overview · Bounced Light Bounced Light leaves fake additive glow behind and simulates how light actually travels, shadows, scatters, and splits through a scene. Over six interactive essays, the series builds a self-lit 2D lava lamp powered by radiance cascades here, a bonfire of thousands of glowing embers in Part 2, volumetric light scattering through fog in Part 3, 3D sphere-traced soft shadows in Part 4, a Monte Carlo path-traced Cornell box in Part 5, and a dispersive glass prism that splits white light into a spectrum in Part 6.

The six parts of the Particle Worlds series simulate gravity, sand, water, swirling dust, a slice of the cosmic web, and an interactive fluid instrument, yet not one of them ever simulates light. Every bright spot in those demos was just additive paint: particles carrying their own color, stacked onto a buffer and tone-mapped afterward. When the core of a galaxy flared up in part one, its spiral arms felt none of that light.

To start exploring actual light transport, a lava lamp in two dimensions turns out to be an ideal test bench. Two coupled problems sit inside that glass bottle. First, there is a heat-driven fluid simulation: energy enters at the heating coil, leaks out near the cap, and buoyancy turns those temperature differences into slow convection. Second, the same material plays both roles in the lighting. Hot wax near the coil glows bright amber, while cool wax sinking back down goes nearly black and casts shadows across the vessel. Because lights here constantly deform, pinch in two, and drift behind opaque blockers, faking the glow with radial sprites stamped onto each blob falls apart immediately. The light actually has to travel through the scene.

Step 1 · One number per particleWax with a temperature

Underneath the glow sits the same smoothed-particle hydrodynamics (SPH) solver written for water in Sorting Water. Particles get binned into a uniform spatial grid, each one sums its neighbors with a smooth falloff kernel to estimate local density, and pressure pushes particles apart wherever they crowd too tightly. Giving the wax a temperature only requires storing one extra float per particle and piggybacking heat conduction onto that same neighbor loop: 

rho   += w·w              // density, as before
tFlux += (T_j − T_i)·w    // conduction: heat diffuses along the same kernel

Particles resting near the bottom coil pick up heat (strongest over the middle of the element), while everything cools slightly all the time and sheds heat much faster inside a cooling zone near the top of the bottle. Temperature then drives motion in two ways: 

rest_i = ρ₀ · (1 − β·T_i)        // thermal expansion: warmer wax prefers more room
acc.y += buoyancy·T_i − gravity   // proxy for the surrounding water lifting hot wax

Lowering a warm particle's target rest density is a cheap approximation of thermal expansion: a hot pocket wants fewer neighbors per unit area, so it pushes outward and swells as it heats up. The second line is an even bigger shortcut. In a real lamp, wax floats inside clear liquid, and buoyancy is that surrounding fluid pushing lighter wax upward. Rather than simulating a second fluid, the solver applies an upward force proportional to temperature and uses velocity damping to stand in for the water's viscous drag. Cold wax sinks, hot wax rises, and the damping keeps everything moving at that sleepy lava-lamp pace.

Left with only pressure and buoyancy, though, a rising plume shreds into wispy streamers. Pressure resists crowding, yet nothing pulls separated particles back together. Real wax beads up because the interface between wax and water carries surface tension. To imitate that at the particle level, the solver adds a pairwise cohesion force that attracts neighbors at medium range and repels them up close. Thin necks now pinch off cleanly, small droplets coalesce into larger ones, and floating blobs round themselves out. You can zero out the surface tension slider in the full lamp further down the page to see how quickly the wax frays without it.

Watch the pool at the bottom for a few seconds and a warm dome swells until its buoyancy beats gravity and pulls a plume upward. As the plume reaches the dashed cooling zone near the cap, it drops temperature, loses lift, and turns back toward the floor. That whole convection cycle emerges directly from the four parameters on the sliders: coil heat, buoyancy, expansion β, and cooling.

Step 2 · Particles become a surfaceMetaballs

Particles make fluid motion straightforward, yet drawn as raw dots they never look like continuous melted wax. Turning a particle cloud into a smooth liquid calls for an implicit surface: each particle splats a soft circular bump into a low-resolution field texture, those overlapping bumps sum together, and any point whose total exceeds a threshold counts as inside the wax. Long before two blobs physically collide, their tails add past the threshold and draw a smooth liquid bridge between them.

Even with 9,000 particles, summing individual bumps still leaves slight lumpiness at the scale of single particles, which a sharp threshold turns into scalloped edges. Before the surface is extracted, a separable Gaussian blur runs across the field (one horizontal pass and one vertical pass, nine taps each) so the contours stay glassy and smooth.

Aside · Blobby molecules

Jim Blinn introduced this technique in 1982 while rendering electron-density clouds around chemical bonds, originally calling them "blobby molecules." The graphics community soon adopted the catchier name metaballs.

Each particle splat deposits its weighted temperature into a second texture channel, so dividing by the local field density yields the average wax temperature at any pixel. From there, every point inside the wax gets an emission color and an opacity for the lighting pass: 

emission  = palette(T) · luminance(T)  // cold: near-black plum … hot: bright amber
occlusion = 1 inside the wax           // hot or cold, wax blocks passing rays

Whether a blob is glowing hot or cooled to dark plum, it blocks light behind it. The same mass of wax starts out as a bright light source near the bottom coil, drifts up past cooler wax, and eventually becomes a dark occluder catching warm rim light from the next rising plume. To render that accurately, every pixel needs to gather light from across the entire canvas while respecting whatever wax sits in the way.

Step 3 · Light transportRadiance cascades

Computing full 2D lighting with shadows in real time runs straight into a brute-force wall: marching hundreds of rays out of every single pixel on every frame scales far too badly to hit 60 fps. Radiance cascades sidestep that wall by taking advantage of a basic geometric property of how light varies across space, often called the penumbra condition.

Light from a nearby source shifts rapidly as you move a few pixels sideways, because small blockers right beside you cast crisp shadows. On the other hand, a nearby source spans a wide angle, so you only need a handful of ray directions to notice it. Distant light behaves in the opposite way: its brightness changes slowly from spot to spot, yet a faraway bright blob occupies a narrow sliver of angle, so you need densely packed ray directions to hit it reliably. Instead of forcing one grid of probes to resolve both fine space and fine angles everywhere, radiance cascades split the distance outward into concentric intervals across a stack of grids: 

cascade 0:  probes every 2 px,   4 rays each,  covering   0 →   4 px
cascade 1:  probes every 4 px,  16 rays each,  covering   4 →  20 px
cascade 2:  probes every 8 px,  64 rays each,  covering  20 →  84 px
// every step up: ¼ as many probes × 4× as many rays = constant cost per level

Each cascade traces only its own hollow ring of distance, starting right where the finer cascade beneath it stopped. Scroll through the diagram below to watch one probe build up its view of the scene across three levels and merge the results back inward.

Once every cascade has traced its own ring, the renderer merges the stack from the coarsest level back down to cascade 0. Whenever a ray finishes its interval without hitting anything, it blends in the radiance found along the matching angular sector in the level above, interpolated across the four nearest coarse probes. By the time the merge reaches cascade 0, every 2×2 block of the light grid knows the light arriving from across the whole scene, with zero Monte Carlo speckle because the ray angles are completely structured.

Three practical refinements clean up the visual artifacts that show up in a textbook implementation. First, naive bilinear interpolation between four coarse probes can draw faint halo rings around small bright lights and let light bleed through thin walls, because the four parent probes view a nearby emitter from slightly different vantage points. Using the bilinear fix (developed by the community after Alexander Sannikov's original write-up) traces four short rays from each probe directly toward its four parent probes and merges each individually, eliminating both ringing and light leaks at four times the ray budget. Second, cascade 0 only has four ray directions, which leaves small emitters able to slip between spokes; rotating the ray directions by a fresh offset every frame and keeping a short temporal running average fills those gaps smoothly. Third, interpolating from fixed coarse probe grids leaves subtle stationary blotches and faint crosses around point lights, so each cascade above level 0 also jitters its probe grid offset inside a single cell each frame, letting the same temporal average dissolve the grid pattern. Put together, every level below the top traces four short rays per light-grid pixel, bringing the five-cascade desktop lamp below to roughly 17 short rays per pixel instead of the thousands brute-force marching would demand.

Aside · Shipped in a game first

Alexander Sannikov designed radiance cascades for the real-time lighting in Path of Exile 2 and published the technique in 2023. The core trade-off, letting coarse distant summaries stand in for details that nearby grids cannot afford to trace individually, echoes the Barnes–Hut tree from Two Falling Dots.

Deep dive: distance fields and the jump flood

Two ingredients keep every ray segment cheap to march. Rather than stepping one pixel at a time, each ray sphere-marches through a 2D distance field that stores the distance from every pixel to the nearest solid surface, allowing the ray to jump safely across open liquid in large strides. That distance field is rebuilt every frame on the GPU using the jump flood algorithm (Rong & Tan, 2006): pixels poll neighbors at halving step sizes (256, 128, 64, ... down to 1) and record the closest surface pixel they hear about. On the lamp's light grid (around 400×260 pixels), nine quick passes are enough to give every pixel its exact distance to the moving wax, following the same power-of-two halving pattern as the prefix scan in part three and the FFT in part five of the previous series.

Before plugging this back into the fluid sim, you can test the lighting engine by itself below. Draw colored emitters with paint light (cycling hues with the color button), sketch opaque blockers with paint wall, or carve openings with erase. The cascades slider truncates the hierarchy: at 1 cascade, light stops after a tiny neighborhood around each source, and every additional level stretches its reach roughly four times farther at the same cost per level. Step 4 · AssemblyThe lamp

With all four stages wired together, each frame flows straight down a single GPU pipeline: SPH particle step with temperature, metaball splat and Gaussian blur, scene texture (emission plus occlusion), jump-flood distance field, radiance cascades, and final compositing. The heating coil is drawn right into the scene texture as another bright shape, while the metal base, cap, and tabletop are painted as dark occluders. The glass silhouette is composited last without blocking light.

Because soft illumination varies far more slowly than silhouette edges, the cascades run at a fraction of the canvas resolution. The final display pass then re-samples the blurred metaball field at full display resolution so every wax outline stays razor-sharp, combines it with the upsampled radiance, and sends the high-dynamic-range result through bloom and ACES tone mapping.

Clicking view: final cycles through the intermediate buffers. Scene (what the rays see) reveals the stark emission-and-occlusion input handed to the lighting solver. Distance field exposes the jump flood's rippling contour bands as wax blobs merge and pull apart. Light only strips out the wax's own emission so you see purely the light arriving from elsewhere in the vessel. Switch back to final and follow a dark cooling blob sinking past a bright rising one: the warm rim lighting along its underside comes straight out of the cascade transport.

Wrap-upWhat carried over

Surprisingly little of the fluid code had to be invented from scratch. The spatial grid sort comes directly from the water solver in the previous series, with one temperature float added per particle; splatting particles into a continuous field mirrors the mass-deposit grid from the gravity solver; and merging coarse distant light into fine local probes uses the same hierarchical intuition as Barnes–Hut. What really changed was the question each shader asks: instead of only asking where matter moves next, the GPU now spends most of its time asking what every point in the scene can see.

Matching the sampling structure to the signal is what made that affordable here. Near light changes sharply in space, far light changes sharply in angle, and keeping the cascades on a coarse grid while redrawing geometry edges at full canvas resolution hides the low-res lighting almost completely. A further polish of this lamp would tint the liquid so light picks up color as it travels, add refraction along the glass walls, and replace the temperature-buoyancy shortcut with a true two-fluid sim. First, though, the next part tests how far these same 2D cascades can stretch when a scene fills up with thousands of moving lights, bouncing wall colors, and an open sky.
