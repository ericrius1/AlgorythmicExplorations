Bounced Light · Part 2

The Bonfire

Every ember above is a light, the trees cast shadows, and the ground catches the fire. The renderer from part one, with a scene where nearly everything glows. Stir the embers with your cursor.

Scroll down to read ↓

Part one built the whole pipeline around one lamp: a distance field, a stack of radiance cascades, and a merge. This part keeps that pipeline and changes the scene. The bonfire above has a flame, a log, a few hundred embers, a ground, eight pines and a night sky, and I wanted to see how far the same renderer goes when nearly everything in the picture is a light.

It turns out three small additions are enough: a lot of moving lights, light that bounces, and a sky. None of them touches the cascade code itself.

Step 1 · A fixed costMany lights

Radiance cascades never ask where the lights are. Every probe marches its rays against the distance field and reads whatever emission it lands on, so a light source is just a bright pixel in the scene texture. The cost depends on the size of the light grid and the number of cascades. How many lights there are doesn't enter into it.

That's not how most real-time lighting works. A forward renderer pays for every light at every pixel it might touch, which is why game and film lighting artists have long worked to a budget of lights. The Particle Worlds series spent six parts getting around an n² between bodies, and light has the same n² (every pixel wants light from every source). Radiance cascades get around it by never listing the sources at all: they sample the light field the sources make, the way part five's particle-mesh gravity samples a potential on a grid instead of summing pairs.

The slider goes from 16 lights to 8,192. Watch the frame rate while you drag it: it barely moves. Every spark is a light and also an obstacle, so sparks shadow each other, which you can see when a dense cloud drifts behind one of the pillars. The wind is the curl noise from The Fluid Instrument: take the gradient of smooth noise and turn it a quarter turn, and you get a flow that never piles up or empties out anywhere.

Step 2 · Last frame's lightBounce

Everything so far is direct light: from a source to a surface, one hop. Real rooms are mostly lit by the second hop, light that hit a wall and came off it tinted. The textbook way to get that is to solve a big system that couples every surface to every other. I reuse the previous frame instead: 

emission(x) = base_emission(x) + 2 · albedo(x) · fluence_last_frame(x)
// every surface re-emits a tinted share of the light it received a frame ago

That's one texture read in the scene shader, from the cascade-0 result the previous frame already paid for. On the first frame the surfaces only have direct light. On the second, they glow with it, so the cascades deliver one bounce. The third frame delivers two bounces, and so on. Each bounce is dimmer than the last by the albedo, so the series converges, and it converges in time, at one bounce per frame. At 60 frames a second that's faster than you can see.

The 2 is there because the fluence is an average over every direction, and half of those directions point into the wall, where there's no light. A matte surface re-emits what arrives from the open half, so reading the average and doubling it gets the amount right. At 1 the bounce is there but too faint to notice.

Reading a surface's light needs one detail. Inside a solid, cascade 0 only sees the solid itself, so sampling the light there gives a dark, blocky rim. Every solid in these scenes is a signed distance function, so I step out along the surface normal to clean air and read the light there.

Aside · Radiosity and the Cornell box

The textbook method is radiosity, brought to graphics by Goral, Torrance, Greenberg and Battaile at Cornell in 1984. The same paper introduced the Cornell box, the red-and-green-walled room that graphics papers have been rendering ever since. Radiosity solves the surface-to-surface coupling as one matrix. The frame loop above adds up the same series one term per frame.

The room is seen from above: a wooden floor, plaster walls, a crimson wall and a teal wall, a screen in the middle, a warm lamp, and a dim blue window in the far wall. The coloured walls are not lights. Turn the bounce off and you get the literal room: the lamp's pool of light, a sharp-edged shadow with only the window's blue in it, and dark walls. Turn it back on and carry the lamp toward a wall, and the floor and the far walls take on that wall's colour, even inside the shadow. The strength slider goes to 2.5. Past 1, a wall gives back more light than it receives, which no real paint can do, and the colours wash out as the room fills with its own glow.

Smooth light in an empty room is also where the cascades' flaws show most, so this figure gets a light grid twice as dense as the others, and if your GPU can't keep 60 frames a second at that size it steps the grid down rather than dropping frames. The floor reads the light through a small blur that stops at walls, which hides the last of the probe pattern without softening the shadow much.

Step 3 · Environment lightThe sky is a cascade

Daylight doesn't come from anything in the scene. It's what you see when a ray leaves the scene without hitting anything, and the cascades already have a place for that case. In part one, when a ray of the top cascade got to the end of its interval and hadn't hit anything, it merged with darkness. Here it returns the sky instead: a function of direction, dark blue overhead, warmer toward the horizon, and bright where the sun is. Every probe and every shadow then picks up sky light with no extra pass. 

if (top-cascade ray escapes):
    radiance = sky(direction)   // the light that nothing in the scene claimed

It fits because the top cascade's rays are the longest ones, the light from far away, and an environment map is light from infinitely far away, looked up by direction. Sky light, with soft shadows under the trees and a warm band at the horizon, costs one function evaluated at the top level.

The display pass draws the sky behind everything too, and that took a decision. In 2D the "air" in the picture is the light field itself, and showing all of it at the same brightness turned each tree's shadow into a dark wedge pointing at the stars. So outdoors I show the light field strongly in a layer of haze near the ground and in the smoke over the fire, and only faintly above that, where you mostly see the sky.

Drag from night to day and the balance flips. At night the fire lights every surface, the ground near it is fire-coloured, and the tree shadows point away from the flame. By day the sun lights the scene, the shadows line up, and the fire is a warm smudge. That's the reason a campfire feels different after dark, and it's the same cascades either way.

Step 4 · AssemblyThe whole bonfire

Everything at once, plus the hand controller from part six of the last series. Click ✋, let the camera find your hand, and your palm stirs the wind while your index fingertip sheds embers. Every new ember is a light that falls on the trees, the ground and the other embers, and it costs the renderer nothing it wasn't already paying. The camera only switches on when you click.

The view button shows the same stages as in part one. Scene is a few silhouettes and a swarm of bright dots, which is the entire input to the lighting. Distance field shows the jump flood rippling as embers fly through it. Light only shows only the light that travelled. Try it at night: everything you see in that view arrived at its pixel from somewhere else.

Wrap-upWhat's missing

This part added a few thousand lights, repeated bounce, and an environment map, with three small edits. None of them changed the march, the merge or the distance field. The expensive part of the renderer doesn't care how many lights there are.

  • Sample the field, not the sources. Once the renderer asks what each point sees, the number of lights stops mattering.
  • Last frame's answer is a cheap first guess. Feeding the light back in one frame late gives multi-bounce lighting for one texture read.
  • Look for the slot the algorithm already has. The sky didn't need a new pass. It's what a ray returns when it escapes.

One thing is missing, and it shows. The haze and smoke over the fire are painted in by the display pass. The rays pass straight through them, so smoke never dims the light behind it and never glows on its own. In this world light either travels freely or stops dead. Things I'd try next: smoke that blocks some of the light, embers that cool to dark grey ash, and a moon. Part three does the first of those properly, with fog that absorbs and scatters light along every ray.
