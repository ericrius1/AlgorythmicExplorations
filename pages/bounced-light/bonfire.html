Bounced Light · Part 2

The Bonfire

Hundreds of drifting embers that all cast real light and shadows onto the pines and hill below, powered by the same 2D cascade renderer from part one. Drag your cursor across the fire to stir the sparks.

Scroll down to read ↓

For decades, real-time graphics lived under a strict light budget. In a traditional renderer, every point light you drop into a scene means more loops in the fragment shader and another shadow map to update, so artists learn to ration lamps carefully and fake swarms of sparks with unlit billboard particles. Radiance cascades flip that constraint on its head. Because the solver from part one reads illumination straight out of a scene texture rather than looping over a list of lights, an outdoor clearing packed with a crackling flame, hundreds of flying embers, glowing terrain, and an entire sky fits inside the exact same pipeline.

Getting from an enclosed lava lamp to the bonfire above takes three small additions: a swarm of particle emitters, light that bounces off colored surfaces, and an environment sky for rays that miss the geometry. All three plug into the edges of the existing solver without touching the cascade merge itself.

Step 1 · A fixed costMany lights

A radiance cascade probe never maintains an array of light sources. It marches each ray segment against the jump-flood distance field and picks up whatever emission is painted into the scene texture at the hit point. As a result, rendering cost scales only with the light grid resolution and the number of cascade levels, regardless of how many separate emitters happen to be drawn into that texture.

Forward lighting hits the same all-pairs bottleneck that dominated the Particle Worlds series: every shaded pixel wants to know what every light source contributes to it. Sampling a light field built on a grid dissolves that coupling in much the same way the particle-mesh gravity solver in part five sampled a gridded potential instead of summing forces between every pair of stars.

Drag the lights slider all the way from 16 up to 8,192 and watch how steady the frame rate stays. Every spark is splatted into the scene texture as both an emitter and a tiny solid occluder, so dense clusters cast subtle shadows on each other as they drift around the three vertical pillars. The breeze carrying them is the divergence-free curl noise from The Fluid Instrument: taking the gradient of smooth procedural noise and rotating it ninety degrees produces swirling eddies that never artificially pile particles into sinks.

Step 2 · Last frame's lightBounce

Up to now, every surface has received only direct light: one straight trip from an emitter to a wall. Inside real rooms, a huge share of the illumination comes from light that has already bounced off a floor or painted wall and picked up its tint. Computing all those surface-to-surface exchanges simultaneously usually requires solving a large linear system. When you already have an interactive frame loop running at 60 Hz, there is a much cheaper trick: feed the previous frame's lighting right back into the scene texture. 

emission(x) = base_emission(x) + 2 · albedo(x) · fluence_last_frame(x)
// every surface re-emits a tinted fraction of the light that hit it one frame ago

That costs a single extra texture lookup in the scene pass, reading from the cascade-0 buffer that the previous frame already computed. Frame one delivers direct light; frame two lets every lit wall glow with that light to produce a first bounce; frame three adds the second bounce, and so on. Because each matte bounce multiplies by an albedo below 1, the infinite sum decays geometrically and settles within a fraction of a second.

The factor of 2 accounts for the geometry of a wall. Cascade 0 averages incoming radiance over the full 360° circle, and half of those directions point straight into the dark interior of the solid wall itself. Since a matte surface collects light across its exposed 180° half-circle, doubling the full-circle average restores the proper energy scale; leaving it at 1 produces noticeably dim bounces.

One small geometric snag appears right on a wall's boundary: inside an occluder, cascade 0 only sees the solid interior, so sampling the previous fluence right at the pixel gives a dark, chunky rim. Because all static shapes in these scenes are defined by signed distance functions, the scene shader simply steps a short distance outward along the surface normal into open air and samples the incoming light there.

Aside · Radiosity and the Cornell box

Solving diffuse surface-to-surface light exchange as a global matrix equation is known as radiosity, introduced to computer graphics in 1984 by Cindy Goral, Kenneth Torrance, Donald Greenberg, and Bennett Battaile at Cornell. That same paper debuted the Cornell box, the red-and-green-walled chamber that rendering papers still test against today. Feeding last frame's fluence back into the scene shader evaluates the exact same Neumann bounce series, one term per frame.

Look down into this top-down room: wooden floorboards, neutral plaster walls, a crimson wall on the left, a teal wall on the right, a central divider, a roving warm lamp, and a cool blue window along the top edge. Neither colored wall emits light of its own. Toggle bounce: on off and the room strips down to direct light alone, leaving hard dark shadows touched only by the blue window. Turn bounce back on and drag your cursor to carry the lamp close to the crimson or teal wall; its reflected hue washes across the floorboards and wraps right around behind the divider. Pushing bounce strength above 1 lets surfaces return more energy than they absorb, an unphysical amplifier that quickly floods the room in glowing feedback.

Wide stretches of smooth floor are also where probe-grid artifacts stand out most clearly, so this figure starts on a light grid twice as dense as the others (and automatically steps down a notch if your GPU dips below 60 fps, keeping motion fluid). The floor shader also reads the fluence through a small edge-stopping blur that smooths away faint probe structure without blurring shadows across walls.

Step 3 · Environment lightThe sky is a cascade

Outdoors, most light arrives from the sky dome rather than from objects inside the clearing. Radiance cascades already leave an obvious hook for infinitely distant light. In part one, any ray in the coarsest cascade that finished its interval without hitting wax simply merged with black. Replacing that default with an analytic sky function (deep blue overhead, warm tint near the horizon, and a bright solar lobe along the sun direction) brings ambient sky light and soft tree shadows into every probe for free: 

if (top-cascade ray escapes):
    radiance = sky(direction)   // distant sky light along any unobstructed ray

Because the top cascade holds the finest angular resolution in the entire stack, it samples a tight directional sun cleanly without needing any separate sun pass or shadow map.

Showing the sky in the final display pass did require one artistic call. In 2D lighting demos, empty air normally visualizes the light field directly, which made each pine tree cast a stark dark wedge straight up into the starry night sky. For the outdoor scene, the display pass shows the light field strongly through the low ground haze and the rising plume of smoke above the flames, then fades it down high overhead so the night sky and stars read cleanly behind the trees.

Slide time of day from night through twilight and dusk into day to watch the dominant light source switch hands. After dark, the bonfire owns the clearing, tinting the hill warm orange and throwing pine shadows outward away from the flames. By midday, the bright sky and sun overpower the fire completely, aligning every shadow along the sun angle and reducing the bonfire to a pale flicker.

Wrap-upWhat's still missing

Going from a single enclosed lamp to thousands of drifting sparks, multi-bounce color bleeding, and a full day-night sky took three compact additions without altering a single line of the distance field or cascade merge. Once a renderer queries a shared spatial light field instead of iterating over individual light sources, piling in extra emitters stops hurting performance; feeding last frame's fluence back into the scene shader turns one texture lookup into multi-bounce radiosity; and hooking an environment function onto escaping top-cascade rays brings in sky light with zero extra passes.

Even so, one glaring simplification gives itself away in the bonfire above. The haze hugging the hill and the smoke curling above the flames are painted on during the final display pass, completely invisible to the cascade rays. Light in this 2D world still behaves in binary fashion: it either passes through vacuum untouched or slams into an opaque solid. The next part gives the air real optical density, so smoke and fog dim beams passing through them and scatter trapped light outward along every ray.
