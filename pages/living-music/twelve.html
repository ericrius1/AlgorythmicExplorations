Living Music · Part 4

Twelve Imperfect Notes

Stack twelve pure fifths and you almost land back where you started. The spiral above is that almost, drawn live. Tap a note to hear it.

Part three showed why some pairs of notes sound smooth together: their frequencies sit in simple ratios like 2:1, 3:2 and 5:4, so their harmonics line up instead of rubbing. A singer or a violinist can put a note anywhere, so they can always reach those ratios. A piano can't. Its strings are tuned before the concert starts, and a fretted guitar or a flute with holes drilled in it has the same problem. Building an instrument means choosing a fixed set of pitches in advance.

The obvious way to choose them is to start on C and build every other note out of the smoothest interval after the octave, the fifth. Multiply by 3/2 to get G. Do it again to get D, and fold it back down an octave so it stays in range. Keep going and new notes keep appearing. This article follows that plan to the point where it breaks, and then looks at the ways people have patched it, from medieval organs to the 31-note organ in Amsterdam.

Step 1 · Stacked fifthsThe spiral that doesn't close

The figure at the top of the page draws pitch as a spiral. The angle is where a note sits inside the octave, with C at twelve o'clock, and the radius grows as the pitch rises, so one full turn outward is one octave up. A fifth is 701.96 cents (a cent is a hundredth of an equal-tempered semitone; more on that below), which is 0.585 of a turn. Each new fifth swings a bit more than halfway round and lands one ring further out.

Twelve fifths go round seven times. If 3:2 and 2:1 fit together, the twelfth fifth would land on C, seven octaves up. It lands just past it: 

(3/2)¹² = 129.746        // twelve fifths
2⁷      = 128            // seven octaves
129.746 / 128 = 1.01364  // 23.46 cents: the Pythagorean comma

That 23.46-cent overshoot is the Pythagorean comma, about a quarter of a semitone. Stacking more fifths doesn't help. For any number of fifths to equal some number of octaves you'd need 3m = 2n, and a power of 3 is always odd while a power of 2 is always even. The spiral never closes. Every tuning system on this page is a way of living with that.

Below, the same spiral sits next to the octave-folded view: the notes you've collected, sorted into a scale, with the size of every step and how far each note is from the piano key with the same name.

Watch the step sizes as you add notes. With 5 notes (C G D A E) the octave splits into steps of only two sizes, 203.9 and 294.1 cents. That's the pentatonic scale. With 7 notes there are again two sizes, five steps of 203.9 and two of 90.2. That's the major scale and its relatives, and part five is about why it won. With 12 notes the two sizes are 113.7 and 90.2: a chromatic scale with two different semitones. In between, at 8 notes for example, there are three sizes, never more. Mathematicians call this the three-distance theorem. It's the reason the scales that come out of a chain of fifths have the step counts they do.

The thirteenth note is B♯, and it adds a third step size: a sliver 23.46 cents wide between B♯ and C. That's the comma again, seen from inside the octave. Press C against the 13th note to hear the two together. Near middle C they beat about 3.6 times a second.

Now drag the fifth-size slider. Every fifth changes at once, so the overshoot changes twelve times as fast. At 700.00 cents the thirteenth note lands on C and the spiral closes. At 696.58 cents (a tuning called meantone, coming up in Step 3) it falls 41 cents short instead. Only one fifth size closes the loop, and shaving each fifth by 1.96 cents to get there is equal temperament, the tuning of almost every piano, fretted guitar and synthesizer.

Aside · The same chain, twice

The Greeks credited this tuning to Pythagoras, but the chain of fifths was found more than once. Chinese texts from the third century BCE generate twelve pitches by alternately cutting a pipe to two-thirds of its length and lengthening it by a third, which is the same chain of fifths folded into the same octave. In the first century BCE the theorist Jing Fang carried the chain much further and noticed that 53 fifths come back to within a few cents of the start. That observation comes back in Step 5.

Cents, the logarithm music runs on

The ear hears ratios, not differences: A 220 to A 440 sounds like the same step as A 440 to A 880, even though one is 220 Hz and the other 440 Hz. So to compare intervals you take logarithms. One cent is 1/1200 of an octave, and an interval with frequency ratio r is 1200·log₂(r) cents. Equal-tempered semitones are 100 cents each, and the pure intervals come out as awkward numbers: the fifth 701.955, the major third 386.314, the minor third 315.641.

Alexander Ellis introduced the cent in 1885, in a paper comparing the scales of instruments he had measured from around the world, including Javanese gamelan. His conclusion was that there is no single natural scale. Different musical cultures have tuned the octave in very different ways. Part five comes back to some of them.

Step 2 · BeatsWhere the error is audible

Two cents per fifth is too small to hear in a melody. Part three showed where tuning errors do show up: in notes played together. Every note is a stack of harmonics at whole-number multiples of its frequency. When a harmonic of one note lands a few hertz away from a harmonic of another, the two add up and cancel by turns, and the sound swells and fades at the difference of their frequencies. Those swells are called beats, and they're how piano tuners tune.

The figure draws each note as its ladder of harmonics. Collisions are joined by a line, with the beat rate written on it. The strip at the bottom draws the loudness of the strongest collision over one second. Hold the chord, then switch between pure ratios and equal temperament.

With pure ratios every collision is exact and the chord sits still. In equal temperament the fifth from middle C beats 0.89 times a second: the third harmonic of C is at 784.88 Hz and the second harmonic of G is at 783.99 Hz. That's slow enough to sound like a gentle sway. The major third is a different story. Equal temperament's third is 400 cents against a pure 386.3, 13.7 cents wide, seven times the fifth's error. The fifth harmonic of C and the fourth harmonic of E miss by 10.4 Hz, so a C major chord on a piano shimmers about ten times a second. Inside the triad the minor third between E and G is worse again, at 17.8 beats a second.

Move the root slider and the beat rates scale with it. The miss is a fixed fraction of the frequency, so an octave higher the same chord beats twice as fast. That's part of why tempered chords in the bass sound calmer than the same chords up high.

Most of us have heard tempered thirds all our lives and hear the shimmer as part of the sound of a piano. Musicians who can tune each note as they play often move toward the pure ratios on long chords. Barbershop singers chase it on purpose and call a locked-in chord one that rings. Use the A/B button for a while and the difference gets easier to hear.

Step 3 · TemperamentWhere to hide the comma

The comma has to go somewhere. Twelve fifths have to fit into seven octaves, so between them they must give up 23.46 cents. Choosing which fifths give up how much is called temperament, and the choice decides how every chord in every key sounds.

Pythagorean tuning keeps eleven fifths pure and puts the whole comma into the twelfth, usually between G♯ and E♭. That fifth is 23.46 cents narrow, so badly out of tune that it howls, and organists called it the wolf. Its major thirds are four pure fifths stacked up, 407.8 cents, which is 21.5 cents wide and rough. Medieval polyphony built its cadences on octaves and fifths and treated thirds as unstable sounds that had to move on, which fits this tuning.

When the third became the heart of harmony in the Renaissance, the tuning had to change. Four pure fifths overshoot a pure third by a different comma, the syntonic comma: 81/64 against 80/64, or 21.5 cents. Quarter-comma meantone, described by Pietro Aron in 1523, narrows every fifth by a quarter of that, 5.4 cents, so four fifths make a pure 5:4. Eight major thirds come out pure. The price is a wolf fifth of 737.6 cents and four keys whose thirds are 41 cents wide. Music in meantone stays near home.

The well temperaments of the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries split the difference. Andreas Werckmeister's best-known scheme (1691) narrows four fifths by a quarter of the Pythagorean comma and leaves the other eight pure. Every key is usable, and they aren't all alike: C major's third is 3.9 cents wide, while F♯, C♯ and G♯ major get the old Pythagorean 21.5. Kirnberger and Vallotti published other ways to split it.

The arcs round the outside are the twelve fifths, labelled with how much each was narrowed. The bars pointing in are each key's major third, green for pure and red for rough. Pick meantone and tour the keys: C, G, D, A and E major sound calm, then B, F♯, C♯ and G♯ major arrive with thirds 41 cents wide. Pick Werckmeister III and the same tour goes from smooth to restless without ever falling apart. Pick equal and every key sounds the same.

Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier (Book I, 1722) has a prelude and fugue in each of the 24 major and minor keys, written for a keyboard tuned so that all of them work. It wasn't necessarily equal temperament, and nobody knows what tuning Bach used. In 2005 the harpsichordist Bradley Lehman argued that the looping drawing on the title page encodes one, and that reading is still argued over. Eighteenth-century writers such as Christian Schubart described each key as having its own character. Some of that was association and habit, and some of it was probably the tuning: in a well temperament, remote keys really do sound different.

Aside · Who worked out equal temperament

Equal temperament needs the twelfth root of 2, and two people computed it independently around the same time. Zhu Zaiyu, a prince of the Ming dynasty, published a precise calculation of twelve equal steps in 1584. Simon Stevin worked it out in the Netherlands around 1605. Keyboards were mostly tuned in meantone and well temperaments for two more centuries, and equal temperament only became the normal piano tuning in the nineteenth century.

Step 4 · Why twelveDividing the octave

Equal steps buy freedom: every key is the same, so music can move anywhere. But nothing says the steps have to be semitones. Divide the octave into N equal steps and ask how close the nearest step comes to a pure fifth. The chart does that for every N from 5 to 53, along with the major third.

Five and seven equal steps miss the fifth by 18 and 16 cents. Then twelve lands within 1.96 cents, and nothing does better until 29 (1.49), 41 (0.48) and 53 (0.07). Twelve is the smallest division that gets the fifth right, which is a large part of why we ended up with it.

Where those record-holders come from is number theory. A fifth is log₂(3/2) = 0.58496… of an octave, and an N-step system with its fifth at step p is approximating that number by the fraction p/N. The best fractions for any number come from its continued fraction, and for this one they are 1/2, 3/5, 7/12, 24/41, 31/53, 179/306. The chart labels them. 7/12 is unusually close for such a small denominator, which makes twelve a lucky size.

Twelve is not as lucky with the major third: it misses by 13.7 cents, which is where Step 2's shimmer comes from. Turn on the 7:4 bars too and 12 looks worse again. It misses the seventh harmonic by 31 cents, so the barbershop seventh chord can't be played in tune on a piano at all. The systems that do better on thirds are bigger: 19, 22, 31, 34 and 53.

Step 5 · Other divisionsNineteen, thirty-one, fifty-three

These systems have all been built and played. Below, each is a ring with one tick per step. The pure intervals sit outside it, joined to their nearest step and coloured by the miss. Each key underneath plays one step, so you can drag across to hear how fine the grid is, or play a scale and the chords each system does well.

19 equal has a fifth 7.2 cents narrow and a major third 7.4 cents narrow, so it trades a worse fifth for a much better third, and its minor third is within 0.15 cents of pure 6:5. In 1577 Francisco de Salinas proposed a meantone with a third of a syntonic comma per fifth, which is within a fraction of a cent of 19-equal.

31 equal is meantone that closes. Its fifth (−5.2) is almost quarter-comma meantone's (−5.4), its major third is 0.8 cents from pure, and its 7:4 is 1.1 cents from pure. Play the 4:5:6:7 chord here and then in 12: the seventh is the note that gives the difference away. In 1555 Nicola Vicentino proposed a 31-note extended meantone and had a harpsichord built to play it, and Christiaan Huygens described 31 equal steps in 1691. Adriaan Fokker revived it in the twentieth century, and the 31-tone organ he designed was inaugurated in Teylers Museum in Haarlem in 1950. Since 2009 it has been in the Muziekgebouw aan 't IJ in Amsterdam, where it is still used in concerts.

53 equal gets the fifth within 0.07 cents and the major third within 1.4, so it's nearly Pythagorean and nearly pure at once. Jing Fang's 53 fifths from Step 1 are why. William Holder wrote about it in 1694, and in the 1870s R. H. M. Bosanquet built a 53-step harmonium with a keyboard laid out to make it playable. Turkish makam theory measures intervals in commas, each one ninth of a whole tone, which is one step of 53.

The last option changes the period. The Bohlen–Pierce scale gives up the octave and repeats at 3:1, an octave and a fifth, which it splits into 13 equal steps of 146.3 cents. Its basic chord is 3:5:7, steps 0, 6 and 10, and its harmonics line up the way a major triad's do. Heinz Bohlen worked it out in 1972 (published 1978), Kees van Prooijen found it independently in 1978, and John Pierce found it again in the 1980s. Pierce and Max Mathews later gave it Bohlen's name too. It suits instruments whose sound has mostly odd harmonics, like the clarinet, where 3:1 plays the role 2:1 plays for everything else. Switch the timbre to all harmonics and the 3:5:7 chord gets noticeably muddier. The clarinet maker Stephen Fox has built Bohlen–Pierce clarinets.

Aside · Twenty-four

24 equal steps, quarter tones, are in the figure too. Since the early twentieth century Arabic music has notated its scales on a 24-step grid, with half-flats and half-sharps. The grid is a notation, not the tuning: the notes musicians play between the piano's keys are learned by ear and vary by region. Part five plays some of them.

EpilogueWhat carries over

The tuning problem comes back in every part of this series, so here's what to keep:

  • Pure intervals can't tile the octave. No number of fifths equals a number of octaves, so every fixed-pitch instrument has to put the error somewhere.
  • Errors hide in melodies and show up in chords. They come out as beats between harmonics, and the major third is where equal temperament pays.
  • Twelve is a good fraction. 7/12 is the first close approximation to the fifth's share of the octave, and the next one is 24/41.
  • There are other answers. 19, 31 and 53 steps, meantone and well temperaments, and a scale with no octave at all are all playable.

Things I'd try next: a keyboard that retunes every chord to pure ratios while you play it (several research instruments and commercial keyboards do this already); mapping the step player above onto a MIDI keyboard so 31-equal can be played with two hands; and measuring a real piano, whose stiff strings push the upper harmonics sharp so tuners stretch the octaves wider than 2:1.
