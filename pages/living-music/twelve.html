Living Music · Part 4

Twelve Imperfect Notes

Stack twelve pure fifths and you miss the starting octave by a quarter of a semitone, then have to shave every step to close the loop. Tap a dot on the spiral to hear it.

In part three we saw why simple frequency ratios like 2:1, 3:2, and 5:4 sound so calm together: their harmonics overlap neatly instead of rubbing against each other. If you sing or play the violin, you can nudge any pitch on the fly to hit those ratios cleanly. Once you build a piano, fret a guitar, or drill finger holes into a flute, though, every available pitch has to be decided ahead of time.

A natural first attempt is to start on C and generate the rest of the notes from the cleanest interval after the octave, the fifth. Multiply C by 3/2 to get G, multiply G by 3/2 to get D (dropping it an octave so it stays near home), and keep chaining outward. New notes keep showing up, and for a while it feels like the whole keyboard is going to build itself cleanly out of pure arithmetic. Then you reach the twelfth step and hit a wall that instrument makers have been negotiating since medieval organs, all the way up to a 31-tone concert organ still played in Amsterdam.

Step 1 · Stacked fifthsThe spiral that never closes

The spiral at the top of the page maps pitch onto polar coordinates: angle tracks where a note falls inside an octave (with C at twelve o'clock), and radius grows with height so that each full turn outward adds one octave. A pure 3:2 fifth spans 701.96 cents (where a cent is one hundredth of an equal-tempered semitone), which works out to 0.585 of a turn. Each time you stack another fifth, the point swings just over halfway around the circle and steps outward along the curve.

After twelve fifths, the trail has wound around seven times. You'd hope to land squarely back on C, seven octaves above where you started, but the arithmetic overshoots slightly: 

(3/2)¹² = 129.746        // twelve fifths
2⁷      = 128            // seven octaves
129.746 / 128 = 1.01364  // 23.46 cents: the Pythagorean comma

That 23.46-cent overshoot is the Pythagorean comma, roughly a quarter of a semitone. Adding more fifths only shifts the mismatch around, because getting a stack of fifths to match an exact number of octaves would mean solving 3m = 2n in positive integers. Any power of 3 is odd, and any power of 2 is even, so no two stacks can ever meet. Every fixed-pitch tuning system has to absorb that gap somewhere.

In the figure below, the unfolded spiral sits beside the same notes folded back into a single octave, along with a ruler showing every step size and how many cents each pitch sits away from the matching piano key.

Watch what happens to the step sizes as you click through the chain presets. At 5 notes (C, G, D, A, E), the octave divides into just two step widths, 203.9 and 294.1 cents: a pentatonic scale. At 7 notes you again get two sizes, five steps of 203.9 cents and two of 90.2 cents, which gives you the major scale and its relatives (the subject of part five). At 12 notes the octave splits into two slightly different semitones, 113.7 and 90.2 cents. Pick any count in between, say 8 notes, and you get three step sizes, never four or more. In mathematics this is known as the three-distance theorem: place points around a circle by repeating any fixed rotation, and the gaps between neighboring points can take at most three lengths, dropping to two at special counts.

Adding the 13th note, B♯, introduces a tiny third step size: a 23.46-cent sliver tucked right between C and B♯. That sliver is the Pythagorean comma viewed inside a single octave. Hit C against the 13th note to hear them together; near middle C the two pitches pulse against each other about 3.6 times a second.

If you drag the size of every fifth slider, all twelve intervals stretch or shrink together, so the endpoint moves twelve times as fast. Pull it down to 700.00 cents and B♯ merges cleanly onto C, closing the loop. Pull it further to 696.58 cents (a Renaissance tuning called quarter-comma meantone, coming up in Step 3) and the thirteenth note falls 41.06 cents short instead. Shaving every fifth by 1.96 cents so that twelve of them equal seven octaves is equal temperament, the compromise built into almost every modern piano, fretted guitar, and synthesizer.

Aside · The same chain, twice

Greek tradition attached this tuning to Pythagoras, though Chinese musical treatises from the third century BCE describe the exact same construction independently: cutting a pitch pipe to two-thirds of its length (up a fifth) and then extending it by a third (down a fourth) over and over to produce twelve tones within an octave. In the first century BCE, the Han dynasty theorist Jing Fang kept the chain going much further and discovered that 53 fifths return within a few cents of the starting note, a fact that reappears in Step 5.

Cents, the logarithm music runs on

Pitch perception tracks frequency ratios: jumping from 220 Hz to 440 Hz sounds like the exact same octave leap as 440 Hz to 880 Hz, even though the second jump covers twice as many hertz. Logarithms turn those multiplicative ratios into additive sizes. One cent is 1/1200 of an octave, so any ratio r measures 1200·log₂(r) cents. Equal-tempered semitones come out to a tidy 100 cents apiece, while pure ratios turn into irrational decimals: 701.955 for the 3:2 fifth, 386.314 for the 5:4 major third, and 315.641 for the 6:5 minor third.

Alexander Ellis introduced the cent in 1885 in a landmark survey measuring the pitches of instruments from around the world, including Javanese gamelan ensembles. After cataloging hundreds of scales in cents, he concluded that musical tuning varies widely across cultures rather than following a single universal law, something we'll hear firsthand in part five.

Step 2 · BeatsWhere the error becomes audible

Shaving 1.96 cents off a fifth is practically invisible in a single-note melody. Where you actually hear it, as part three showed, is in chords. Every sustained tone carries a ladder of harmonics at whole-number multiples of its fundamental frequency. When one note's harmonic lands a few hertz away from another note's harmonic, the two waves drift in and out of phase, causing the combined amplitude to swell and dip at the exact difference between their frequencies. Piano tuners listen straight for these beats and count them to set every interval on the keyboard.

Below, each note in the chord appears as its ladder of harmonics on a log-frequency axis. Near-collisions are linked by vertical lines with their beat rates marked, and the ribbon along the bottom traces one second of loudness for the selected collision (click any glowing dot to switch pairs). Hold the chord and flip between pure ratios and equal temperament to hear the difference directly.

Under pure ratios, every linked pair of harmonics locks onto the same frequency and the chord sits completely still. Flip to equal temperament on a C major triad and the harmonics split apart. For the fifth from middle C, C's 3rd harmonic sits at 784.88 Hz while G's 2nd sits at 783.99 Hz, producing a slow 0.89-beat-per-second sway that is easy to miss unless you listen closely. The major third is much rougher. Equal temperament puts E at 400 cents instead of the pure 386.3 cents, an error of 13.7 cents, seven times worse than the fifth. C's 5th harmonic and E's 4th miss by 10.4 Hz, giving a piano's C major chord a brisk ten-per-second shimmer. Meanwhile the minor third inside that same triad, between E and G, misses by 15.6 cents and beats 17.8 times a second at E×6 against G×5.

Try dragging the root note slider up and down. Because a fixed cent error means a fixed percentage difference in frequency, raising the chord by an octave doubles every beat rate. That scaling explains a lot about piano voicing: close chords in the middle and upper registers sparkle and buzz, whereas the same interval down low rolls along slowly.

Growing up around pianos and guitars trains your ear to accept that shimmer as normal. Even so, singers and string players routinely pull sustained thirds closer to 5:4 without thinking about it, and barbershop quartets chase pure alignment deliberately to make a chord "ring." Turning on A/B every 2 s for half a minute is the quickest way to hear how dramatic the contrast really is.

Step 3 · TemperamentWhere to hide the comma

Since twelve fifths have to squeeze into seven octaves, those twelve intervals must give up a combined 23.46 cents. Deciding how to distribute that deficit across the circle of fifths is called temperament, and different choices give every key on a keyboard a noticeably different sound.

Pythagorean tuning keeps eleven fifths completely pure and dumps the entire 23.46-cent shortfall into one leftover interval, usually between G♯ and E♭. That lopsided fifth howls so violently that organists nicknamed it the wolf. Stacking four pure fifths also pushes every major third up to 407.8 cents, 21.5 cents sharper than 5:4. Medieval polyphony treated octaves and fifths as resting places and thirds as tense passing sounds that needed to resolve, which suited a Pythagorean keyboard well.

Once Renaissance composers made the major third the backbone of harmony, 21.5-cent errors became unbearable. Four pure fifths overshoot a pure 5:4 third by a slightly smaller gap called the syntonic comma (81/64 versus 80/64, or 21.51 cents). In 1523 Pietro Aron described quarter-comma meantone, which narrows eleven fifths by a quarter of the syntonic comma (5.4 cents each). Four of those fifths land dead on a pure 5:4 major third, giving you eight gorgeously calm keys (E♭ through E on the circle of fifths). The tradeoff hits hard on the far side: the wolf fifth stretches out to 737.6 cents (+35.7 cents wide), and B, F♯, C♯, and G♯ major end up with thirds 41.1 cents sharp, keeping composers safely close to C.

By the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, theorists invented well temperaments to make all twelve keys playable without flattening them into identical copies. Andreas Werckmeister's popular 1691 system (Werckmeister III) narrows four fifths by a quarter of the Pythagorean comma and leaves the remaining eight pure. No wolf remains, and every key keeps a distinct flavor: C major's third is only 3.9 cents wide, whereas remote keys like F♯, C♯, and G♯ major inherit the full 21.5-cent Pythagorean bite. Johann Kirnberger and Francesco Vallotti later published their own variations on the same idea.

In the wheel above, the outer arcs show each fifth's deviation from pure (with any wolf drawn as a jagged coral line), while the inward bars and key circles track major-third error, shifting from green for pure through amber and red into magenta as the third stretches wider. In ¼-comma meantone, click around from C through G, D, A, and E to hear dead-still major triads, then click B, F♯, C♯, or G♯ to feel the tuning fall off a cliff. Switch to Werckmeister III and run tour the keys: the chords warm from serene to edgy and back again without ever breaking. Switch to equal and all twelve bars snap to the exact same +13.7-cent shimmer.

Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier (Book I, 1722) paired a prelude and fugue in all 24 major and minor keys for a keyboard tuned so every key worked. Historians still debate exactly which scheme Bach preferred; in 2005 harpsichordist Bradley Lehman proposed that a row of decorative loops on Bach's handwritten title page is actually a tuning diagram, an idea that sparked years of lively argument. Meanwhile eighteenth-century writers like Christian Schubart cataloged distinct emotional moods for every key. Habit and instrument ergonomics played a role, certainly, yet in a well temperament remote keys genuinely have faster-beating thirds and a more restless physical edge than home keys.

Aside · Who worked out equal temperament

Calculating twelve equal semitones requires the twelfth root of 2, and two scholars cracked it independently within about twenty years of each other. Ming dynasty prince Zhu Zaiyu published an accurate calculation of twelve equal ratios in 1584, and Flemish mathematician Simon Stevin derived the same numbers around 1605. Even with the math in hand, keyboard players stuck with meantone and well temperaments for another two centuries; equal temperament only became standard piano practice in the nineteenth century.

Step 4 · Why twelveDividing the octave

Equal steps let you modulate anywhere on the keyboard without hitting a bad chord, which raises an obvious follow-up: if we're slicing the octave into equal pieces, why stop at twelve? You can divide the octave into any whole number N of equal steps and ask how close its nearest pitch lands to a pure 3:2 fifth or a pure 5:4 major third. The chart below runs that check for every N from 5 to 53.

Dividing the octave into 5 or 7 equal steps leaves the fifth off by 18 and 16 cents. At 12 steps the fifth suddenly drops to 1.96 cents of error, and no larger division beats it until 29 (1.49 cents), 41 (0.48 cents), and 53 (0.07 cents). Twelve is the smallest equal grid that nails the fifth cleanly, which goes a long way toward explaining why it took over.

Number theory explains why those specific counts stand out. A pure fifth fills log₂(3/2) = 0.58496… of an octave, so placing a fifth at step p of an N-step scale means approximating that irrational number with the fraction p/N. The best rational approximations for any real number come from its continued-fraction expansion, and for log₂(3/2) those convergents are 1/2, 3/5, 7/12, 24/41, 31/53, and 179/306 (tagged right above their columns on the chart). The jump in accuracy at 7/12 is unusually steep for such a small denominator.

Twelve has much worse luck with the major third, missing 5:4 by 13.7 cents (the source of the 10.4-Hz shimmer in Step 2). Switch the toggle to + harmonic 7th (7:4) and 12 looks rougher still: its nearest step misses 7:4 by 31.2 cents, leaving the classic ringing barbershop seventh chord completely out of reach on a standard piano. Getting clean thirds and sevenths alongside decent fifths pushes you to larger divisions like 19, 22, 31, 34, and 53.

Step 5 · Other divisionsNineteen, thirty-one, fifty-three

Musicians and instrument builders have actually built keyboards, guitars, and pipe organs for all of these systems. Below, each tuning appears as a ring with one tick per equal step, while pure target ratios float just outside, linked to their closest step and colored by the size of the miss. Tap or drag across the keyboard strip underneath to hear how dense the grid feels, or audition each division's scale and signature chords.

19-equal gives up a little accuracy on the fifth (−7.2 cents) to cut the major-third error nearly in half (−7.4 cents), and its minor third lands within 0.15 cents of a pure 6:5. Way back in 1577, the Spanish theorist Francisco de Salinas described a third-comma meantone that matches 19-equal to within a tiny fraction of a cent.

31-equal behaves like quarter-comma meantone with the wolf removed. Its fifth (−5.2 cents) sits right next to meantone's (−5.4 cents), its major third is just 0.8 cents sharp of pure, and its harmonic seventh 7:4 comes within 1.1 cents. Play the 4:5:6:7 chord in 31-equal and then switch back to 12-equal; that seventh harmonic locks into place in 31 and grates immediately in 12. Nicola Vicentino commissioned a 31-note harpsichord in 1555, and Christiaan Huygens worked out the exact 31-equal logarithms in 1691. In the twentieth century physicist Adriaan Fokker revived the system and had a 31-tone pipe organ installed at Teylers Museum in Haarlem in 1950. Relocated in 2009 to the Muziekgebouw aan 't IJ in Amsterdam, that organ still hosts regular concerts today.

53-equal lands within 0.07 cents of a pure fifth and 1.4 cents of a pure major third, giving you practically pure fifths and thirds in the same equal grid thanks to Jing Fang's 53-fifth cycle from Step 1. William Holder analyzed it in 1694, and in the 1870s R. H. M. Bosanquet built a 53-note harmonium with a clever generalized keyboard so chords kept uniform shapes across all keys. Traditional Turkish makam theory still measures intervals in commas, defining a whole tone as nine commas, which corresponds directly to one step of 53-equal.

The final chip breaks with the octave altogether. The Bohlen–Pierce scale replaces the 2:1 octave with a 3:1 "tritave" (an octave plus a fifth) and divides that span into 13 equal steps of 146.3 cents. Its consonant triad is 3:5:7 (steps 0, 6, and 10), whose odd harmonics line up cleanly the way a major triad's harmonics do in an octave-based scale. Heinz Bohlen derived it in 1972 (publishing in 1978), Kees van Prooijen arrived at it independently the same year, and John Pierce rediscovered it in the 1980s alongside Max Mathews, later crediting Bohlen in its name. Because the scale drops the 2:1 octave, it pairs naturally with timbres dominated by odd harmonics like a clarinet's cylindrical bore. Switch the timbre from odd harmonics only to all harmonics while playing 3:5:7 and notice how quickly the even harmonics muddy the chord. Clarinet maker Stephen Fox has even built working Bohlen–Pierce clarinets.

Aside · Twenty-four

You can also try 24-equal (quarter tones) in the figure above. Since the early twentieth century, Arabic music theory has often written its scales on a 24-step grid using half-flats and half-sharps. In practice, that grid serves as shorthand on the page while performers tune those intermediate notes by ear to match the maqam and regional style, which we'll explore closely in part five.

EpilogueLiving with the gap

Every fixed-pitch instrument begins with the same stubborn arithmetic: powers of 3 never meet powers of 2, so pure intervals can never tile an octave cleanly. Melodies mostly hide the resulting compromise, whereas sustained chords expose it immediately through beating harmonics, especially in equal temperament's 13.7-cent-wide major thirds. Twelve notes won because 7/12 is an unusually tight fraction for such a compact keyboard, and the next clean leap for fifths doesn't arrive until 41 or 53 steps. Still, historical temperaments, meantone cousins like 19- and 31-equal, and even octave-free systems like Bohlen–Pierce show how wide the space of workable answers really is.

If I were extending this page further, three experiments sit near the top of my list: a dynamic-tuning keyboard that listens to whatever notes you hold and nudges each chord onto pure ratios in real time (several software instruments and research keyboards already do this); wiring the step player above to a physical MIDI controller so 31-equal can be played two-handed; and recording a real acoustic piano, whose stiff steel strings push upper harmonics sharp enough that tuners intentionally stretch octaves slightly wider than 2:1.
