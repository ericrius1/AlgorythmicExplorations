Living Music · Part 8

The Accompanist

A band in the browser that listens before it plays. The rings above light up with how well each of the 24 keys fits what it has heard so far.

The circle at the top of the page is listening. A stand-in player is working its way through a slow tour of keys, and the ring lights up the key it thinks the music is in. Tap it and a band joins in: bass, electric piano, brushes, and a vibraphone that answers back. Then take over. Tap the inner circle to play a note, tap a key's name to play its chord, or type on your computer keyboard.

Part seven ended with a band that writes plausible music and can't hear anything. This one runs the other way: notes come in, theory gets applied, notes go out, quickly enough to feel like playing with somebody. Nothing here listens to a microphone. Your notes arrive as events, key down and key up with a pitch and a time, so there's no pitch detection to get wrong. What's left are three questions a human accompanist answers without thinking about them: what key are we in, what chord is this, and how much should I play?

Step 1 · The dataWhat listeners hear

A key isn't written anywhere in the notes. It's an impression that builds up as you listen. In 1982 Carol Krumhansl and Edward Kessler measured that impression. They played musically trained listeners something that sets up a key, a tonic chord or a short cadence, followed by a single probe tone, and asked them to rate on a scale of 1 to 7 how well the probe fit. After all twelve probes, in major and in minor, they had two key profiles: how much each scale degree belongs to the key.

You can run the same test on yourself. Each question plays a I–IV–V–I cadence in C, then one note. Rate it, and your answer lands on the chart next to the 1982 average. As in the original study, every sound is a Shepard tone: one note name spread over six octaves under a bell-shaped loudness curve, so you're judging which note it is and not how high it is.

If your profile looks like theirs, the tonic C is near the top, G and E come next, the rest of the C major scale sits in the middle, and the five notes outside the scale are at the bottom. The minor context gives a different shape: E♭ jumps up, E drops, and A♭ rises. Nobody told the listeners which notes were in the scale. The hierarchy came out of what they heard.

Step 2 · The keyFinding the key

The profiles turn key finding into a matching problem. Count how much of each pitch class you've heard, then compare that histogram with the major profile rotated to all twelve tonics and the minor profile rotated the same way. That's 24 candidate keys. The comparison is a correlation, and the best-correlated key wins. Krumhansl and Mark Schmuckler published this as an algorithm in Krumhansl's 1990 book, weighting each note by how long it sounds, and most key finders since have been built on it.

A live player needs one more thing, a memory that fades. Notes from a minute ago shouldn't count as much as notes from a second ago, or the ear could never follow a key change. So the histogram decays: 

// every frame, dt seconds since the last one
hist[pc] *= 2 ** (-dt / 5)          // half-life of 5 seconds
hist[pc] += 0.5                     // when a note starts
hist[pc] += 0.45 * dt               // for as long as it's held

r[key] = correlation(hist, profile turned to key's tonic)   // × 24 keys

In the figure, the bars are the histogram and the blue dots are the winning key's profile. Both are drawn as distance from their own average, which is what a correlation compares: where the bars and the dots point the same way, the match gets better. The grid on the right scores every key at once.

Start with Twinkle. After its first four notes, C C G G, C minor is a close second, because there's no third yet to say major or minor; the A on note five settles it. The tune never plays a B or a B♭, the one note that would separate C major from F major, and C still wins easily because the melody keeps landing on C and G, the two highest points of the major profile. The A minor tune shows the minor profile at work. A minor and C major share all seven notes, so what separates them is which notes get the weight, plus the G♯ that only the minor tune uses.

The third tune plays a phrase in C and then the same phrase twice in E♭. Watch the grid during the switch. The ear doesn't go straight to E♭ major. For a few seconds its memory holds the old C and G alongside the new E♭ and A♭, and that mix is a C minor scale, so C minor takes over first (with a shorter or longer memory, G minor or B♭ major gets a turn too). E♭ major wins once the C major notes have faded, about nine seconds after the first E♭. None of those changes happens instantly, because I added some hysteresis: the current key keeps its place until another key has beaten it by 0.05 for a second and a half (the green bar fills while that happens). Without it, one borrowed chord would send the bass player to a new key for a bar. The last button plays all twelve notes at once. The histogram is flat, every correlation is zero, and the ear declines to name a key, which is the right answer.

The memory slider is a real trade-off. Short memories follow a modulation quickly and lose the key during a chromatic run. Long memories hold steady and lag. Five seconds is about two bars at the band's tempo, which felt right to me when I played with it.

Aside · A map of keys

Krumhansl and Kessler also correlated the 24 profiles with each other. Keys a fifth apart came out similar, and so did each major key and its relative and parallel minors. Laid out in space by similarity, the keys fall around the circle of fifths, and Krumhansl and Kessler found the arrangement wraps onto the surface of a torus. The hero at the top of this page is a flat version of that map: majors around the outside in fifths order, each relative minor just inside it.

Better profiles, and what this method can't do

David Temperley revisited the algorithm in 1999 and proposed adjusted profiles and a version that judges short segments with a penalty for changing key, which did better on his test set. Later work fit profiles to large corpora of scores instead of listener ratings. I kept the 1982 numbers because they come from what people heard, and the figure above is about that.

Any method built on a histogram ignores order. C–E–G and G–E–C count the same, and so does a melody played backwards. Order matters to listeners (a G going to C says "C major" more strongly than C going to G), so a better ear would also look at intervals and where the notes fall in the bar.

Step 3 · The chordNaming the chord

The key changes every few phrases. The chord changes every bar or two, so the chord ear uses a second, much faster memory, with a half-life of 0.9 seconds, plus whatever you're holding down right now. It scores every triad, 12 roots times major, minor and diminished, against that evidence: 

fit   = (1.1·ev[root] + 1.0·ev[third] + 0.75·ev[fifth] − 0.6·ev[other notes]) / Σ ev

score = fit
      + 0.10 if the chord belongs to the key     // + 0.04 more for I, IV and V
      − 0.08 if it's diminished                  // − 0.12 if it's outside the key too
      + 0.06 if it's already playing             // changing has to be worth it
      + 0.03 if its root is a fifth below        // V→I, ii→V: the strongest move

The fit term does most of the work. Notes that aren't in the chord count against it, so a chord can't win just by containing your notes. The fifth counts least because it's shared by the most chords. The other terms are the key prior, small nudges that only matter when the evidence is ambiguous, which is often. Two notes are rarely enough to name a chord.

Try the examples. D and A, with no third, could be D major or D minor, and the fit can't tell them apart. The prior decides: in C major it's D minor (ii), in G major it's D major (V), and with the prior off the two tie. A and C is A minor rather than F major, because C is A minor's third but only F major's fifth. G B D F is G7: the F counts against G major's fit, but nothing else explains the other three notes as well. B D F is a real diminished chord and gets named as one. B♭ D F isn't in C major at all and still wins, because the evidence outweighs the prior. That's how borrowed chords like ♭VII get through. In A minor, E G♯ B comes out as V, because I let minor keys use the major dominant, as most minor-key music does.

The band decides on a chord twice a bar, on beats one and three. It will also change on beats two and four, but only if you're holding at least two notes and they fit a different chord clearly. That keeps the harmony from twitching under a melody while still letting you push a chord change by playing it.

Step 4 · The bandVoicing, bass and brushes

A chord name still isn't music. Somebody has to decide which notes to play, in which octave, and when. I gave the band four players.

The pianist plays rootless voicings: the third, fifth, seventh and ninth of the chord, with no root. That's how jazz pianists usually voice chords when a bassist already has the root, and it sounds fuller than a triad while using the same four fingers. The sevenths come from the key, so ii gets a minor seventh, V a dominant seventh, and I a major seventh. Where the ninth would clash with the scale (on iii, for example) the root goes back in. Each new chord is voiced the way part six described: try every inversion with the bottom note between E3 and D♯4, and keep the one whose fingers move the least from the last chord.

The bassist plays the root on beat one and the fifth on beat three, what jazz players call a two-feel, between C2 and B2. Now and then it adds a pickup a half step from the root on the last eighth of the bar.

The drummer plays brushes: a ride cymbal on every beat with a swung skip into beats two and four, a brush swish on two and four, and a soft kick on one and three. The off-beat eighths are pushed late, 60% of the way to a full triplet swing.

All of them follow one rule about how much to play, and it's the rule a human rhythm section follows most: the busier the soloist, the quieter the band. 

notesPerSecond = your notes in the last 4 seconds / 4

style = notesPerSecond < 0.7 ? "fill"   // arpeggios into the space you're leaving
      : notesPerSecond < 2.6 ? "comp"   // short chords, on and off the beat
      :                        "hold"   // long pads, so someone holds the floor

The style is re-read at the start of every bar. You'll see it switch in the last figure as you play more or less.

Step 5 · ConversationCall and response

Comping keeps the harmony going. What makes it feel like a bandmate is that it says something back. When you play a phrase and stop, the vibraphone answers. A phrase counts as finished after at least three notes and a beat of silence. The answer comes in at the same spot in the bar where your call started, one phrase later, so call and answer line up like the two halves of a sentence.

The answer borrows your rhythm, snapped to eighth notes and cut to one bar at most. For the pitches there are four options, and all of them move in scale steps rather than semitones, so the answer stays in the key:

  • Echo repeats your phrase, moved so it starts on a tone of the current chord.
  • Mirror turns your contour upside down: where you went up a third, it goes down a third.
  • Sequence repeats your phrase a third higher, the oldest way to develop a tune.
  • Riff improvises from your habits. Every interval you play goes into a table of which step tends to follow which, and the riff walks that table.

Then two rules clean it up. Notes on the beat are moved to the nearest chord tone, and the last note resolves to a chord tone and rings. Without them the answers sounded like random notes in the right key. With them they sound like replies. If you start playing again in the middle of an answer, the band stops talking.

The example phrases are handy for hearing the four modes side by side. Pick one mode, play the same call a few times, then switch. Echo and sequence are the most obviously "yours". Mirror is the one that surprised me: it often sounds like a real answer, a rising question followed by a falling reply. Riff starts out vague and gets more like you the longer you play, as the table fills up.

Aside · Machine bandmates

People have built these for forty years. Roger Dannenberg's 1984 score follower accompanied a live soloist through a written piece, tracking where in the score the player was. Robert Rowe's Cypher and George Lewis's Voyager, both developed through the 1980s and 90s, went the other way, toward free improvisation. Lewis, a trombonist, designed Voyager to act as an independent player with its own ideas rather than a follower. In 2002 François Pachet's Continuator learned a player's style from their phrases with variable-order Markov chains and answered in kind. The riff mode here is a very small cousin of it: one step of memory instead of many.

Step 6 · All togetherThe whole band

Here's everything at once. Tap the figure, then play the on-screen keys or your computer keyboard: A S D F G H J K are the white keys from middle C, W E T Y U the black keys, and Z/X shift the octave. Any MIDI keyboard works too, after you click the MIDI button and allow it. Your notes are white, the bass violet, the piano amber, the vibraphone's answers green. Everything right of the line hasn't happened yet: the band plans half a bar ahead, so you can watch its next notes coming.

A few things to try. Hold a chord for a bar and watch the band settle into it. Play sparse single notes and it fills in arpeggios; play fast and it backs off to pads, and the meter in the corner shows why. Play a phrase in C, then one that leans on F♯, and watch the key name change after the hysteresis lets it. If you don't want to play, the stand-in button hands the keys to the same player that's in the hero.

It has two big limits. The band keeps the time and you follow it. Following a player's tempo, with rubato, is the problem Dannenberg was solving, and this doesn't try. And the harmony is greedy: it picks the best chord for the last second, with no idea of form. It will never hold the IV back for the chorus or set up a deceptive cadence. Both come from what I put in. The band doesn't understand music. It runs the assumptions I wrote down, and some of them came from Krumhansl's listeners.

EpilogueWhat the series built

A few lessons from this part:

  • Ask listeners, then fit the data. The key profiles are the core of the ear, and they came from people rating notes, not from a theory book.
  • Memory is a design choice. Two fading histograms with different half-lives give a slow sense of key and a fast sense of chord from the same notes.
  • Priors break ties. The key prior only matters when the evidence is thin, and with two or three notes it usually is.
  • Restraint is a feature. The density seesaw is one line of code, and it does more for the feel than any of the voicing work.

Things I'd try next: follow the player's tempo instead of a fixed clock; learn chord progressions so the band can anticipate the next chord instead of reacting to the last one; give the riff mode a longer memory, closer to the Continuator; and add an ear for intervals and metre, so G–C means more than C–G.

That's the end of Living Music. It started with a single note: a vibrating string, and the three numbers that describe what you hear. Then oscillators and filters to make any sound; why some intervals are smooth and some rough; why the octave got twelve notes; scales and modes; triads and voice leading; a generative band; and now one that listens. Every part was already in the accompanist. The pitch classes are part four's twelve notes, the key profiles rate part five's scales, the voicings are part six's triads, and the band's clock comes from the jukebox. It ends as a band that needs one more player.
