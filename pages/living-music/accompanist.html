The Accompanist

A small band in the browser that listens before it plays. The glowing rings above score all twenty-four keys against what the ear has heard so far.

Sit down beside a good jazz pianist or bassist, play four or five notes, and watch how fast they lock in. Within a couple of seconds they have already settled on a key, inferred a plausible chord under your fingers, and judged whether to lay back or step into the empty space you leave. Try it on the compass above: a stand-in player is currently strolling around the circle of fifths while the rings track its key. Tap the canvas and the band comes alive with bass, electric piano, brushed drums, and a vibraphone ready to trade phrases. Once the sound is running, jump right in by tapping the inner spokes for single notes, tapping any key wedge around the rings to strike its tonic chord, or playing on your computer keyboard.

In part seven we built a generative band that composes and mixes whole songs from a seed, completely deaf to the outside world. Here we flip the pipeline around: notes flow in, theory runs in real time, and accompaniment flows back out fast enough to feel conversational. Rather than wrestling with microphone pitch detection, every note arrives as a clean key-down and key-up event carrying an exact MIDI pitch and timestamp. That strips accompaniment down to three musical questions a human bandmate solves almost subconsciously: what key are we in, what chord fits right now, and how busy should the band be?

Step 1 · The dataWhat listeners hear

Musical key lives in the listener's head, accumulated over a few seconds of notes rather than stamped onto any single pitch. In 1982, psychologists Carol Krumhansl and Edward Kessler set out to measure that mental hierarchy directly. They played musically trained listeners a short context that firmly established a key (a tonic chord or brief cadence), followed by a single probe tone, and asked them to rate on a 1-to-7 scale how well that final note fit. Repeating the question across all twelve chromatic pitch classes in both major and minor contexts gave them two empirical key profiles, capturing how strongly each scale degree feels like it belongs.

You can take their exact test below. Each trial plays a four-chord cadence in C followed by one probe note; click a rating from 1 to 7 and your dot drops onto the graph alongside the 1982 group average. Just as in the original experiment, every note is synthesized as a Shepard tone (the same pitch class layered across six octaves under a bell-shaped loudness envelope), which removes octave height as a cue so you judge the note's tonal fit alone.

Most listeners produce a curve surprisingly close to Krumhansl and Kessler's bars. In C major, C sits clearly on top, G and E form the next tier, the other four diatonic notes cluster through the middle, and the five chromatic notes sink to the bottom. Flip the context toggle to minor and the shape rearranges itself: E♭ leaps toward the top, E drops sharply, and A♭ climbs up. Nobody handed the 1982 participants a scale diagram; that whole multi-tiered hierarchy came straight out of their ears.

Step 2 · The keyFinding the key

Once you have those two twelve-number profiles, identifying a key becomes template matching. Tally how strongly each of the twelve pitch classes has appeared in the music, then correlate that histogram against the major profile shifted to all twelve tonics and the minor profile shifted the same way. That yields 24 correlation scores, and the highest correlation names the key. Krumhansl and Mark Schmuckler introduced this method in Krumhansl's 1990 book, weighting each note by its duration, and decades of MIR key-finding systems trace back to it.

For live playing, the ear also needs a fading memory. Notes you played two minutes ago shouldn't drag down a key change you just made, so the histogram decays exponentially every frame while active notes keep topping it up: 

// every frame, dt seconds since the last one
hist[pc] *= 2 ** (-dt / 5)          // half-life of 5 seconds
hist[pc] += 0.5                     // when a note starts
hist[pc] += 0.45 * dt               // for as long as it's held

r[key] = correlation(hist, profile turned to key's tonic)   // × 24 keys

On the left side of the figure below, the bars show the ear's fading pitch-class memory and the blue curve shows the winning key's profile. Both are plotted as deviations above and below their own average, which is literally what Pearson correlation measures: whenever tall bars line up with high dots and missing notes line up with low dots, the score climbs toward 1. On the right, all twenty-four keys light up live in circle-of-fifths order.

Try Twinkle (C major) first. Through the opening four notes (C, C, G, G), C minor trails close behind because no third has appeared yet; the A on note five breaks the tie. Notice that Twinkle never once plays B or B♭, the sole note separating C major from F major, yet C major wins comfortably because the tune keeps leaning on C and G, the two tallest peaks in the major profile. Next try a tune in A minor: A minor and C major share the same seven white keys, so the ear separates them by which pitches carry the weight, helped along by the leading tone G♯ at the cadences.

The third button, C major, then E♭ major, shows how a fading memory handles a real modulation. Watch the grid as the melody jumps into E♭. Instead of teleporting straight to E♭ major, the ear briefly holds fading C and G alongside fresh E♭ and A♭, a blend that resembles C minor, so C minor claims the lead for a few seconds (and depending on the half-life slider, G minor or B♭ major can take a brief turn too) before E♭ major wins out roughly nine seconds after the switch. To keep a single passing chromatic chord from yanking the bass player into another key for one measure, the ear uses hysteresis: a challenger has to beat the current key by at least 0.05 for a full 1.5 seconds (tracked by the green progress bar under the tile) before taking over. Finally, hit all twelve notes at once: the histogram flattens out, every correlation collapses to zero, and the ear sensibly refuses to guess a key.

Dragging memory half-life reveals an unavoidable engineering trade-off. A short half-life pivots quickly on modulations, yet gets easily spooked by chromatic runs; a long half-life stays rock steady and lags behind real key changes. Five seconds works out to around two bars at the band's default tempo, which struck a comfortable balance when I played against it.

Aside · A map of keys

Krumhansl and Kessler also correlated all 24 key profiles against one another. Keys a perfect fifth apart scored high similarity, as did every major key paired with its relative and parallel minors. Embedding those pairwise similarities into geometry arranges the keys along the circle of fifths and wraps the whole neighborhood onto the surface of a torus. The hero compass at the top of this page flattens that torus into two concentric rings: major keys around the outside in fifths order, each relative minor tucked right inside it.

Better profiles, and what this method can't do

In 1999, David Temperley refined the algorithm with adjusted profiles and a dynamic segmentation pass that penalizes frequent key switches, improving accuracy across classical corpora; later systems fit profiles directly from large databases of scores rather than behavioral ratings. I kept the original 1982 numbers here because they come directly from human listeners, tying step one straight into the live band.

One blind spot affects every histogram-based key finder: order disappears. Playing C–E–G produces the exact same histogram as G–E–C, and reversing an entire melody leaves every correlation untouched. Real ears care heavily about direction and meter (a G resolving to C signals C major much more strongly than C drifting up to G), so a more complete model would score intervals and metric placement alongside pitch duration.

Step 3 · The chordNaming the chord

While a key usually holds steady across several phrases, chords turn over every bar or two. To track harmony at that faster pace, the ear maintains a second pitch-class histogram with a much shorter half-life of 0.9 seconds, summed together with whatever keys you are holding down at this instant. Every one of the 36 basic triads (twelve roots across major, minor, and diminished) gets scored against that evidence: 

fit   = (1.1·ev[root] + 1.0·ev[third] + 0.75·ev[fifth] − 0.6·ev[other notes]) / Σ ev

score = fit
      + 0.10 if the chord belongs to the key     // + 0.04 more for I, IV and V
      − 0.08 if it's diminished                  // − 0.12 if it's outside the key too
      + 0.06 if it's already playing             // changing has to be worth it
      + 0.03 if its root is a fifth below        // V→I, ii→V: the strongest move

The raw evidence fit carries the main weight. Any active note outside a triad's three pitches counts negatively, preventing a chord from winning simply because it overlaps one or two notes, while the fifth carries the smallest bonus since so many chords share it. The remaining terms form a lightweight key prior, gentle tie-breakers that step in whenever the notes under your hands leave room for interpretation (which happens constantly when you play single-line melodies or open two-note intervals).

Click through the six examples to watch the prior and the evidence negotiate. D + A lacks a third, so D major and D minor tie on raw fit; the key prior breaks the deadlock, picking Dm (ii) in C major, D (V) in G major, and leaving them dead even when you switch key prior to off. A + C goes to A minor over F major because C serves as A minor's third (weight 1.0) rather than F major's fifth (weight 0.75). G B D F is a dominant seventh: the extra F subtracts a little from G major's fit, yet G still outperforms every rival across the other three notes. B D F overcomes the diminished penalty to win cleanly as B°, and B♭ D F wins even in C major because strong evidence overrides the diatonic bonus, letting borrowed chords like ♭VII through naturally. Switch key to A minor and try E G♯ B: it shows up outlined as V because the minor key table admits the major dominant used in most minor-key music.

In the full band, harmony updates on beats one and three of every bar. It will also switch on beats two and four, provided you are actively holding at least two keys that point unmistakably to a new chord. That compromise prevents passing melody notes from rattling the accompaniment while letting you steer quick chord changes whenever you grab them.

Step 4 · The bandVoicing, bass and brushes

Knowing the chord symbol is only the starting point; turning "Dm" into live accompaniment requires deciding which octave each instrument occupies, which extensions to add, and how often to strike. The backing section splits those jobs across three rhythm-section players.

The pianist comps with rootless four-note voicings: the third, fifth, seventh, and ninth of the chord, leaving the root out because the bassist already owns the bottom end. Sevenths come straight from the active key (so ii receives a minor seventh, V a dominant seventh, and I a major seventh), and whenever the major ninth would clash with the key's scale (such as on iii), the voicing folds the root back in. Every chord change runs through the minimum-motion voice-leading search from part six, testing each inversion with its bottom note between E3 and D♯4 and picking whichever sits closest to the previous hand position.

The bassist anchors the floor between C2 and B2 with a relaxed jazz two-feel, landing on the root on beat one and the fifth on beat three, occasionally slipping in a chromatic pickup a half step away from the root on the final eighth note of the bar.

The drummer plays a quiet brush kit: a ride cymbal on every beat with a swung skip leading into beats two and four, a soft brush swish on the backbeats, and a featherweight kick on one and three. Off-beat eighths are delayed 60% of the way toward a full triplet swing.

The density seesaw ties all three players together around one simple rule that good human rhythm sections follow instinctively: leave space when the soloist is busy, and step forward when they pause. 

notesPerSecond = your notes in the last 4 seconds / 4

style = notesPerSecond < 0.7 ? "fill"   // arpeggios into the space you're leaving
      : notesPerSecond < 2.6 ? "comp"   // short chords, on and off the beat
      :                        "hold"   // long pads, so someone holds the floor

The band re-checks your note rate at the top of each bar, shifting smoothly between arpeggiated fills, syncopated comping, and sustained pads as your playing speeds up or thins out.

Step 5 · ConversationCall and response

Solid comping gives you a floor to stand on, while call and response makes the band feel like a partner listening back. Whenever you play a phrase of at least three notes and then leave a full beat of silence, the vibraphone waits until the corresponding beat of the next phrase and plays a reply, lining up call and answer like the two halves of a spoken exchange.

Every reply borrows your call's rhythm, quantized onto an eighth-note grid and trimmed to at most one bar. For the pitches, the vibraphone works in scale degrees rather than raw semitones so its replies stay inside the key, choosing among four melodic transformations:

  • Echo shifts your phrase vertically so it opens on a tone of the current chord while preserving your contour.
  • Mirror flips your melodic contour upside down around its opening pitch, turning every rising third into a falling third.
  • Sequence transposes your motif up a diatonic third, one of the oldest devices for extending a musical idea.
  • Riff improvises from your own melodic habits: every scale-step interval you play updates a small first-order transition table, and the vibraphone walks that table to spin a reply in your style.

Two finishing touches pull these replies together: notes landing on strong beats snap to the closest chord tone, and the final note always resolves onto a chord tone and rings out. Before I added those two constraints, the replies sounded like generic scale noodling; with them in place, they land with clear harmonic punctuation. If you jump back in before the vibraphone finishes, it immediately yields the floor and cancels its remaining notes.

The two preset phrases (a phrase in C and a phrase in A minor) make it easy to compare the four modes back to back under answer by. Echo and sequence feel immediately recognizable as variations on your own line. Mirror turned out to be the nicest surprise: flipping a climbing question into a descending contour often sounds genuinely conversational. Riff starts off generic on the first few phrases and gradually takes on your fingerprints as your interval table fills up.

Aside · Machine bandmates

Interactive computer accompanists go back more than four decades. Roger Dannenberg's 1984 real-time score follower tracked a live soloist through a written piece and adapted its tempo to match. Around the same time and into the 1990s, Robert Rowe's Cypher and trombonist George Lewis's Voyager explored free improvisation, with Lewis deliberately designing Voyager as an autonomous peer with its own musical agenda rather than a passive follower. In 2002, François Pachet built The Continuator, which learned a pianist's phrasing on the fly using variable-order Markov chains and immediately traded licks in their voice. Our riff mode is a tiny single-step cousin of that idea.

Start by holding a slow three-note chord for a bar and watch the key label and chord symbol snap into focus in the upper right. Leave generous gaps between sparse single notes and the bottom-right meter stays in fill while the piano threads gentle arpeggios through the empty space; play a fast flurry of notes and the meter swings into hold as the band steps back onto quiet sustained pads. Play a few bars in C major, then lean into F♯ and D to nudge the ear toward G major and watch the hysteresis wait before committing. If you would rather just listen and watch the mechanics run, click stand-in plays to hand the keyboard back to the hero's automatic soloist.

Playing with it for a few minutes also makes its two main boundaries obvious. First, the band runs on a fixed clock, so you follow its groove rather than pulling it around with expressive rubato (the exact tempo-tracking problem Dannenberg tackled). Second, its harmonic sense is purely local: it grabs the best chord for the last second of evidence without any awareness of verse-chorus form, so it can never anticipate a bridge or hold back a resolution for dramatic effect. Both reflect the simplicity of the model: every move comes directly from fading histograms, heuristic chord priors, and the empirical profiles gathered from Krumhansl's listeners.

EpilogueWhat the series built

Building an accompanist from scratch highlights a few recurring principles. Grounding the key finder in actual listener ratings beats hand-coded textbook heuristics, and splitting pitch memory into two decay rates (a slow five-second window for key and a quick 0.9-second window for harmony) pulls two timescales of structure out of the exact same note stream. Small priors matter far more than you'd expect, because live playing spends most of its time in ambiguous two- or three-note territory where pure template fit ties. And the one-line density seesaw that quiets the band whenever you play faster contributes as much to the musical feel as all the rootless jazz voicings put together.

Given more time, the next features on my wish list would be an adaptive beat tracker so the band can follow a soloist's rubato, a chord transition model so it can anticipate where a progression is heading instead of always reacting half a bar behind, a deeper multi-step Markov memory for the vibraphone closer to Pachet's Continuator, and an ear sensitive to melodic intervals and meter so G moving to C carries more tonal weight than C moving to G.

That brings Living Music to a close. Eight essays ago, we started with one vibrating string and the three basic dimensions of a single tone, moved through oscillators, filters, sensory roughness, the math behind twelve-tone tuning, scales and modes, triads and voice leading, and a self-contained generative jukebox, arriving finally at a band that listens back. Nearly every earlier part is still running quietly inside this last figure: the twelve pitch classes from part four, the scale hierarchies from part five, the voice-led triads from part six, and the swung look-ahead clock from part seven, all waiting for a human pair of hands on the keys.
