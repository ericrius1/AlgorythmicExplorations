Living Music · Part 7

The Infinite Jukebox

Rhythm from Euclid's algorithm, timing with a human drift, melodies that remember what they just sang, and a generative band that turns a single seed number into a full song. Tap the rings above to start it; when one song wraps up, it writes the next.

Six essays in, we have built a vibrating note, a synthesizer, sensory consonance, twelve-tone tuning, scales, and chords. Everything so far lives purely in pitch space, frozen in place. Putting music into motion turns out to borrow ideas from surprising places: a neutron accelerator in Tennessee, high-speed measurements of jazz drummers' wrists, and a handful of statistical habits shared by folk melodies all over the world.

Those concentric rings spinning at the top of the page are the drum patterns for the current song, one ring per instrument, orbiting the active chord in the center with the song's form traced around the outer rim. Every pattern on those rings comes straight out of the algorithm in step one, and by step five the entire band (drums, bass, keys, pad, and lead) comes together behind a single function called writeSong(seed).

Step 1 · Where the hits goEuclid's drummer

Suppose you divide a bar into n equal ticks and want to place k drum hits across them. When k divides n cleanly, the answer takes no thought: four hits across sixteen steps gives four on the floor. When k doesn't divide n, the gaps can't all match, so the interesting problem is spreading the hits as evenly as the grid permits. Put three hits on eight steps and the cleanest split is gaps of 3, 3, and 2 (x..x..x.). Play that out loud and you immediately recognize the Cuban tresillo, the backbone of huge stretches of Latin, dancehall, and pop music.

In 2003, computer scientist Eric Bjorklund wrote a neat little procedure for this exact spacing problem while designing timing gates for the Spallation Neutron Source at Oak Ridge National Laboratory. Start with k one-step groups containing a hit and n − k one-step groups containing a rest. Pair off as many leftover groups from the back as you can, tucking one onto the end of each front group. Repeat with whatever remains unpaired until at most one leftover group sits at the tail. Notice what the group counts do on each pass: they shrink by the exact remainder steps of Euclid's ancient algorithm for the greatest common divisor. The right side of the figure below traces every pass, while the circle on the left bends the final pattern into a polygon.

Two years later, Godfried Toussaint noticed that Bjorklund's accelerator algorithm rediscovers an astonishing catalog of traditional rhythms from around the globe. E(3,8) is the Cuban tresillo and E(5,8) is the Cuban cinquillo. E(7,12) is the classic West African bell pattern over a twelve-pulse cycle, E(5,12) matches a clapping pattern of the Venda people in South Africa, and E(7,16) is a Brazilian samba pattern. Whenever your slider settings land on a rhythm from Toussaint's paper, the center of the circle labels it. A few traditional patterns only match once you shift which hit counts as beat one: E(5,16) becomes the bossa nova clave when you turn the necklace with the rotate slider, leaving the spacing around the circle untouched while changing where the downbeat falls.

Click bell pattern E(7,12) and look at the gaps printed below the algorithm: 2, 1, 2, 2, 1, 2, 2. Rotate that sequence by two steps and you get 2, 2, 1, 2, 2, 2, 1, the exact step sizes of the major scale from part five. Spreading seven pitches as evenly as possible around twelve semitones and spreading seven bell strokes as evenly as possible around twelve time pulses are mathematically identical problems, so they produce the same object. Theorists John Clough and Jack Douthett called these solutions maximally even sets, and Jeff Pressing pointed out the bridge between the West African bell and the diatonic scale back in 1983. Every Euclidean rhythm shares the signature property reported beneath the stages: at most two gap sizes, always one step apart.

The algorithm, as coded 
// k one-hit groups, n − k one-rest groups
let front = Array.from({ length: k }, () => [1]);
let back = Array.from({ length: n - k }, () => [0]);
while (back.length > 1) {
  const pairs = Math.min(front.length, back.length);
  const merged = [];
  for (let p = 0; p < pairs; p++) merged.push([...front[p], ...back[p]]);
  // whatever didn't get a partner is the new remainder
  const rest = front.length > pairs ? front.slice(pairs) : back.slice(pairs);
  front = merged;
  back = rest;
}

On E(3,8), that loop finishes in three passes: three hits and five rests, then [x.][x.][x.][.][.], then [x..][x..][x.]. The generative band further down the page calls this same function for its hi-hats, bells, congas, and clave.

Step 2 · Two pulses at onceThree against two

Euclidean rhythms pack uneven gaps onto a single shared grid. A polyrhythm goes the other way, stacking two completely even pulses inside the same cycle even though neither divides the other. Play three evenly spaced hits against two evenly spaced hits and the two drums only coincide on beat one. To line both pulses up on one common ruler, you need a grid of six (their least common multiple), and combining both tracks on that grid weaves an uneven composite rhythm called the resultant: gaps of 2, 1, 1, and 2.

Let 3 : 2 loop for a few bars and try shifting your attention between the amber low drum and the blue high drum. Your brain can lock onto either pulse as the main beat, flipping back and forth like a Necker cube, or relax and hear the combined 2 + 1 + 1 + 2 phrase as one syncopated pattern. The low drum and high drum buttons act as quick mutes if you want to isolate one pulse, let it settle into your head, and then bring the other back in. Higher ratios get denser fast: 4 : 3 needs a grid of 12 (resultant 3 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 3), and by 5 : 4 (grid of 20) the composite pattern starts to stretch past what most listeners can track as a single groove.

Three against two shows up constantly in twelve-pulse music because twelve factors cleanly into both four groups of three and six groups of two. In the generative band's 12/8 bell groove style, the seven-stroke Euclidean bell rides directly over a kick hammering four beats of three, with a shaker ticking all twelve subdivisions and a conga on a rotated E(5,12).

Step 3 · FeelSwing and the human wobble

Up to now every note has landed on exact mathematical grid lines. Real musicians never hit dead on the grid, and two very different kinds of offset separate a groove from a clock. The first is intentional: swing pushes every second subdivision slightly late, stretching even pairs of notes into long–short pairs. At 50% the pair is dead even; at 67% the first note takes two-thirds of the pair (a 2:1 ratio), giving the rolling triplet shuffle of jazz and blues. The swing slider below runs from 50% to 75% and prints both the percentage and the long-to-short ratio.

Even among jazz drummers, swing isn't locked to a single number. Anders Friberg and Andreas Sundström measured recorded ride-cymbal patterns across tempos and found that drummers swing dramatically harder at slow tempos (often well past 2:1) and straighten out toward even eighths as the tempo climbs. Our generative band borrows that habit: its slow lo-fi style (72 to 84 bpm) swings between 56% and 61%, while its brisk bossa style (124 to 136 bpm) stays almost straight.

The second kind of departure is the tiny, involuntary timing fluctuation in every human strike, typically a few milliseconds early or late. Most drum machines "humanize" by adding independent random jitter to each hit (the random option under JITTER above), where every stroke rolls a fresh die and the error graph spikes wildly up and down. When physicist Holger Hennig and his colleagues analyzed recordings of real drummers, however, they found that human timing errors carry memory across many bars. Each deviation correlates with the ones before it as 1/f (pink) noise: a player subtly leans ahead of the beat for a stretch, then gradually settles behind it. In blind listening tests, listeners consistently preferred grooves humanized with 1/f drift over the exact same beats jittered with uncorrelated random noise.

The 1/f mode generates each drum's drift with a Voss–McCartney pink-noise generator: seven random values summed together, where the k-th source only gets redrawn every 2k hits. Push humanize up near 10 ms and toggle between random and 1/f while the groove runs. Watch the bottom trace: white noise looks like jagged static, whereas 1/f noise meanders smoothly across the zero line. Crank the slider up to 25 ms (or tap sloppy) and either mode falls apart into a drunken stumble.

In the full band below, each instrument gets both its own steady lean and its own 1/f jitter budget tailored to the style. In lo-fi, the snare lays back 14 ms late, the keys drag 12 ms behind, the hi-hat pushes 3 ms early, and jitter scales from 4 ms on the kick up to 9 ms on the lead melody. Synth-pop tightens almost everything down to 1.5 to 4 ms of jitter with barely any lean at all.

Step 4 · What to playA walker with rules

Just like rhythm, melodies follow surprisingly consistent statistical habits. Across huge corpora of folk songs and classical themes, most consecutive notes either repeat or move just one step to a neighboring scale tone, leaving larger leaps relatively rare. And when a melody does make a leap, it usually reverses direction right afterward. Music cognition researcher David Huron gathered many of these patterns in Sweet Anticipation, and Paul von Hippel and Huron showed that much of that post-leap reversal comes down to simple regression to the mean: a wide jump tends to carry a singer near the top or bottom of their comfortable range, where most remaining notes lie back toward the middle.

The melody walker below turns those habits into a small probabilistic state machine playing over a looping I–V–vi–IV progression in C. At each eighth note, it chooses a scale move heavily weighted toward single steps, with the leaps slider controlling how often it risks bigger intervals. On strong beats (beats one and three), gravity pulls the melody onto a tone of the current chord so the line stays tethered to the harmony. Two toggleable rules sit on the right: step back after a leap biases the note after any skip to reverse by one step, and repeat motifs takes each fresh two-bar phrase and replays its rhythm and contour once more, refitting its strong beats onto the next pair of chords before inventing a new idea.

Watch the live histogram on the right while the flute plays. With the default settings, roughly 60–70% of moves are single steps, around 10–15% are repeated notes, and nearly every strong beat lands on a green chord tone. Drag gravity down to zero and the tune immediately drifts untethered above the chords; push leaps all the way up and it fractures into jagged angular jumps. Even more revealing is turning off repeat motifs: every individual interval still obeys good melodic statistics, yet without short-term repetition the ear has nothing to grab onto and the line dissolves into aimless wandering.

Dice music and the Illiac Suite

Letting chance choose notes has a long history. Johann Philipp Kirnberger published a musical dice game for assembling polonaises and minuets in 1757, and a famous 1792 waltz game often credited to Mozart had players roll two dice per bar to pick pre-composed measures from a lookup table. Two centuries later, in 1957, Lejaren Hiller and Leonard Isaacson programmed the ILLIAC I computer at the University of Illinois to compose the Illiac Suite for string quartet, generating candidate notes at random and filtering them through formal counterpoint rules. Our melody walker works in that same spirit at tiny scale: the musical taste lives inside the weights, range limits, and motif rules, while the random number generator explores the space those constraints leave open.

Step 5 · The bandOne number, one song

Both the song clock at the top of this essay and the open-hood band below are driven by a single pure function, writeSong(seed), paired with a look-ahead scheduler and a small mixer. Pass in one integer seed, initialize a deterministic random number generator, and every arrangement choice cascades from there:

  • Style, key, and tempo: First the generator picks one of four styles (lo-fi, synth-pop, a 12/8 bell groove, or bossa), each carrying its own tempo band, swing range, instrument patches, and mode weights (lo-fi favors major and Dorian, while the bell groove leans toward Dorian and Mixolydian), along with any of the twelve tonics.
  • Form: Every song lays out an arc of intro (4 bars, or 8 for the bell groove), verse, chorus, verse, chorus, bridge, chorus, and a 4-bar outro, with 8 bars for each interior section (56 bars total, or 60 in the bell groove). Each section carries an energy score that controls orchestration: intros hold back the bass and lead, while the final chorus brings every instrument in.
  • Harmony: Verses, choruses, and bridges each draw a four-bar progression from a mode-specific bank (for instance, I–V–vi–IV in a major verse followed by IV–V–iii–vi in the chorus), and the outro always resolves home on the tonic. Lo-fi and bossa enrich chords with sevenths and ninths; synth-pop sprinkles in sus and add9 colors. Keys and pad are voiced with the minimum-motion solver from part six, dropping the root on ninth chords since the bass already anchors it.
  • Drums: Euclidean patterns run wherever the style calls for them: busy synth-pop sections spin hi-hats on E(11,16) through E(13,16), the bell groove interlocks the E(7,12) bell with a rotated E(5,12) conga, and bossa rotates E(5,16) into its clave. Transition bars heading into verses or choruses trigger fills, and pop sections kick off with a crash cymbal.
  • Bass: The bass locks onto the kick pattern using roots and fifths, often sliding into a chord change via a chromatic half-step approach note on the final beat of the bar.
  • Melody: Each verse and chorus runs the step-four walker across four two-bar phrases arranged as A A′ B A′: a fresh motif, that same motif refitted to the new chords, a contrasting phrase, and a final return ending on the tonic. When a verse or chorus returns later in the song, its melody comes back with small variations.

writeSong lays the entire piece down up front as timestamped events on a step grid. As the song plays, a look-ahead scheduler reads ahead and injects swing, per-part lean, and 1/f drift right before each event hits the audio engine. Once the outro finishes, a fresh seed rolls and the next song begins without stopping the clock.

The scrolling piano roll plots each note at the exact shifted instant it actually sounds, so you can watch swing and 1/f humanization nudge hits slightly off the vertical beat lines. Try muting everything except drums and bass under PARTS to hear the rhythmic pocket underneath, or cycle humanize through 1/f, random, and off mid-song. Clicking style: any locks subsequent songs to a single genre.

Getting the mix right turned out to matter just as much as the note generation. Every track feeds its own channel strip with gain, stereo panning, and auxiliary sends into a shared 3.4-second plate reverb (high-passed at 220 Hz on the way in so low frequencies stay clean, with zero reverb send on the bass) plus a tempo-synced dotted-eighth delay on the lead. On every kick drum hit, a sidechain gain briefly ducks the keys and pad (a subtle 10% dip in bossa, up to a pumping 40% in synth-pop), carving out headroom for the low end. To balance the faders, I rendered each part soloed offline and measured its RMS level: in a full chorus, drums, bass, and lead sit near −29 dBFS RMS, keys tuck 4 to 8 dB beneath them, the pad sits 10 to 13 dB down, and the combined mix peaks around −7 dBFS, well clear of the series' master limiter.

Hit next song a handful of times and listen closely. Some seeds immediately click into a hook you'd happily let loop, while others feel forgettable even though both obeyed the exact same rules. That gap is the hard boundary of rule-based generation: code can guarantee coherent grammar, good voice leading, and a clean mix, while still having no ear for which melody actually catches.

EpilogueFrom rules to listening

Building a full song from scratch exposes how many quiet layers sit behind a groove that feels effortless. Euclid's algorithm spreads uneven hits across a grid and happens to recover rhythms from Cuba, West Africa, and Brazil (with the West African bell turning out to be the major scale's step pattern mapped into time). Stacking two even pulses creates composite polyrhythms like 3 against 2; deliberate swing and 1/f correlated drift soften grid-locked drums into something a human wrist might play; and a simple random walker starts sounding melodic as soon as you favor steps, anchor strong beats on chord tones, and repeat short motifs. Just as important, panning, reverb filtering, and sidechain ducking do half the work of turning five separate synthesizers into a band occupying the same room.

If I kept pushing this generator further, I'd want to add real key modulations into bridges using the Tonnetz moves from part six, generate drum fills and endings with a rhythmic walker of their own, and let listeners vote on seeds so the generator could gradually discover which combinations of rules people actually want to replay.
