Living Music · Part 7

The Infinite Jukebox

Rhythm from Euclid's algorithm, timing with a human wobble, melodies with memory, and a band that writes a new song from every seed. The rings above are its drum patterns. Tap to start it; when a song ends, it writes the next one.

The first six parts of this series built a note, a synthesizer, consonance, a tuning, a scale and a set of chords. All of that is about pitch, and none of it moves. This part adds time. First rhythm: where the hits go, how two pulses combine, and how far off the grid a hit can land before it stops sounding like a machine. Then melody: a walker that steps around the scale under a few rules. Then a band that uses all of it to write, arrange and mix a song from a single number.

The rings at the top of the page are the current song's drum patterns, one ring per drum, with the chord in the middle and the song's sections around the outside. Every pattern on them comes out of the algorithm in step one.

Step 1 · Where the hits goEuclid's drummer

Divide a bar into n equal steps and put k drum hits on them. If k divides n, the even answer is obvious: 4 hits on 16 steps is four on the floor. If it doesn't, the hits can't all be the same distance apart, and the question is how to spread them as evenly as the grid allows. Three hits on eight steps come out as gaps of 3, 3 and 2: x..x..x. That's the tresillo, the Cuban rhythm under a great deal of Latin and popular music.

Eric Bjorklund wrote down an algorithm for this in 2003, for spacing timing pulses evenly in the control system of the Spallation Neutron Source, a particle accelerator at Oak Ridge. Start with k groups that each hold one hit and n − k groups that each hold one rest. Tuck one leftover group under each group at the front. Repeat with whatever is left over, until at most one leftover remains. Each pass is a step of Euclid's algorithm for the greatest common divisor, run on the counts of hits and rests. The figure draws every pass.

In 2005 Godfried Toussaint pointed out that this algorithm, written for an accelerator, produces a long list of traditional rhythms from around the world. E(3,8) is the tresillo and E(5,8) is the Cuban cinquillo. E(7,12) is the bell pattern of much West African drumming, played on a 12-pulse cycle. E(5,12) is a clapping pattern of the Venda in South Africa, and E(7,16) is a samba pattern from Brazil. The figure names a pattern when it's one of the rhythms from his paper. Some only match after a turn of the necklace: E(5,16) is the bossa nova pattern once you start it on the right step, which is what rotate does. Turning the necklace doesn't change the gaps, only which hit counts as the first.

Pick the bell pattern and read the gaps: 2, 1, 2, 2, 1, 2, 2. Rotate them and you get 2, 2, 1, 2, 2, 2, 1, the step pattern of the major scale from part five. Seven notes spread as evenly as possible over twelve semitones and seven strokes spread as evenly as possible over twelve pulses are the same problem, so they have the same answer. John Clough and Jack Douthett called the answers maximally even sets, and Jeff Pressing noticed the match between the West African bell and the diatonic scale in 1983. Every Euclidean rhythm has the property the figure reports below the stages: at most two gap sizes, one step apart.

The algorithm, as coded 
// k one-hit groups, n − k one-rest groups
let front = Array.from({ length: k }, () => [1]);
let back = Array.from({ length: n - k }, () => [0]);
while (back.length > 1) {
  const pairs = Math.min(front.length, back.length);
  const merged = [];
  for (let p = 0; p < pairs; p++) merged.push([...front[p], ...back[p]]);
  // whatever didn't get a partner is the new remainder
  const rest = front.length > pairs ? front.slice(pairs) : back.slice(pairs);
  front = merged;
  back = rest;
}

For E(3,8) that's three passes: 3 hits and 5 rests, then [x.][x.][x.][.][.], then [x..][x..][x.]. The band below calls the same function for its hi-hats, bells, congas and clave.

Step 2 · Two pulses at onceThree against two

A Euclidean rhythm puts uneven gaps on one grid. A polyrhythm puts two even pulses on one cycle that don't share a grid. Three evenly spaced beats against two evenly spaced beats only line up on the downbeat. To write both down you need a grid of six, the least common multiple, and on that grid the two together make a new uneven rhythm, the resultant: gaps of 2, 1, 1 and 2.

Listen to 3 : 2 for a few cycles and try to hear each drum as the beat in turn. You can flip between them, and when you can't decide, you hear the resultant as one pattern. 4 : 3 needs a grid of 12 and gives 3, 1, 2, 2, 1, 3. 5 : 4 needs 20, and by then the resultant is hard to hold as a pattern at all. The mute buttons help: drop one drum, let the other settle in as the beat, then bring the first back.

Three against two is everywhere in music on a 12-pulse cycle, because twelve splits both ways: four beats of three pulses or six beats of two. The bell-groove style in the band runs the seven-stroke bell from step one over a kick on four beats of three, with a shaker on all twelve pulses and a conga on a rotated E(5,12).

Step 3 · FeelSwing and the human wobble

So far every hit lands on the grid. People don't play that way, and two kinds of departure matter. The first is deliberate. Swing delays every second subdivision, so pairs of equal notes become long–short pairs. At 50% the pair is even. At 67% the first note takes two thirds of the pair, a 2 : 1 ratio, the triplet feel of a shuffle. The figure's swing slider runs from 50% to 75% and shows the ratio.

Swing isn't a fixed ratio in real playing. Anders Friberg and Andreas Sundström measured jazz drummers' cymbal patterns and found the swing ratio falls as the tempo rises: well above 2 : 1 at slow tempos, close to even at fast ones. So the band's lo-fi style, at 72 to 84 bpm, swings between 56% and 61%, and its bossa, at 124 to 136, barely swings at all.

The second departure is the small, unintended error in every hit, a few milliseconds either way. Drum machines that "humanize" usually add independent random offsets, the random setting here: every hit's error is a fresh draw, and the error plot jitters. Holger Hennig and colleagues measured the timing errors of real drummers and found they aren't independent. Each error is related to the ones before it, over long stretches, which makes them a kind of 1/f noise: the player drifts slightly ahead for a while, then slightly behind. In their listening test, people preferred music humanized with long-range correlated errors to the same music with independent ones.

The 1/f setting draws each instrument's errors from a Voss–McCartney pink noise generator: seven random sources, the k-th redrawn every 2k hits, summed. Set humanize to about 10 ms and switch between the two. The plot shows the difference more clearly than the ear does at first: the random line is grass, the 1/f line wanders. At 25 ms both start to sound sloppy.

The band uses both kinds of departure on purpose. Each instrument in a style gets a steady lean and its own jitter. In lo-fi the snare sits 14 ms late and the hi-hat 3 ms early, the keys drag by 12 ms, and the jitter runs from 4 ms on the kick to 9 ms on the melody. Synth-pop is tighter, with 1.5 to 4 ms of jitter and almost no lean.

Step 4 · What to playA walker with rules

Melodies have strong statistical habits. In large collections of folk and art melodies most moves are steps to a neighbouring scale note or repeats, and leaps are the exception. After a leap, melodies tend to turn back the other way. David Huron's Sweet Anticipation collects these regularities, and Paul von Hippel and Huron showed that the turn after a leap is largely regression to the mean: a big leap usually lands near the edge of the melody's range, where most of the available notes are back the other way.

So the walker below picks its next scale step at random, mostly steps, with the leaps slider setting how often it jumps. On beats one and three, gravity pulls it onto a note of the current chord, which is what makes the line sound like it belongs over the chords. Two rules can be switched on. One makes it step back after a leap. The other repeats each two-bar idea once, with the strong beats moved onto the new chords, before writing a new one.

Watch the bars on the right while it plays. At the default settings about 60% of the moves are steps, a fifth are repeated notes, and nearly every strong beat lands on a chord tone. Drop gravity to zero and the line wanders through the chords without noticing them. Push leaps to the top and it turns into a pattern of jumps that's hard to follow. Turn off repeat motifs and something else goes: every phrase is new, and nothing sounds like a tune. Repetition lets you guess a little of what comes next, and that makes a line much easier to follow.

Dice music and the Illiac Suite

Composing by chance is old. Johann Philipp Kirnberger published a dice game for writing polonaises and minuets in 1757, and the best-known one, attributed to Mozart, was printed in 1792: roll two dice for each bar and look up a pre-written bar in a table. In 1957 Lejaren Hiller and Leonard Isaacson used the ILLIAC computer at the University of Illinois to write the Illiac Suite for string quartet, generating notes at random and keeping the ones that passed counterpoint rules. The walker here does the same thing in a smaller way. All the musical knowledge is in the weights and the rules; the dice only choose among what the rules allow.

Step 5 · The bandOne number, one song

The band at the top of the page and in the figure below is one program, writeSong(seed), plus a mixer and a scheduler. The seed feeds a small random number generator, and every choice after that is a draw from it.

  • Style, key and tempo. One of four styles: lo-fi, synth-pop, a 12/8 bell groove, or bossa. Each style has a tempo range, a swing range and a weighted list of modes. Lo-fi favours major and Dorian, the bell groove Dorian and Mixolydian. The tonic is any of the twelve notes.
  • Form. Intro (4 bars, 8 for the bell groove), verse, chorus, verse, chorus, bridge, chorus, outro (4), with 8 bars for each middle section: 56 bars in all, 60 for the bell groove. Each section has an energy level, and the arrangement reads it: the intro has no bass and no melody, and the final chorus has everything.
  • Harmony. Each section draws a four-bar progression of scale degrees from a bank written for its mode, so a major verse might be I–V–vi–IV and its chorus IV–V–iii–vi. The outro ends on the tonic. Lo-fi and bossa add sevenths and ninths; pop adds the odd sus or add9. The keys and the pad are voiced with the minimum-motion search from part six, inside fixed ranges, and ninth chords drop their root because the bass already has it.
  • Drums. Euclidean wherever the style allows: pop hats on E(11,16) to E(13,16) in the busy sections, the E(7,12) bell and an E(5,12) conga in the bell groove, an E(5,16) clave rotated into the bossa pattern. The last bar before a verse or chorus gets a fill, and pop verses and choruses start on a crash.
  • Bass. Roots and fifths on a pattern that follows the kick. When the chord is about to change, the last note of the bar is often a semitone from the next root, leading into it.
  • Melody. The walker from step four, in four two-bar phrases per section, in the shape A A′ B A′: an idea, the idea refitted to new chords, a second idea, and the first again ending on the tonic. When a verse or chorus comes back, its melody comes back too, refitted and slightly changed.

All of that is written up front as a list of notes on a step grid. A look-ahead scheduler then plays them, adding swing, each instrument's lean and its 1/f jitter at the last moment. When the outro ends, it writes the next song and carries on.

The piano roll shows a few bars around now, with notes drawn where they actually sound: you can see the swing and the humanizer move them off the bar lines. Mute everything but the drums and bass to hear the groove the rest sits on, or switch humanize between 1/f, random and off while it plays. Style locks the next songs to one style.

Mixing turned out to matter as much as composing. Each part goes through its own channel with a level, a pan position and sends to a shared reverb (3.4 seconds long, high-passed at 220 Hz going in so it doesn't muddy the low end; the bass has no send) and, for the melody, a dotted-eighth delay. Every kick briefly turns down the keys and pad, by 10% in bossa up to 40% in synth-pop, which keeps the low end clear and gives the pop style its pumping. I set the faders by rendering each part soloed offline and measuring it. In a chorus, drums, bass and melody each sit near −29 dBFS RMS, the keys 4 to 8 dB under that, and the pad 10 to 13 dB under, and the whole mix peaks around −7 dBFS, well under the series' master limiter.

Press next song a few times. Some seeds are better than others, and you can hear which without being able to say why. The program can't: it writes songs that follow its rules, and has no way of judging them.

EpilogueWhat carries over

  • Even spacing on a grid is Euclid's algorithm. It produces the tresillo, the West African bell and the bossa nova, and the bell is the major scale's step pattern in time.
  • Two even pulses make an uneven rhythm. Three against two on a grid of six is 2 + 1 + 1 + 2.
  • Timing errors should remember. Swing is a deliberate delay; human error drifts, and 1/f noise imitates that better than independent random offsets.
  • A melody needs steps, chord tones on the strong beats, and repetition. Without repetition there's nothing to follow.
  • Writing the song is half of it. Voicing, levels, panning, reverb and ducking decide whether it sounds like a band.

Things I'd try next: let the band listen to itself and keep the seeds people replay; write drum fills and endings with the same walker idea used for the melody; and modulate, moving a bridge to a new key along the Tonnetz from part six.
