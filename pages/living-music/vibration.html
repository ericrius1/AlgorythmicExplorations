All eight strings share the same gauge and tension, cut to fractions of 1, ½, ⅓, down to ⅛ of the longest length, which is why their bottom anchor points sweep upward in a curve and why their pitches climb in exact multiples: 110, 220, 330, all the way to 880 Hz. That sympathetic cascade under the untouched strings is the central clue to how music works. A single musical note turns out to be a small crowd of vibrations happening at once. Before building up to synthesizers, harmony, scales, chords, and generative bands, this first part lays out what a single note is actually made of: how high it sounds, how loud it is, and what gives it its distinct character.

Step 1 · A ratePitch and loudness

Sound travels through air as tiny ripples of pressure. When something pushes the air back and forth on a regular schedule, your ear hears a definite pitch, and the number of complete cycles per second is its frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). 

110 Hz   // the open A string on a guitar
220 Hz   // the A one octave higher
440 Hz   // the A an orchestra tunes to

Below is a live oscilloscope connected to a single oscillator, freezing a 20 ms slice of the pressure wave so you can inspect its shape. At the starting pitch of 220 Hz, the orange bracket marks one full cycle, or period, lasting 4.55 ms. Hit play and sweep the frequency slider between 55 and 1760 Hz: as the pitch climbs, the waves pack tightly together and the period shrinks. Moving the level slider between −50 and −10 dB changes only how tall the wave reaches.

While the tone is running, click through the four waveform buttons: sine, triangle, square, and sawtooth. The pitch stays locked where you left it, yet the voice shifts from glassy and smooth, to woody and hollow, to bright and buzzing. The strip beneath the scope lays the same sound out across the full range of human hearing, from 20 Hz to 20 kHz, with one glowing bar per frequency component. The sine wave puts up a single solitary bar. The other three shapes light up whole rows of extra bars at 2, 3, 4, and higher multiples of the fundamental frequency. Those extra bars account for almost everything that separates one instrument from another.

Notice how both sliders behave logarithmically, because human hearing works in ratios. Jumping from 110 to 220 Hz feels like the same musical leap as jumping from 220 to 440 Hz, even though the second jump adds twice as many hertz: every doubling is one octave, and healthy ears span roughly ten of them, from around 20 Hz to 20 kHz (the ceiling creeps downward with age). Loudness compresses the same way. Decibels express amplitude ratios on a log scale, so −20 dB corresponds to an amplitude of 0.1 and −40 dB to 0.01, and a signal generally needs about a 10 dB boost before it feels twice as loud.

Aside · Counting vibrations by eye

For centuries nobody had a way to count hundreds of oscillations per second directly. In the early 1600s, the French friar Marin Mersenne solved it with patience and very long strings. By stretching heavy cords far enough to swing slowly, he could count their cycles by eye, deduce how pitch scales with length, tension, and mass (published in his 1636 Harmonie universelle), and extrapolate down to singing strings. Galileo arrived at the same relationships around the same time. Mersenne's first law (cut a string's length in half and its frequency doubles) is what shapes the curved harp at the top of this page.

Step 2 · One stringStanding waves

Pin a string firmly at both ends and its geometry immediately limits how it can move. The only self-sustaining vibration shapes are waves that hit zero at both anchors: a single broad arch, two opposing half-waves with a still point in the center, three arches with two still points, and so on. Physicists call these shapes modes, or standing waves. Mode n follows the curve sin(nπx), and because shorter ripples shuttle back and forth across the string faster, mode n oscillates at exactly n times the rate of the first mode. Any messy kink or wiggle you put into the string decomposes into a sum of these modes.

That modal math drives both the picture and the audio in the string below. Grab the string anywhere along its length, pull it aside, and let go. At release, the simulation projects your triangular shape onto the first 24 standing-wave modes and lets each one swing and decay on its own clock. Because a real string vibrating at 110 Hz is just a faint blur to the eye, the drawing runs in slow motion so the fundamental always rocks at 1.25 cycles per second (88× slower than the sound at the default pitch).

The 16 bars beneath the string display the live harmonic recipe reaching your ears. Try the preset buttons to see how strongly pluck position colors the tone. Clicking pluck at ½ blanks out every even-numbered bar: pulling right at the midpoint forces the string to move at the exact spot where modes 2, 4, 6, and every other even mode have a stationary node, so none of them can get started. Clicking at ⅕ wipes out harmonics 5, 10, and 15. Clicking near the end plucks at 1/14 of the length, energizing almost every bar and turning the exact same 110 Hz note thin and twangy. Guitarists exploit this by instinct: picking over the soundhole yields a warm, round tone, while picking right back by the bridge sounds sharp and metallic. 

// what the bridge hears from mode n after a pluck at p (0..1 along the string)
const heard = (p: number, n: number): number => Math.sin(n * Math.PI * p) / n;

Notice that the heard amplitude falls off as 1/n rather than 1/n². Decomposing a triangle shape into sines gives displacement amplitudes proportional to 1/n², which drops off quickly. What actually drives the wooden top of a guitar or harp, however, is the sideways force the string exerts on the bridge, which depends on the string's angle (its spatial slope) at the anchor. Taking the derivative of sin(nπx) multiplies each mode by n, cancelling one power of n and restoring a bright 1/n spectrum.

Switch the mode toggle to bow (hold) and press down on the string. A rosined bow operates on a completely different cycle: it catches the string, drags it sideways until tension overwhelms static friction, lets it snap back, and grabs it again once every period. That stick-slip cycle establishes what is known as Helmholtz motion: a single crisp corner racing back and forth along the string inside a smooth parabolic envelope, traced by the dashed orange outline above. Wherever you set the bow down, that traveling kink injects every harmonic at 1/n, delivering a pure sawtooth force to the bridge and giving a bowed violin its unmistakable bite.

Deep dive: why the bow's corner goes round and round

Hermann von Helmholtz worked this out in the 1860s by squinting at a bowed violin string through a vibration microscope. He noticed that any given point on the string moves at just two constant speeds: gliding slowly along with the bow hair, then zipping rapidly back the other way. The only geometric shape that moves like that at every point simultaneously is a sharp V-shaped kink traveling around a parabolic envelope, with straight string segments stretching to both anchors. Written as a modal sum, it is Σ sin(nπx)·sin(nωt)/n², which is the exact sum drawn above while you hold the bow down.

Real violin bowing is far touchier than this clean ideal. Too little bow weight lets the string slip twice per cycle (producing that airy beginner "surface sound"), while pressing too hard breaks the cycle into a gritty scratch. Good string players spend years learning to stay inside the narrow window where Helmholtz motion locks in.

Step 3 · The hidden scoreThe harmonic series

Because a plucked or bowed string vibrates in all its standing-wave modes simultaneously, a single 110 Hz note is also quietly ringing at 220, 330, 440, 550 Hz, and beyond. That whole-number ladder of frequencies is the harmonic series. Each rung is a harmonic (or partial), and the lowest one is the fundamental. Strings, brass horns, woodwinds, and the human vocal folds all naturally generate sounds built on this integer grid.

Here is one steady A2 assembled from its first 16 sine harmonics, mapped onto a piano keyboard along a logarithmic pitch axis. Start the tone with play A2 and your brain fuses all 16 sines into a single rich note. Tap any rung and that harmonic swells by 9 dB for about a second before sinking back into the mixture. Oddly enough, once your ear locks onto an individual overtone during that spotlight, you can often keep hearing it float inside the blend even after it drops back down. Pressing climb the ladder steps up through all 16 rungs at 0.6 s intervals, producing a shimmering melody very close to overtone singing.

Check which piano keys glow beneath the lower rungs. Harmonics 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 are all A's separated by octaves. Harmonic 3 lands on E, a fifth above A3, and harmonic 5 lands on C♯, the major third. Right near the bottom of one single vibrating string, nature spells out an A major triad, a coincidence that echoes through centuries of Western harmony. Higher up, though, the series drifts off the keyboard grid. Harmonic 5 sits 14 cents flat of a modern piano's C♯ (a cent is one hundredth of a semitone), and harmonic 7 lands 31 cents flat of G, far enough into the crack between keys that standard pianos can't play it. Part 4 digs into why keyboards had to bend these ratios.

Aside · Helmholtz and the glass spheres

In the 1850s, long before spectrum analyzers or microphones existed, Helmholtz proved that musical timbre is a mix of harmonics using nothing more than blown glass and brass. He had a series of hollow spheres crafted in graded sizes, each with an opening on one side and a small tapered neck on the other designed to fit snugly into his ear. Each sphere resonated at a single narrow frequency. By holding them up to his ear one after another while a violin or organ played, he could isolate individual harmonics out of the air. Those tuned bulbs are still called Helmholtz resonators, and his 1863 treatise On the Sensations of Tone remains astonishingly fresh.

Step 4 · RecipesBuilding a timbre from sines

If vibrating objects naturally split into stacks of sine waves, the reverse should work too: you ought to be able to invent any steady timbre just by adding sine waves together. In 1822, while studying how heat flows through metal, Joseph Fourier proved that any repeating waveform can be expressed as a sum of sines at whole-number multiples of the repeat rate, each with its own amplitude and starting offset (phase). Below is a 16-harmonic additive synthesizer wired directly to that theorem. Drag across the bars to paint your own harmonic recipe, and watch the curve above trace two full cycles of the resulting sum.

The presets reconstruct the classic oscilloscope shapes from Step 1 by hand, using simple formulas for each harmonic's amplitude and phase: 

// [amplitude, phase] of harmonic n; a phase of π flips the sign
sawtooth: (n) => [1 / n, n % 2 ? 0 : Math.PI],
square: (n) => [n % 2 ? 1 / n : 0, 0],
triangle: (n) => [n % 2 ? 1 / (n * n) : 0, (n - 1) % 4 === 2 ? Math.PI : 0],

A square wave contains only odd harmonics, which gives it a hollow, woody bite very close to a clarinet's low register (a cylindrical pipe closed at one end favors odd standing waves for a closely related reason). The organ preset mimics a classic Hammond drawbar registration using harmonics 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 8 (the 8′, 4′, 2⅔′, 2′, 1⅗′, and 1′ drawbars). Behind the scenes, the synth keeps total RMS output constant as you draw, so changing the bars shifts the tone color while keeping overall loudness steady. Pitch stays anchored too (even if you click drop the fundamental to zero out bar 1), because the spacing between neighboring harmonics remains 110 Hz.

Click scramble phases while a sawtooth or square wave is sounding. Every tiny clock dial under the bars spins to a random starting angle, and the waveform above collapses into an unrecognizable jagged scribble. Listen closely, though: the sound scarcely budges. Your inner ear behaves much more like a frequency-magnitude detector than an oscilloscope, an empirical rule known as Ohm's acoustic law. Phase can occasionally become audible on very low, buzzy pitches with dozens of harmonics, yet in everyday listening the bar heights predict what you hear far better than the time-domain waveform ever does.

All of this explains why a flute and a violin playing the same A4 sound worlds apart. A flute's spectrum is mostly fundamental with a handful of gentle upper harmonics and a wisp of breath noise. A violin starts with the bright sawtooth of a bowed string and filters it through a carved wooden box that amplifies certain frequency bands and damps others. Their beginnings differ just as sharply: a flute note blooms out of air, whereas a violin note opens with a brief rosin scratch, and the brain relies heavily on those first few tens of milliseconds to name an instrument.

Step 5 · ResonanceA voice from a buzz

That wooden violin body points to an acoustic mechanism that shapes both instruments and human speech: resonances fixed in frequency. When you sing or speak a vowel, your vocal folds clap together to produce a raw harmonic buzz containing every integer harmonic, rolling off at roughly 6 dB per octave by the time it radiates past your lips. Your throat, mouth, and nasal cavities act as a chain of resonant chambers that boost whichever harmonics happen to fall near their resonant peaks, called formants. Reshaping your tongue and jaw slides those formant peaks to new frequencies, turning one vowel into another, while singing up and down a scale moves the underlying harmonic comb beneath formants that stay put. Gunnar Fant codified this source-filter view of speech in his 1960 book, and it still underpins speech acoustics today.

The synthesizer below builds a vocal tract directly from that recipe: a 120 Hz harmonic buzz (with a gentle 5.3 Hz vibrato to take the robotic edge off) feeds through four resonant filters wired in series, following Dennis Klatt's cascade formant model. The box on the left plots the first two formants in the standard phonetic vowel layout. Moving down opens your jaw and raises F1; moving left pushes your tongue toward the front of your mouth and raises F2 ("heed" sits high-left, while "who'd" sits high-right). The nine targets mark the adult male averages Gordon Peterson and Harold Barney measured in 1952.

Tap voice and drag the glowing dot from "father" up to "heed" while watching the spectrum on the right. The vertical harmonic lines stay parked 120 Hz apart while the blue formant hills slide over them, lighting up different clusters of harmonics. Then leave the vowel alone and sweep the pitch slider from 80 up to 600 Hz. Now the blue hills stay frozen while the harmonic comb stretches apart underneath. By the time pitch climbs into soprano territory, the harmonics stand so far apart that they can straddle a formant peak and miss it completely, making sung vowels blur into one another. Trained operatic sopranos actually solve this physically on high notes by dropping their jaw wide to pull F1 up onto the fundamental, trading crisp diction for a ringing, effortless projection. Clicking sing ah · ee · oo glides slowly across three corners of the vowel space, spending 1.1 seconds on each.

Step 6 · Pitch is a guessThe missing fundamental

Up to now, it has been easy to equate a note's pitch with the frequency of its first harmonic. Reality is sneakier. Below, the opening phrase of Beethoven's "Ode to Joy" loops in C, with every note built from 12 harmonics. Each horizontal stack of colored bars on the scrolling score shows one note's harmonic ladder at 1, 2, 3, … 12 times its fundamental.

Start playback and slowly slide lowest harmonic kept upward. At 2, every fundamental vanishes completely. Push all the way to 7 and the bottom six harmonics of every note are stripped out; the lowest frequency physically leaving your speaker is now 916 Hz. The tone grows thin and tinny (overall loudness stays normalized), yet the melody refuses to jump upward. It keeps humming right down at C3 (131 Hz), marked by the dashed orange line where zero acoustic energy exists. Your auditory system spots that the surviving partials are spaced 131 Hz apart and reconstructs the missing fundamental (or residue pitch) that would produce that spacing. That single trick of perception is why tiny laptop and phone speakers, which physically cannot push air below a few hundred hertz, still let you follow a bass guitar line.

Aside · Seebeck, Ohm, and Schouten

In the 1840s, the German physicist August Seebeck spun acoustic sirens perforated with carefully spaced hole patterns and insisted he could hear pitches whose frequencies were completely absent from the Fourier spectrum. Georg Ohm, the same physicist behind Ohm's acoustic law, argued Seebeck must be mishearing something, and Helmholtz largely agreed with Ohm. Almost a century later, around 1940, Jan Schouten settled the debate with electronic tone generators: Seebeck had been right all along. The brain infers pitch from the collective pattern of upper harmonics, and neuroscientists are still unpacking the exact circuits that do it.

Step 7 · Two kinds of highA staircase that never ends

Back in the harmonic ladder, you saw that notes separated by an octave share the same letter name and color because the ear perceives them as versions of the same note. That means pitch actually has two distinct dimensions: raw height (low rumble versus high squeak) and chroma (which pitch class a note belongs to, cycling through C, C♯, D, and back around every octave). Put both together and pitch wraps into a three-dimensional helix: moving around the circle changes chroma, while climbing vertically changes height.

In 1964, psychologist Roger Shepard designed an auditory illusion that turns the chroma wheel while holding overall height completely still. It stacks nine pure sine waves spaced exact octaves apart (here spanning C1 at 33 Hz up to 16.7 kHz) underneath a fixed, bell-shaped loudness curve on a log-frequency axis. All nine sines step upward together one semitone at a time. Whenever the highest partial climbs past the top of the bell curve and fades to silence, a fresh partial fades in quietly at the bottom, so after twelve steps the spectrum lands back in the exact configuration where it began.

Hit play and listen to the endless climb (you can also reverse it with down). On the helix to the left, all nine partials line up along one vertical spoke; as the tone steps, the spoke rotates around the ring of pitch classes, carrying your ear smoothly from C to C♯ to D, while the center of gravity of the glowing dots stays anchored right in the middle of the bell curve. steps (Shepard) advances one semitone every 0.42 seconds, just like Shepard's original tape. Switching to glide (Risset) plays Jean-Claude Risset's continuous version from a few years later, sliding upward at an eighth of an octave per second. Leave it running for a minute and watch the readout count multiple octaves of apparent climb while every sine wave stays trapped between 33 Hz and 16.7 kHz.

EpilogueInside one sound

Taken together, these seven experiments dismantle the naive idea that a note is just a single frequency. Pitch begins as a repetition rate heard in ratios, yet the brain infers it from the whole harmonic spacing, even when the fundamental is stripped out or the octaves loop back on themselves like an Escher staircase. Timbre emerges from the harmonic recipe a vibrating body emits (reshaped by where you pluck or bow it), sculpted further by fixed physical resonances like a violin's spruce belly or the shifting shape of your mouth, and stamped by the first few milliseconds of attack.

There are plenty of rabbit holes left to poke at here: consonant transitions, where rapid formant glides tell a "b" apart from a "d"; stretched membranes like drumheads, whose two-dimensional modes break clean integer ratios altogether; or a live microphone view so you could sing into the page and watch your own harmonics march under your own formants.

That also settles the puzzle from the eight-string harp at the top of the page. Because strings 2 through 8 are tuned to the harmonic series of string 1, every time that single low string vibrates, its standing-wave modes pour acoustic energy straight into the frequencies of all seven strings beside it.
