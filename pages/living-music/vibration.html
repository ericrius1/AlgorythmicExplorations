Living Music · Part 1

The Anatomy of a Note

Pitch, loudness, and timbre, taken apart. The harp above has eight strings tuned to the harmonics of one low A. Drag across them to play.

Scroll down to read ↓

The harp at the top of this page has eight strings at 110, 220, 330, and so on up to 880 Hz. They're all the same string, under the same tension, cut to lengths of 1, ½, ⅓ down to ⅛. That's why the bottom ends trace a curve. Tap it to turn the sound on, then drag across the strings (or press the keys 1 to 8). Under each string a trace falls away showing how much sound there is at that string's frequency. Pluck the longest string and traces start falling under the other seven too, even though you didn't touch them. By the end of this article you'll know why.

This series works its way up to music theory from the physics of sound. Scales, chords, and keys make a lot more sense once you know what a single note is made of, so that's where it starts. A note has three properties your ear cares about: how high it is, how loud it is, and what it sounds like. Each one has a physical cause, and each figure below lets you change one of them while you listen.

Step 1 · A ratePitch and loudness

Sound is a pressure wave in the air. A note is a pressure wave that repeats. Whatever made it pushed the air, and pushed it again, on a steady schedule, and the number of repeats per second is its frequency, measured in hertz. Frequency is what you hear as pitch. 

110 Hz   // the A string on a guitar
220 Hz   // the A one octave up
440 Hz   // the A an orchestra tunes to

The figure below is an oscilloscope wired to one oscillator. It shows 20 ms of what the oscillator is playing, held still so the shape doesn't scroll. The bracket marks one period, the time for one repeat: 4.55 ms at the starting 220 Hz. Press play and drag the frequency slider (55 Hz to 1760 Hz). The pitch climbs and the waves pack closer together. The level slider runs from −50 to −10 dB and changes only the height of the wave.

Then try the four shape buttons. The pitch stays where it is, but the sound changes from smooth (sine) to hollow (triangle and square) to bright and buzzy (sawtooth). The strip under the scope explains it. It puts the same sound on a frequency axis that spans everything people hear, 20 Hz to 20 kHz, with one bar for each component. A sine has one bar. The other shapes have a whole row of them at 2, 3, 4… times the frequency you set. Those extra bars are the subject of the rest of this article.

Two things about hearing are worth knowing before going on. First, the ear hears pitch as ratios. 110 to 220 Hz sounds like the same size of step as 220 to 440 Hz, because each one doubles the frequency, and a doubling is what musicians call an octave. Human hearing spans about ten octaves, from roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz (less as you get older). Second, loudness works on ratios too. Decibels measure the ratio of two amplitudes on a log scale: −20 dB is an amplitude of 0.1, −40 dB is 0.01. A sound has to go up by about 10 dB to seem twice as loud.

Aside · Counting vibrations by eye

Nobody could measure a frequency directly until the 1600s. Marin Mersenne, a French friar, worked out how a string's pitch depends on its length, tension, and weight (published in Harmonie universelle, 1636), using strings long and heavy enough that he could count their swings by eye. Then he used those laws to extrapolate to strings short enough to sing. Galileo reached the same laws around the same time. Mersenne's first law is the one the harp uses: halve the length and the frequency doubles.

Step 2 · One stringStanding waves

A string tied down at both ends can't vibrate any way it likes. The only shapes that keep their form as they move are the ones that fit a whole number of half-waves between the ends: one hump, two humps, three. These are the string's modes, or standing waves. Mode n has the shape sin(nπx), and because a shorter wave on the same string moves back and forth faster, it vibrates at n times the frequency of the first one. Any motion the string makes, however messy, is a sum of these modes.

That is how the figure below works. Grab the string anywhere, pull it, and let go. At the moment you release, it works out how much of each of the first 24 modes is in the shape you made, then lets each mode swing and fade on its own. The picture you see and the sound you hear come from the same numbers. The picture runs slowed down so that the fundamental always swings at 1.25 times a second (88× slower than the sound at the default 110 Hz). A real string at that pitch is a blur.

The bars under the string are the recipe you're hearing, one bar per harmonic. Press "pluck at ½" and every even bar goes dark: a pluck in the middle sits on the node of every even mode, so none of them get started. "At ⅕" removes harmonics 5, 10, and 15. "Near the end" plucks at 1/14 of the length and lights nearly every bar, and the same string at the same pitch sounds thin and twangy. Guitarists use this all the time. Picking near the bridge sounds bright, picking over the soundhole sounds round. 

// what the bridge hears from mode n after a pluck at p (0..1 along the string)
const heard = (p: number, n: number): number => Math.sin(n * Math.PI * p) / n;

The 1/n comes from two effects. A pluck is a triangle, and a triangle's share of mode n falls off as 1/n². But the sound doesn't come from the string's position. It comes from the force the string puts on the bridge, which goes with its slope at the end, and that brings back a factor of n.

Now switch to "bow (hold)" and press on the string. A bow does something different. It grips the string, drags it along, lets it slip back, and grips it again, once per period. The result is called Helmholtz motion: a single sharp corner racing around the string inside a parabola-shaped outline, which you can see in the figure. It puts every harmonic in at 1/n, wherever you bow, which makes a sawtooth at the bridge. That's why a bowed violin string sounds so much buzzier than a plucked one.

Deep dive: why the bow's corner goes round and round

Hermann von Helmholtz worked this out in the 1860s by watching a violin string through a vibration microscope. He found that each point of the string moves at two constant speeds: slowly with the bow, then quickly back the other way. The only motion that does that everywhere at once is a kink travelling around the string, with straight segments on either side. As a sum of modes it's Σ sin(nπx)·sin(nωt)/n², which is what the figure draws while you hold the bow down.

Real bowing is messier. Too little pressure and the string slips twice a period (the "surface sound" of a beginner); too much and it scratches. The model here is the ideal version that a good player is aiming for.

Step 3 · The hidden scoreThe harmonic series

So a plucked or bowed string plays all of its modes at once. A 110 Hz note is also sounding at 220, 330, 440, 550 Hz and up. These frequencies are the harmonic series, each one is a harmonic (or partial), and the first one is the fundamental. Most pitched instruments make sounds with this structure: strings, the human voice, and every wind instrument.

The next figure is one A2 built from its first 16 harmonics, each a sine wave, laid over a piano keyboard on the same log axis. Press play and you hear one note. Then tap a rung. That harmonic gets 9 dB louder for about a second and drops back into the blend. Once you've heard it on its own, you can usually keep picking it out of the steady tone. "Climb the ladder" goes up all 16, one every 0.6 s, which sounds a bit like overtone singing.

Look at which keys light up. Harmonics 2, 4, 8, and 16 are A's, each an octave higher. Harmonic 3 is an E, a fifth above A3, and harmonic 5 is a C♯, the major third. The bottom of the ladder spells out an A major chord, and that's a big part of why the major chord sounds so settled, a point the later parts of the series come back to. The higher harmonics don't land on the keys as neatly. Harmonic 5 is 14 cents flat of the piano's C♯ (a cent is a hundredth of a semitone), and harmonic 7 is 31 cents flat of G, far enough that no piano key matches it. The keyboard is a compromise, and part four is about why.

Aside · Helmholtz and the glass spheres

Hermann von Helmholtz showed that timbre is a recipe of harmonics in the 1850s with no electronics at all. He used a graded set of hollow glass and brass spheres, each with a small neck that fit into his ear. Each one resonates at a single frequency, so holding them up one by one while an instrument played let him hear its harmonics one at a time. The spheres are now called Helmholtz resonators, and his 1863 book On the Sensations of Tone is still worth reading.

Step 4 · RecipesBuilding a timbre from sines

If every note is a stack of sines, you should be able to build any note by stacking sines yourself. In 1822 Joseph Fourier showed that any repeating wave can be written that way: one sine at the repeat frequency and others at whole-number multiples of it, each with its own amplitude and phase. The figure below is that idea with 16 harmonics. Drag across the bars to draw a recipe, and the curve above shows two periods of the sum.

The presets are the same shapes the oscilloscope played, built by hand: 

// [amplitude, phase] of harmonic n; a phase of π is a minus sign
sawtooth: (n) => [1 / n, n % 2 ? 0 : Math.PI],
square: (n) => [n % 2 ? 1 / n : 0, 0],
triangle: (n) => [n % 2 ? 1 / (n * n) : 0, (n - 1) % 4 === 2 ? Math.PI : 0],

A square wave is the odd harmonics alone. A clarinet's low register is short on even harmonics for a related reason (a cylindrical tube closed at one end favours odd modes), and you can hear the family resemblance. The organ preset is a drawbar registration: harmonics 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 8, which are the 8′, 4′, 2⅔′, 2′, 1⅗′ and 1′ drawbars on a Hammond. The level stays at the same RMS whatever you draw, so the recipe changes the sound's colour without making it louder. And nothing you draw changes the pitch. The pitch belongs to the spacing of the harmonics, 110 Hz apart, however much of each there is.

Now press "scramble phases". Every harmonic gets shifted in time by a random amount, and the curve turns into something unrecognisable. The sound barely changes. Your ear mostly measures how much of each frequency there is and largely ignores how they line up, an observation known as Ohm's acoustic law. It isn't the whole story (at low pitches with many harmonics you can sometimes hear a phase change as a different buzz), but it's why the bars predict the sound so much better than the shape of the wave does.

It also explains why a flute and a violin playing the same A4 sound nothing alike. The flute's recipe is mostly fundamental with a few weak harmonics, plus breath noise. The violin's is the bowed sawtooth from the string figure, passed through a wooden body that boosts some frequency ranges and cuts others. They also start differently. A flute note breathes in, a violin note starts with a scratch from the bow, and a lot of what you recognise happens in those first tens of milliseconds.

Step 5 · ResonanceA voice from a buzz

That wooden body raises an important point. Some of what shapes a timbre stays put in frequency while the pitch moves. Your voice is the clearest example. Your vocal folds make a buzz with every harmonic in it, falling at about 6 dB per octave by the time it leaves your lips. Your throat and mouth then act as a set of resonant filters that boost whatever harmonics land near their resonant frequencies. These resonances are called formants. Move your tongue and jaw and the formants move, and that's how you make different vowels. Change pitch and the harmonics move under formants that stay where they are. Describing speech as a source and a filter became standard with Gunnar Fant's 1960 book on the acoustics of speech.

The figure below is a small formant synthesizer. A buzz at 120 Hz runs through four resonant filters in a row, the way Dennis Klatt's cascade synthesizer models the vocal tract, with a little vibrato (5.3 Hz) so it sounds less mechanical. The chart on the left is the vowel space. Up and down is the first formant, F1, which goes up as your jaw opens. Left to right is F2, which is high when your tongue is at the front of your mouth ("ee") and low when it's at the back ("oo"). The nine vowels are placed at the average formants Peterson and Barney measured for adult men in 1952.

Drag the dot from "father" to "heed" and watch the right side. The harmonics stay 120 Hz apart, and only the peaks of the curve move. Then leave the dot alone and drag the pitch slider (80 to 600 Hz). Now the harmonics spread out and the curve stays still. At high pitches the harmonics are so far apart that they can miss the formant peaks entirely, and the vowels start to blur together. This is why the words of a soprano's highest notes are hard to make out, and why trained sopranos open their jaw on high notes to move F1 up next to the fundamental. "Sing ah · ee · oo" glides between three vowels, 1.1 s each.

Step 6 · Pitch is a guessThe missing fundamental

So far the pitch of a note has been the frequency of its first harmonic. The next figure shows that it isn't that simple. It plays the opening of Beethoven's "Ode to Joy" in C, with each note built from 12 harmonics. The picture is a score made of those harmonics, scrolling past: every note is a stack of bars at 1, 2, 3… times its frequency.

Raise the slider while the tune plays. At 2, every fundamental is gone. At 7, the first six harmonics of every note are gone, and nothing below 916 Hz is playing at all. The tone gets thinner and brighter (the level is held constant), but the melody stays at the same pitch, down to the C3 at 131 Hz. Your ear notices that what's left is spaced 131 Hz apart and reports the pitch that spacing implies, even with no energy there. This is called the missing fundamental or residue pitch. It's how a phone speaker that can't reproduce anything below a few hundred hertz still lets you follow a bass line.

Aside · Seebeck, Ohm, and Schouten

In the 1840s August Seebeck played sirens with patterns of holes and heard pitches that had no matching component in the sound. Georg Ohm, of the acoustic law above, argued that this had to be an illusion of some kind, and Helmholtz mostly sided with Ohm. A century later, Jan Schouten's experiments with electronically generated tones (around 1940) showed Seebeck had been right: the ear forms a pitch from the pattern of the higher harmonics. How the brain does it is still being worked out.

Step 7 · Two kinds of highA staircase that never ends

The ladder figure showed that notes an octave apart sound alike. Musicians give them the same letter, and the figures on this page draw them in the same colour. So pitch has two parts. One is how high the note is. The other is which note it is, called its chroma or pitch class, which comes back around every octave. You can draw that as a helix: going around is chroma, going up is height.

In 1964 Roger Shepard built a tone that has chroma and no height. It's nine sine waves, each an octave apart (here from C1 at 33 Hz up to 16.7 kHz), under a fixed bell-shaped loudness curve on a log-frequency axis. All nine rise one semitone at a time. The top one fades out as it climbs off the curve, and a new one fades in at the bottom, so after twelve steps the sound is the same as where it started.

Play it and it seems to rise forever. On the helix all nine components sit on one spoke: the spoke turns, which is the chroma changing, but the stack never goes up. "Steps" moves a semitone every 0.42 s, in discrete steps like Shepard's. "Glide" is the continuous version Jean-Claude Risset made a few years later, rising an eighth of an octave per second. Run it long enough and the readout below the figure will tell you it has climbed several octaves while the components still span the same range. Your ear tracks the chroma step by step and has nothing to tell it the height hasn't changed.

EpilogueWhat a note is

  • Pitch is a repetition rate, and the ear hears it in ratios. A doubling is an octave, whatever frequency you start from.
  • Timbre is mostly a recipe of harmonics. Strings, pipes, and voices make harmonics at whole-number multiples of the fundamental, and the mix of them (plus resonances fixed in frequency, plus the attack) is what tells instruments apart.
  • Where you excite a string decides the recipe. A pluck at ½ removes every even harmonic. A bow puts them all in.
  • Pitch is an inference. Take away the fundamental and you still hear it. Keep the chroma moving with no change in height and you hear a pitch that rises forever.

Things I'd try next: a sung vowel with consonants (formant transitions are what make a "b" different from a "d"); the inharmonic overtones of a drum skin, where the modes aren't whole-number multiples at all; and a figure that records your own voice and shows its harmonics and formants live.

The harp at the top is still there. Pluck the low A string and watch the traces fall under strings 2 to 8. Those strings are tuned to its harmonics, so the sound of one string includes all the others.
