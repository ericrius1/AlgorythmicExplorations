Living Music · Part 5

Seven of Twelve

Twelve notes, seven lit, and a melody wandering among them. Every few phrases one note moves a semitone and the mode changes colour. Tap the ring to play along.

Part four ended with twelve equal notes per octave. Hardly any music uses all twelve at once. Most melodies you know live inside a set of seven, and a lot of folk music inside five. On a piano the white keys are one such set: 

C   D   E   F   G   A   B   C
  2   2   1   2   2   2   1      // steps, in semitones

Seven steps, five of them whole steps and two half steps, and the two half steps aren't evenly spaced. This article is about why that lopsided pattern works so well, what you get by starting it from a different note, how keys relate to each other, and what some musical traditions that don't use the piano's grid do instead.

Step 1 · The ringA scale is a step pattern

Draw the twelve notes round a clock, C at the top, and a scale becomes a shape: a necklace with some beads lit. The arcs between lit beads are the steps, labelled W for a whole step and H for a half step. The figure starts on the major scale.

The half steps are the landmarks. Because the two of them sit at different distances from each other, every note in the scale has a different set of neighbours, so your ear can tell where it is. B is a half step below C, and that's why a melody that reaches B leans toward C. Switch the pattern to whole tone, six equal steps, and the landmarks disappear: every note looks like every other, and the scale has no obvious home. Debussy used it for that floating sound in pieces like Voiles. The pentatonic pattern goes the other way and has no half steps at all, which is why you can noodle on the piano's five black keys and nothing clashes.

Now turn on the drone and press rotate the pattern. The shape stays the same, but it starts from its next note while C stays home. Starting the major pattern on its second note gives 2 1 2 2 2 1 2, on its third 1 2 2 2 1 2 2, and so on. Each rotation puts the half steps somewhere else relative to C, and each one has its own name and its own colour. These are the modes.

Step 2 · ModesBrightest to darkest

The seven modes of the major scale can be put in order. Sort them so each one has lower notes than the one before, and something tidy happens: each step down the ladder lowers exactly one note by a semitone. Nothing else moves.

Lydian, at the top, has a raised fourth, and it sounds bright and slightly unresolved; the Simpsons theme is built on it. Ionian is the major scale. Lower its seventh and you get Mixolydian, the sound of a lot of rock and folk ("Norwegian Wood" is in it). Lower the third and you're in Dorian, a minor mode with a raised sixth that keeps it from sounding sad ("So What", "Scarborough Fair"). Aeolian is the natural minor. Phrygian's half step right above home is common in flamenco. Locrian even lowers the fifth, and with no stable fifth above its home note it's rarely used as a home at all.

Lower the one note Locrian has left, the root itself, and you're back at Lydian a semitone down. The ladder is a loop.

Aside · Where the names come from

The names are Greek, but the scales aren't. Medieval church theorists borrowed names like Dorian and Phrygian from Greek writings and attached them to their own modes, which weren't the scales the Greeks meant. Heinrich Glarean added Ionian and Aeolian in 1547, which brought the major and minor scales into the system.

Step 3 · Why sevenSpread as evenly as possible

There are 66 different ways to choose seven notes out of twelve (counting transpositions as the same), so why this one? One answer is evenness. Seven doesn't divide twelve, so seven notes can't be spaced perfectly evenly, but they can be spaced as evenly as the semitone grid allows. Put the steps as close to 12/7 as possible, which means a mix of 2s and 1s with the 1s spread apart, and you get the major scale. John Clough and Jack Douthett called these sets maximally even in 1991, and for every number of notes there's one such shape.

Slide through the counts and many familiar names come out: 3 is the augmented triad, 4 the diminished seventh chord, 5 the pentatonic scale, 6 the whole-tone scale, 7 the diatonic scale, 8 the octatonic scale. Click notes on the left ring to build a set of your own; the evenness score drops as soon as you bunch notes up.

The right ring shows the same notes in circle-of-fifths order, and for 5 and 7 they form one unbroken chain: C G D A E for the pentatonic, F C G D A E B for the major scale. That's the chain from part four's spiral. Stacking fifths and spreading notes evenly turn out to give the same scales.

The bars count how many pairs of notes sit 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 semitones apart. For the major scale those counts are 2, 5, 4, 3, 6 and 1, all different. Music theorists call a scale with this property deep. It matters for the next step. Move a scale by some interval and the number of notes it keeps is the number of pairs at that distance (doubled for the tritone). For the major scale that's 6 notes kept for a move of a fifth, 5 for a whole step, 4 for a minor third, 3 for a major third, and 2 for a half step or a tritone.

Aside · Five notes everywhere

Pentatonic scales turn up in an enormous range of music: Scottish and Irish folk songs, Chinese and Japanese traditional music, West African music, spirituals and the blues. At the 2009 World Science Festival, Bobby McFerrin had an audience sing notes as he jumped between spots on the stage, then jumped to a spot he hadn't shown them. The whole room sang the next note of the pentatonic scale without being told. Whether that's built into hearing or learned from a lifetime of music is still argued about.

Step 4 · KeysThe circle of fifths as a map

Rotating the pattern against a fixed C gave modes. The other move is to keep the pattern and move the home note. Start the major pattern on D and you get D major, which sounds like C major moved up a step. In equal temperament every key is the same shape, so the useful question is how far apart two keys are.

The circle of fifths answers it. Arrange the keys so each is a fifth above the last and neighbours share six of their seven notes. C major and G major differ only in F versus F♯. Two steps round, five notes are shared; across the circle, only two.

Each key is marked with how many notes it shares with the selected one. Click another key and the figure modulates: it plays the old home chord, then the new key's dominant seventh chord, then the new home chord. To a neighbouring key the move is barely noticeable, because six of the seven notes stay put. To the far side of the circle it's a jolt. That's why most music that changes key moves to a neighbour, and why a jump across the circle is something composers save for an effect. The inner ring holds the relative minors, which use the same notes as the major key outside them with a different home.

Key signatures follow the same map. Each step clockwise adds one sharp, in the order F♯ C♯ G♯ D♯ A♯ E♯ B♯, which is the chain of fifths again. Each step anticlockwise adds a flat, B♭ E♭ A♭ and so on.

Aside · The first map

The oldest known circle-of-fifths diagram is in Nikolai Diletsky's Grammatika of 1679, a composition handbook written in Moscow. Johann David Heinichen published his own "musical circle" in Germany in 1711, and the version in Western textbooks descends from his. Both were practical tools for working out which keys could follow which.

Step 5 · Other mapsScales between the piano's keys

Everything so far assumes the twelve-note grid. Many musical traditions don't use it, and their scales have notes that fall between the piano's keys. Here are three, with each note pinned where it's tuned above a strip of the twelve equal-tempered semitones. They're simplified: in all three traditions a scale is only part of what defines a piece, and intonation is learned by ear rather than read off a chart.

Arabic maqam. A maqam is built from ajnas (singular jins), short groups of three to five notes, stacked so that the second starts on a pivot note. Maqam Rast is jins Rast on C followed by jins Rast or Nahawand on G. Some ajnas contain steps of about three quarters of a tone, and the notes between the piano's keys are written with a half-flat sign, a flat with a slash through it. Since the early twentieth century those have been notated on a 24-step quarter-tone grid, but the grid is a notation: how high E half-flat sits depends on the maqam, the region and the performer. The figure uses the pitches MaqamWorld plays for its examples. In Rast, E half-flat is 355 cents above C. In Bayati the second note is 151 cents above D, and the F above it is tuned 19 cents below the piano's F. A maqam also comes with a characteristic path through its notes, its sayr, and that's as much a part of its identity as the scale.

Hindustani rāga. North Indian classical music is played against a drone, usually a tanpura whose four strings sound Sa and Pa (the tonic and fifth), and every note is heard against that drone. The notes are named Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni, and Re, Ga, Dha and Ni can be lowered (komal) and Ma raised (tīvra). Yaman, an evening rāga, uses the raised Ma, the same notes as the Lydian mode, but it has its own ascent and descent: it climbs Ni Re Ga Ma Dha Ni Sa, skipping Sa and Pa on the way up, and descends through Pa. Bhairav, a morning rāga, uses komal Re and komal Dha, and both are played with a slow sway called andolan, which the figure imitates. Ancient theory, going back to the Nātyaśāstra, divided the octave into 22 śrutis. Measurements of real performances, starting with Nazir Jairazbhoy and A. W. Stone in 1963, have found that intonation varies with the rāga, the phrase and the performer rather than sitting on fixed positions. The ratios in the figure (5:4 for Ga, 3:2 for Pa and so on) are a common way to approximate notes tuned against a drone, not a standard.

Javanese gamelan. A gamelan is a set of bronze instruments, mostly gongs and metal bars, tuned together and never retuned to match another set. Java has two tuning systems. Slendro has five notes per octave in steps that are roughly equal. Pelog has seven notes in uneven steps, and most pieces use a five-note subset of them, which the figure lets you pick. There is no standard tuning for either: each gamelan has its own, and no two sets are the same. In 1972 Wasisto Surjodiningrat, P. J. Sudarjana and Adhi Susanto published measurements of dozens of respected gamelan. The slendro in the figure is the average of their slendro sets (0, 231, 474, 717, 955 cents, with an octave stretched to 1208). The pelog is a model: Martin Braun found that the same pelog measurements cluster near steps of a nine-step equal division of the octave, 133 cents each, and the figure uses those positions. The metal-bar timbre matters too. Bars and gongs have overtones that aren't whole-number multiples, so the harmonic alignment from part three works differently on these instruments.

Aside · Measuring other scales

Alexander Ellis invented the cent in 1885 (part four) to compare the scales of instruments like these, including Javanese gamelan instruments. His conclusion was that the musical scale is not one natural thing but many, built differently by different cultures. Western music theory took a long time to absorb that.

Step 6 · ExplorerOne slider for the fifth

Back to the seven-note scales, with one more idea from part four. Pythagorean tuning, quarter-comma meantone, and 12-, 19-, 31- and 53-equal are all chains of identical fifths; they differ only in how big each fifth is. A mode is a set of positions on that chain. The major scale is F C G D A E B, positions −1 to 5 counting from C. So a single number, the size of the fifth, tunes every note of every mode. 

whole step  = 2 × fifth − 1200
half step   = 3600 − 5 × fifth
major third = 4 × fifth − 2400     // pure (386.3¢) when the fifth is 696.58¢

The figure below puts that number on a slider. Play the pads, use your keyboard (A S D F G H J K L ; ' play the scale upward), or press the Roman-numeral buttons, which play the triad built on each degree from the scale's own notes.

Drag the fifth all the way down to 685.7 cents and the whole steps shrink until all seven steps are equal, 171.4 cents each: the major scale has turned into seven-equal and lost its landmarks. Drag it up to 720 and the half steps shrink to nothing. E lands on F, B lands on C, and the seven-note scale collapses into five-equal, a pentatonic. In between are all the historical tunings, and the major chord (press I) changes character along the way: pure at quarter-comma meantone, shimmering at 12-equal, rough at Pythagorean. Andrew Milne, William Sethares and James Plamondon call this range the syntonic tuning continuum and have built software instruments that let a player slide along it while playing.

EpilogueWhat carries over

What to keep from this part:

  • A scale is a step pattern. The uneven placement of the half steps gives every note a different set of neighbours, which is how the ear finds home.
  • Modes are rotations. Ordered from bright to dark, each one lowers a single note.
  • Seven is the evenest odd split of twelve. The major scale is the maximally even seven-note set and a chain of seven fifths at the same time.
  • Distance between keys is shared notes. The circle of fifths is a map of how much changes when music moves.
  • Twelve is one answer. Maqam, rāga and gamelan scales tune notes the piano doesn't have, and they're learned by ear, not from a grid.

Things I'd try next: a rāga figure that bends between notes the way a singer does (meend), since a sequence of fixed pitches leaves out most of what makes a rāga sound like itself; maqam modulation between ajnas; and the explorer on an isomorphic button grid, where every chord keeps its shape at any fifth size.
