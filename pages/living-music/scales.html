Living Music · Part 5

Seven of Twelve

A melody wandering across seven lit notes out of twelve. Every few phrases a single note slides by a semitone and shifts the mode's color. Tap the ring to play along.

Look down at a piano keyboard and you see something slightly odd. Part four ended with twelve equal semitones per octave, yet the front row of white keys skips five of them in a lopsided clump of two and three: 

C   D   E   F   G   A   B   C
  2   2   1   2   2   2   1      // steps, in semitones

Hardly any song uses all twelve pitches at once. Most melodies stick to seven of them, and a huge share of the world's folk tunes get by on five. Five whole steps and two half steps spaced unevenly around the octave sounds like an awkward accident until you inspect its geometry, start rotating it, map how keys overlap, and listen to what musical traditions outside the piano's grid do when they carve up an octave on their own terms.

Step 1 · The ringA scale is a step pattern

Arrange the twelve chromatic pitches around a clock face with C at twelve o'clock, and any scale becomes a necklace of lit beads. The arcs connecting adjacent scale notes show the step sizes, labeled W for a whole step (two semitones), H for a half step (one semitone), and W+H for a three-semitone gap. The ring below opens on C major.

Those two half steps act as landmarks. Because E–F and B–C sit at different distances from each other around the ring, every scale degree has a distinct neighborhood of surrounding intervals, giving your ear an instant sense of orientation. B sits just a semitone below C, which gives melodies reaching B a strong pull upward into home. Switch the pattern to whole tone (six equal whole steps) and every landmark vanishes: every bead sees identical surroundings, so the scale floats without gravity, an effect Debussy leaned on in pieces like Voiles. Switch to pentatonic and the half steps disappear for a different reason, leaving only whole steps and three-semitone leaps, which is why improvising freely on the piano's five black keys rarely produces a harsh clash.

Turn on drone on C, switch back to major (diatonic), and click rotate the pattern →. Keeping C anchored as home while starting the exact same necklace on its second step produces 2 1 2 2 2 1 2; starting on the third gives 1 2 2 2 1 2 2, and so on around the circle. Each rotation relocates the two half steps relative to the C drone, transforming the mood of the exact same tune button. Those seven rotations are the modes.

Step 2 · ModesBrightest to darkest

Instead of ordering the seven modes by necklace rotation, sort them from the highest collection of pitches down to the lowest. A tidy pattern falls out: every step down the ladder lowers exactly one note by a single semitone while all six other notes stay put.

At the top sits Lydian, whose raised fourth (F♯) gives it a bright, buoyant lift familiar from film scores and the Simpsons theme. Lower F♯ to F and you arrive at Ionian, the standard major scale. Lower B to B♭ for Mixolydian, a mainstay of rock and folk ("Norwegian Wood" lives there). Lower E to E♭ to reach Dorian, a minor scale whose raised sixth keeps it brisk and unsentimental ("So What", "Scarborough Fair"). Lower A to A♭ for Aeolian, the natural minor. Lower D to D♭ for Phrygian, whose half step right above the tonic drives flamenco harmonies. One last drop lowers G to G♭ for Locrian, which loses its stable fifth above C and is rarely treated as a home key.

If you keep going one more notch and lower the only unaltered note Locrian has left (the root C down to B), the entire pattern wraps back around into Lydian a semitone lower.

Aside · Where the names come from

Medieval church theorists borrowed labels like Dorian and Phrygian from ancient Greek music treatises and attached them to their own liturgical modes, even though the Greek originals referred to different pitch arrangements. In 1547 the Swiss theorist Heinrich Glarean added Ionian and Aeolian to the list, folding the major and minor scales into the same family.

Step 3 · Why sevenSpread as evenly as possible

Counting rotations and transpositions as equivalents, there are 66 distinct ways to pick seven notes out of twelve. Why did this particular pattern end up dominating so much of music? Evenness gives one clean geometric answer. Since 7 does not divide 12 evenly, seven beads can never sit at identical spacing on a twelve-hour clock, yet you can spread them out as evenly as the semitone grid permits. Approximating steps of 12/7 semitones forces a mix of five 2s and two 1s with the 1s pushed as far apart as possible, which yields the diatonic scale uniquely. Music theorists John Clough and Jack Douthett formalized these shapes in 1991 as maximally even sets, one unique necklace for every note count from 1 to 12.

Sweep the how many notes slider from 3 to 8 and a roster of textbook structures pops out automatically: 3 gives the augmented triad, 4 the diminished seventh chord, 5 the pentatonic scale, 6 the whole-tone scale, 7 the diatonic scale, and 8 the octatonic scale. You can also click any dot on the left chromatic ring to toggle notes by hand and watch the evenness score dip the moment pitches bunch together.

The middle ring plots the exact same pitches in circle-of-fifths order. At 5 notes (C, G, D, A, E) and 7 notes (F, C, G, D, A, E, B), the maximally even set collapses into a single unbroken chain of fifths, the very same chain that grew out of part four's spiral. Spreading notes evenly around the octave and stacking pure fifths converge on the exact same scales.

The horizontal bars on the right tally how many pairs of notes in the scale lie 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 semitones apart. For the diatonic scale those counts come out to 2, 5, 4, 3, 6, and 1: six nonzero integers, all completely different. Theorists call a scale with unique interval multiplicities a deep scale. That uniqueness pays off directly when a piece changes key: transpose a scale by any interval, and the number of notes it retains equals the count for that interval (doubled for the symmetric tritone). Moving a major scale by a fifth keeps 6 of its 7 notes; moving by a whole step keeps 5; by a minor third keeps 4; by a major third keeps 3; and by a half step or tritone keeps just 2.

Aside · Five notes everywhere

Pentatonic scales appear across an extraordinary range of traditions: Scottish and Irish folk songs, Chinese and Japanese classical repertoires, West African melodies, spirituals, and the blues. At the 2009 World Science Festival, Bobby McFerrin got an entire auditorium singing along as he hopped between imaginary notes across the stage; when he jumped to a spot he had never demonstrated, the crowd immediately sang the next degree of the pentatonic scale in unison. Researchers still debate how much of that instinct is acoustic and how much comes from a lifetime of shared musical exposure.

Step 4 · KeysThe circle of fifths as a map

Step 1 rotated the diatonic necklace against a fixed C to generate modes. The other option is to keep the major pattern fixed and slide its starting note somewhere else. Starting W W H W W W H on D gives D major, which preserves every internal interval while shifting the pitch height. Under equal temperament every major key has the exact same internal geometry, so what matters most when composing or improvising is how cleanly one key transitions into another.

The circle of fifths turns that shared-note math from Step 3 into a geographic map. Place keys around a ring one fifth apart, and every adjacent pair shares six of its seven pitches: C major and G major differ only by F versus F♯. Two steps away, five notes overlap; straight across the circle, only two survive.

Each key around the rim shows how many notes it shares with your current home. Click any other key to hear a short modulation: the old home chord, the new key's dominant seventh chord (V7), and finally the new home chord. Stepping one click to a neighbor sounds smooth and natural because six pitches stay in place. Jumping straight across to F♯ feels like a sharp harmonic jolt, which is why composers usually modulate to nearby keys for continuity and save cross-circle leaps for dramatic turns. Click any node on the inner ring to switch to the relative minor keys, which share the exact same pitch collection as their outer major partner while centering the ear on a different tonic.

Written key signatures follow the exact same circle. Each step clockwise adds one sharp in fifth order (F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯, A♯, E♯, B♯), and each step counterclockwise adds one flat in reverse (B♭, E♭, A♭, and so on).

Aside · The first map

The earliest surviving circle-of-fifths diagram appears in Nikolai Diletsky's Grammatika (1679), a composition treatise written in Moscow. Johann David Heinichen published a "musical circle" in Germany in 1711 that became the template for later Western textbooks. Both authors drew the wheel as a practical cheat sheet for figuring out which keys connected smoothly in real compositions.

Step 5 · Other mapsScales between the piano's keys

Everything up to this point has lived on the twelve-semitone grid. Huge swathes of the world's music grew up around fretless strings, voices, and bronze percussion where pitches regularly land right between piano keys. The figure below pins the notes of three major traditions at their tuned cent values directly above a standard twelve-key strip. Every example here is necessarily a sketch: in all three traditions, scale pitches are only one layer alongside melodic phrasing, ornament, and intonation learned by ear.

Arabic maqam. A maqam is assembled from short three- to five-note building blocks called ajnas (singular jins), joined at a shared pivot note. Maqam Rast, for instance, stacks jins Rast on C with a second jins Rast (or Nahawand on the way down) starting on G. Many ajnas feature neutral intervals roughly three-quarters of a tone wide, written with a slashed half-flat sign. While twentieth-century notation often maps these onto a 24-step quarter-tone staff, performers adjust the exact height of an E half-flat depending on the maqam, region, and expressive context. The examples here use the tunings recorded by MaqamWorld: in Rast, E half-flat sits 355 cents above C (45 cents below the piano's E), and descending passages drop the seventh to B♭. In Bayati on D, the second degree sits at 151 cents and the F above it sits 19 cents flatter than an equal-tempered F. Beyond pitches, each maqam carries a traditional melodic journey, its sayr, that defines its character just as strongly as the intervals.

Hindustani rāga. North Indian classical music unfolds over a continuous drone, typically a four-stringed tanpura plucking Sa and Pa (tonic and fifth), so every melodic pitch is judged directly against those sustained overtones. The seven scale degrees are named Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, and Ni; Re, Ga, Dha, and Ni can be lowered (komal), while Ma can be raised (tīvra). Rāga Yaman, played in the evening, uses tīvra Ma (matching Lydian's pitch set) yet follows its own prescribed ascent and descent, climbing Ni Re Ga Ma Dha Ni Sa and bringing Pa in on the way down. Morning rāga Bhairav pairs komal Re and komal Dha with a slow, deliberate oscillation called andolan, which you can hear when playing its notes above. Classical treatises dating back to the Nātyaśāstra describe 22 śrutis per octave, though acoustic studies of live performers starting with Nazir Jairazbhoy and A. W. Stone in 1963 show that fine intonation shifts fluidly with the rāga, phrase direction, and artist. The simple ratios used here (such as 5:4 for Ga and 3:2 for Pa) are a practical approximation of tuning against a drone rather than a rigid specification.

Javanese gamelan. A Javanese gamelan is an ensemble of cast bronze gongs, kettles, and keyed metallophones tuned together at the forge as a single unified set and never matched to another ensemble. Java uses two distinct tuning systems: slendro, a five-note scale with nearly equal wide steps, and pelog, a seven-note scale with sharply contrasting small and large steps from which most pieces draw a five-note pathet subset (try the pelog subset buttons when pelog is selected). No universal standard governs either system, so every gamelan has its own recognizable voice. In 1972 Wasisto Surjodiningrat, P. J. Sudarjana, and Adhi Susanto measured dozens of venerated Central Javanese gamelan; the slendro above averages their data (0, 231, 474, 717, and 955 cents, with an octave slightly stretched to 1208 cents). For pelog, Martin Braun noticed that those same measurements cluster tightly around steps of a nine-equal division of the octave (multiples of 133 cents), which is the model played here. The bronze timbre plays a direct acoustic role too: vibrating metal bars produce non-harmonic overtones, changing which interval sizes fuse cleanly compared with harmonic strings and pipes.

Aside · Measuring other scales

Alexander Ellis invented the cent in 1885 specifically so he could measure and compare instruments like these, including Javanese gamelan brought to London. His measurements convinced him that musical scales are cultural inventions shaped by instruments and aesthetics rather than copies of a single natural template, an insight that took Western music theory decades to fully accept.

Step 6 · ExplorerOne slider for the fifth

Return to the seven-note diatonic family for a moment and bring back the chain-of-fifths view from part four. Pythagorean tuning, quarter-comma meantone, and 12-, 19-, 31-, and 53-equal all share the same underlying recipe: stack identical fifths and fold them into one octave. Every mode is simply a chosen window along that chain (for instance, the major scale spans positions −1 through 5 around C: F, C, G, D, A, E, B). That means a single parameter, the width of the fifth, smoothly retunes every note and every chord of every mode simultaneously: 

whole step  = 2 × fifth − 1200
half step   = 3600 − 5 × fifth
major third = 4 × fifth − 2400     // pure (386.3¢) when the fifth is 696.58¢

The explorer below puts that parameter directly on a slider. Tap the pads, play from your computer keyboard (A S D F G H J K L ; ' climb the scale), or fire the Roman-numeral buttons (I through VII) to audition diatonic triads built from the scale's own degrees.

Slide the fifth all the way down to 685.7 cents and the whole steps shrink until they match the half steps at 171.4 cents apiece: the diatonic scale flattens into seven-equal and every landmark melts away. Push the slider all the way up to 720 cents and the half steps squeeze down to zero until degree 3 collides with degree 4 (E merges into F) and degree 7 collides with the upper octave (B merges into C), collapsing the seven-note scale into a five-equal pentatonic. Every historical keyboard tuning sits in the narrow corridor in between. Press I while sliding across the middle and listen to the major triad pass smoothly through pure stillness at ¼-comma meantone, gentle piano shimmer at 12-equal, and bright roughness at Pythagorean. Andrew Milne, William Sethares, and James Plamondon named this single-parameter sweep the syntonic tuning continuum and built software controllers that let performers morph tunings mid-phrase.

EpilogueSeven notes, and beyond

The piano's familiar white keys look asymmetric at first glance, yet their layout turns out to solve several problems at once. Placing two half steps unevenly around twelve semitones gives every degree its own recognizable neighborhood; rotating that same pattern yields seven modes that step cleanly from bright to dark one lowered pitch at a time; spreading seven notes as evenly as the grid permits automatically produces a seven-fifth chain with unique interval counts, which in turn makes the circle of fifths a smooth map for changing keys. And stepping outside the twelve-note grid into Arabic maqam, Hindustani rāga, and Javanese gamelan shows how much richer pitch space becomes once fixed equal semitones are no longer calling the shots.

A few things worth building next: a rāga synthesizer with continuous pitch glides (meend), since stepped notes miss half of what gives a rāga its voice; an interactive maqam explorer that modulates smoothly from one jins into another on a shared pivot; and pairing the tuning continuum above with an isomorphic hexagonal button grid so every chord keeps the exact same finger shape no matter how wide you stretch the fifth.
