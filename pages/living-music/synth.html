The Synthesizer

Once you have pulled a musical note apart into sines and resonances (as we did in Part 1), the natural next impulse is to reverse the process and build sounds from nothing at all. That spectral mountain range scrolling at the top of the page is already doing it. Each glowing ridge is one slice of time split into frequencies from low on the left to high on the right, with the newest moment in front. Touch or drag anywhere on it and a two-oscillator synthesizer wakes up on the nearest note of a C minor pentatonic scale between C2 and C6: the tall peak under your finger is the fundamental, the smaller peaks trailing off to the right are its harmonics, and dragging upward opens the filter to let more of those harmonics spill through. Until you touch it, a silent stand-in drifts through a slow melody while the ridges are calculated analytically from the patch.

Every figure down the page is a working slice of a real-time synth engine running in an AudioWorklet on the browser's audio thread. It supports up to twelve voices, three synthesis architectures (subtractive, FM, and physical string modeling), and streams live telemetry back about 75 times a second so every trace and meter reflects what the audio thread is actually computing. You can play any figure with the on-screen keys, your computer keyboard (A through K span an octave of white keys from C, continuing onto L, ;, and ', with W E T Y U O P on the black keys and Z/X shifting the octave), or a USB MIDI controller via the MIDI button under any keyboard strip. Keyboard and MIDI input follow whichever figure you last touched (or the one sitting squarely in view, outlined with a soft warm ring), and audio gently fades out as soon as you scroll away.

Step 1 · OscillatorsFour shapes

Every electronic synthesizer begins with an oscillator, a circuit or loop of code that repeats one cycle of a waveform over and over. Its repetition rate sets the pitch, and its geometric shape determines the starting stack of harmonics. Four classic shapes form the basic vocabulary of synthesis:

  • Sine: a single fundamental with zero overtones. Pure and plain.
  • Triangle: odd harmonics only, dropping steeply as 1/n² (12 dB per octave). Round and flute-like.
  • Square: odd harmonics again, falling more gently as 1/n (6 dB per octave). Hollow and reedy.
  • Saw: every integer harmonic at 1/n. Dense, bright, and buzzy.

Pick a shape below and hold a key. The left panel shows two cycles of the ideal geometric curve, and while a note rings, the glowing orange trace on top is the real audio signal measured at the output. On the right, colored bars plot the theoretical harmonic recipe in decibels relative to the fundamental, with live white dots verifying each measured harmonic straight from the speaker signal.

The fifth button, pulse, generalizes the square wave. Instead of spending half of each cycle high and half low, a pulse stays high for a fraction d (its pulse width) and low for the remainder, giving harmonic amplitudes proportional to |sin(πnd)|/n. At d = 50% you get the classic square wave. Slide it to 25% and sin(πn/4) hits zero for every fourth harmonic, knocking out 4, 8, 12, and so on. At 20% every fifth harmonic vanishes. Narrow pulses sound reedy and nasal, and gently sweeping the pulse width during a held note creates one of the richest animation tricks in analog synthesis (we'll wire an LFO to it in Step 5).

Synth designers lean heavily on the sawtooth because it contains every harmonic right out of the gate, giving you raw marble to carve away with filters. Before we get to carving, though, generating a clean sawtooth inside a digital computer turns out to hide a nasty trap.

Aside · Why these shapes

These four waveforms became canonical simply because they are easy to generate with a handful of analog components. A typical analog oscillator trickles a constant current into a capacitor, producing a linear voltage ramp, and abruptly discharges it to zero at a threshold: instant sawtooth. Feed that ramp into a comparator against a reference voltage and you get a variable-width pulse. Simple wave-shaping circuits then fold the ramp into a triangle and soften the triangle's corners into a close approximation of a sine.

Step 2 · Going digitalAliasing

In code, the most tempting way to synthesize a sawtooth is to advance a phase counter t from 0 to 1 each cycle and output 2 * t - 1. That is the naive setting below. Down in the bass it sounds passable, yet climb a few octaves and a harsh, metallic whine creeps underneath the note. The culprit is the instantaneous vertical reset. A mathematically crisp jump contains harmonics stretching to infinite frequency, whereas digital audio sampled at 48 kHz (the figure uses your device's sample rate) can only represent frequencies up to half that rate, 24 kHz, known as the Nyquist frequency. Harmonics generated above Nyquist fold right back down into the audible range: energy that belongs at 25 kHz shows up at 23 kHz, and energy at 30 kHz lands at 18 kHz. These reflected ghosts are aliases, and because their frequencies rarely align with the note's harmonic series, they sound glaringly out of tune.

The spectrogram below runs our engine's own oscillator code through a slow sweep from A3 up to A7 and back down (eight seconds each way), with time across the horizontal axis and frequency climbing up to the pink Nyquist line at the top. Watch the bright real harmonics fan upward as the pitch rises. On naive, every line that strikes the top ceiling ricochets downward, moving in the opposite direction as the note climbs. Click sweep to listen. Beneath the spectrogram sits a single-pitch snapshot (at A6, 1.76 kHz, until you play another key), separating true harmonics in blue-to-gold from stray aliases in red.

At A6, only 13 true harmonics fit beneath 24 kHz, while the naive saw sprays over a hundred audible aliases across the band; the loudest below 5 kHz sits just 28 dB beneath the fundamental, loud enough to cut straight through the mix. Switching to the other two modes shows the two classic ways to clean it up.

additive constructs the sawtooth directly from Fourier's recipe, summing only the sine waves that fall below Nyquist. Because no frequency ever crosses the ceiling, aliasing drops straight down to the measurement floor around −93 dB. The catch is raw CPU cost: every single sample requires summing every partial, which is 109 sines per sample down at A3 and hundreds more in the sub-bass.

band-limited (polyBLEP) takes a far cheaper shortcut. All the infinite high-frequency energy of a sawtooth comes from the instantaneous discontinuity at the reset edge, affecting just the one or two samples straddling the jump. Subtracting a tiny quadratic polynomial (a Polynomial Band-Limited Step) right across those two samples rounds the step smoothly enough to suppress the reflections at constant cost per sample, whatever the pitch. This is what the rest of the page's engine actually runs: 

// t: phase in [0, 1); dt: phase step per sample (frequency / sample rate)
function polyblep(t, dt) {
  if (t < dt) {
    const x = t / dt;
    return x + x - x * x - 1;
  }
  if (t > 1 - dt) {
    const x = (t - 1) / dt;
    return x * x + x + x + 1;
  }
  return 0;
}

saw = 2 * t - 1 - polyblep(t, dt);

Those ten lines knock A6's loudest alias down to −70 dB, buried safely beneath the psychoacoustic masking of the note itself, for just a handful of multiplications per sample. Square and pulse waves apply the same correction at both vertical transitions, and triangles (which have sharp slope corners rather than vertical jumps) get the integrated cousin of the polynomial, called a polyBLAMP.

Why the aliases fall as the note rises

At a 48 kHz sample rate, a discrete sequence of samples is mathematically identical for frequency f and for 48,000 − f (and for f + 48,000). Any overtone trying to climb past 24 kHz mirrors across that line, reappearing at 48 kHz minus its true frequency. Raising the played note pushes the true harmonic higher, which drives 48 kHz minus that harmonic downward. For A6 (1.76 kHz), the 14th harmonic belongs at 24.64 kHz and folds back to 23.36 kHz; the 20th belongs at 35.2 kHz and lands all the way down at 12.8 kHz.

Try switching the wave to square in the naive mode too: because a square wave contains only odd harmonics, its reflected lattice has half as many lines as the saw's.

Aside · Analog shapes, digitally

Eliminating digital oscillator aliasing kept computer music researchers busy throughout the 1990s. Tim Stilson and Julius Smith at Stanford introduced band-limited impulse trains (BLIT) in 1996, sparking a family of analytical step-correction methods. Vesa Välimäki and Antti Huovilainen popularized the lightweight polynomial approximation used here in their 2007 antialiasing survey. Modern software synths almost all rely on descendants of PolyBLEP or on banks of pre-band-limited wavetables swapped by pitch register.

Step 3 · Subtractive synthesisThe resonant filter

In Part 1, passing a raw buzz through resonant cavities turned it into a human vowel. Most classic synthesizers work on the exact same principle, called subtractive synthesis: start with a bright harmonic-rich waveform and sculpt it down with a lowpass filter that passes frequencies below a chosen cutoff and progressively attenuates everything above it.

Our engine models Robert Moog's famous transistor ladder filter: four simple one-pole lowpass stages chained in series, each sloping off at 6 dB per octave above the cutoff for a combined 24 dB per octave drop (the slope buttons tap the ladder after 1, 2, 3, or 4 stages). Adding resonance feeds a fraction k of the fourth stage's output back to the input with its polarity inverted. Right around the cutoff frequency, the four stages collectively lag the signal by half a cycle (180°), turning that negative feedback into positive reinforcement and pushing up a sharp resonant spike. With no feedback (k = 0), the four-pole cascade sits 12 dB down at the cutoff. At k = 2 the peak climbs back to 0 dB, at k = 3 it pokes 8 dB upward, and at k = 4 the loop gain reaches unity: the filter breaks into pure self-oscillation and sings a sine wave at the cutoff frequency even with zero input.

Drag the glowing orange handle right on the plot (or use the cutoff and resonance sliders). Moving sideways shifts the cutoff from 40 Hz to 16 kHz; lifting vertically pushes up the resonance. Faint vertical lines show the raw saw harmonics entering the filter, bright bars show what survives at the output, and while you hold a key, a thin white curve traces the live measured spectrum right over them.

Hold a low note with resonance around 3 and drag the cutoff slowly across the frequency axis: you will hear the narrow resonant peak spotlight one individual harmonic after another, creating the unmistakable liquid sweep of analog synth records. Switch the source to noise and raise resonance toward 4 to hear white hiss condense into a pure whistle tuned to the cutoff. Flipping between 6 dB/oct and 24 makes the slope difference visceral: a single pole barely dulls a saw, while all four poles strip a saw nearly down to a sine wave a couple of octaves above the cutoff.

How the engine runs the ladder

Digitizing a resonant feedback loop has a subtle catch: the filter output you want to feed back depends instantaneously on the current input sample you haven't finished computing yet. Inserting a naive one-sample delay into the feedback path detunes the resonant peak and makes high cutoffs behave erratically. Instead, our engine solves the algebraic loop simultaneously at every sample using Vadim Zavalishin's zero-delay-feedback (topology-preserving) integrator. Each of the four stages takes just three lines of arithmetic: 

// G = g / (1 + g), where g = tan(π · cutoff / sample rate)
let t = (u - v.s1) * G;
const y1 = t + v.s1;
v.s1 = y1 + t;

Wrapping the input in Math.tanh softly saturates the signal so self-oscillation stabilizes at a clean amplitude, echoing the natural soft clipping of Moog's transistor pairs. One quirk also carries straight over from the analog circuit: cranking resonance steals bass, because inverted feedback cancels low frequencies too (costing 14 dB of low end at k = 4). The engine partially compensates by multiplying the output by 1 + k/2, reducing that bass dip to 4.4 dB.

Aside · Moog and Buchla

Robert Moog filed the patent on his transistor ladder filter in October 1966 (granted in 1969). It sat at the heart of the modular cabinets he had been shipping since 1964 and the portable Minimoog of 1970, anchoring the punchy bass sound still tied to his name. Wendy Carlos's 1968 album Switched-On Bach, tracked painstakingly on a Moog modular, brought that sound into millions of living rooms.

On the West Coast at nearly the same time, composers Morton Subotnick and Ramon Sender at the San Francisco Tape Music Center commissioned Don Buchla (1963) to build a live electronic instrument. Buchla skipped the piano keyboard in favor of touch-sensitive capacitive plates and focused on wavefolders and complex oscillator modulation over subtractive filtering. Synth builders still loosely call these philosophies "East Coast" and "West Coast" synthesis.

Step 4 · Shape in timeEnvelopes

A sawtooth parked behind a stationary filter drones identically for as long as you hold a key, whereas acoustic instruments evolve continuously from attack to silence. A piano string bursts out brightest and loudest the instant the felt hammer rebounds; a French horn blossoms in both loudness and harmonic sparkle as the player leans into the breath. Synthesizers sculpt that motion with envelopes: one-shot control curves triggered every time a key goes down. The classic design breaks each note into four stages, abbreviated ADSR:

  • Attack: how quickly the level climbs from zero to peak when a key is pressed.
  • Decay: how long it takes to settle from the peak down to the sustain level.
  • Sustain: the steady level held while your finger remains on the key.
  • Release: how long the tail takes to fade back to zero once you lift off.

The patch below wires up two independent envelopes. The blue curve on top controls overall volume; the amber curve below sweeps the filter cutoff upward by the amount set on envelope → cutoff (0 to 6 octaves) before letting it fall back. Drag the glowing corner handles directly on either curve, or step through the five presets. While you play, a bright dot for each active voice rides along both curves using live telemetry from the engine, and the strip along the bottom records the last three seconds of audio, shifting color from cool blue to warm orange as the filter opens.

Compare brass and pluck to see how much personality lives strictly in timing. On brass, volume rises in 60 ms while the filter takes a slightly lazier 90 ms to swing three octaves upward from 320 Hz to 2.56 kHz, making the note bloom brighter as it swells before relaxing into sustain. On pluck, sustain drops to zero on both envelopes and the filter snaps shut twice as fast as the volume decays, so the upper harmonics die away first and leave a warm tail, much like a real plucked string.

Notice the gentle bend in every segment. Attack curves follow an analog RC charging curve aimed at 1.5 and clamped at 1.0, giving a brisk initial lift that eases into the peak. Decay and release are true exponentials whose times specify how long it takes to fall 60 dB (effectively to silence), matching our logarithmic perception of loudness so a fade sounds smooth rather than rushing to a cliff at the end.

Aside · Where ADSR came from

The four-parameter ADSR layout traces back to Vladimir Ussachevsky at the Columbia–Princeton Electronic Music Center, who asked Robert Moog in the mid-1960s to build an envelope generator with independent attack, initial decay, sustain level, and final release controls. It proved so flexible that it became the default control layout across nearly sixty years of synthesizers.

Step 5 · ModulationLFOs

Whereas an envelope triggers once per keypress, a low-frequency oscillator (LFO) cycles continuously in the background, acting as an invisible hand turning a knob back and forth. Below, a single LFO on the left patches into four destinations on the right, with each lane displaying the real-time parameter value reaching the synth engine. Push up any destination slider (or choose a preset) to bring its patch cable alive:

  • → pitch: vibrato. Around 5 to 6 Hz at ±25 cents evokes a sung or bowed vibrato.
  • → cutoff: a rhythmic wah sweeping the filter up and down.
  • → volume: tremolo, pulsing loudness like a guitar amp.
  • → pulse width: pulse-width modulation (PWM), continuously sliding the square wave through narrower pulses and back so one oscillator thickens into a chorus of detuned voices.

Try the random wave shape (or the random preset, which patches it to cutoff at 7 Hz): it grabs a fresh random value once per cycle and holds it flat, an old circuit trick called sample-and-hold responsible for countless bubbling 1970s sci-fi computer effects. If you have a MIDI keyboard plugged in, its mod wheel is wired to add up to 30 cents of vibrato across every figure on this page.

Now click audio rate and hold a note. All this preset does is crank vibrato to an absurd extreme: a 110 Hz LFO throwing the oscillator's pitch by ±900 cents. Once modulation crosses roughly 20 Hz, your ear can no longer track individual wobbles as motion in time. Look down at the live spectrum strip: brand-new partials have sprung into existence on either side of the original harmonics, transforming the tone into something clanging and metallic. Pushing modulation into audio rates opens up an entire synthesis world of its own.

Step 6 · Frequency modulationFM: bells from two sines

Take two pure sine oscillators. One, the carrier, goes straight to your speakers. The other, the modulator, stays unheard on its own and instead wiggles the carrier's phase rapidly back and forth at an audio frequency: 

y(t) = sin(2π·fc·t + I · sin(2π·fm·t))

Even though both ingredients are completely pure sines, warping the carrier's phase generates an entire family of new frequencies called sidebands at fc ± n·fm for every integer n. How strongly the modulator pushes is controlled by the modulation index I, and the amplitude of the nth sideband pair is given exactly by the Bessel function Jn(I). At I = 0 you hear only the lone carrier. Raise I and energy spills outward into roughly I + 1 sideband pairs on each side. Because Bessel functions oscillate above and below zero, individual components swell, vanish, and flip phase as you sweep the index: right at I = 2.405, J0 crosses zero and the carrier itself disappears.

The figure below plots the analytical Bessel spectrum on the top left (scaled in multiples of the played note, with negative-frequency sidebands reflected back above 0 Hz in pink), the resulting warped waveform underneath it, and J0 through J5 on the right. Drag directly across the Bessel graph or move the index I slider to sweep the modulation depth.

The frequency ratio between carrier and modulator (c:m) controls whether the resulting spectrum sounds harmonic or metallic. Every sideband lands at f·(c + n·m). Pick small integers and every partial snaps onto a clean harmonic grid: 1:1 produces every harmonic like a bright sawtooth, while 1:2 puts energy exclusively on odd harmonics like a square wave. Pick an incommensurate-looking ratio like 1:1.4 (7:5), however, and sidebands scatter onto multiples of f/5 with no neat fundamental spacing for your ear to lock onto, producing the shimmering clang of a struck bronze bell.

Just as with subtractive filters, the real magic happens when the modulation index moves over the life of a note. Wiring an envelope to the modulator lets the index curve evolve automatically, and while a note sounds, the bright white cursor on the Bessel graph glides along in real time. On bell (1:1.4), the index plunges from 10 down toward zero as the bell rings, thinning a dense crash of metallic partials into a calm pure sine tail. On brass (1:1, peak index 5), the index rises and falls alongside loudness so louder notes automatically blow brighter. These presets adapt the classic examples from John Chowning's 1973 paper, including his wood drum at a 1:0.6875 (80:55) ratio whose huge index of 25 collapses in a few tens of milliseconds, alongside reed-inspired clarinet (3:2) and bassoon (5:1) patches.

Aside · Chowning, Stanford and Yamaha

John Chowning stumbled onto FM synthesis at Stanford in 1967 while pushing digital vibrato far beyond human speeds and depths, and published the theory in 1973. Yamaha secured an exclusive license from Stanford in 1975 and spent nearly eight years designing custom digital chips cheap enough for a keyboard instrument. When the Yamaha DX7 arrived in 1983, housing six sine operators that could be rewired into 32 routing topologies called algorithms, its glassy electric pianos, punchy basses, and sparkling bells swept across 1980s pop music and turned Chowning's patent into one of Stanford's most lucrative licenses.

Strictly speaking, modulating the carrier's phase as written above is phase modulation (and that is what the DX7 computes as well). For sinusoidal modulators it yields the exact same Bessel sidebands as true frequency modulation while keeping pitch rock-solid whenever the index changes rapidly.

Step 7 · Physical modelingA string in a delay line

Subtractive and FM synthesis both start from spectra and aim to sculpt harmonics directly. Physical modeling takes a completely different route: simulate a simplified physical object and let its natural dynamics generate the sound. One of the most disarmingly simple algorithms in all of audio programming is the Karplus–Strong plucked string. Allocate a short circular buffer of samples, stuff it full of random noise, and play it in a loop. Each time a sample passes the read head, replace it with the average of itself and its neighbor. That tiny loop (scarcely five lines of arithmetic) sings out like a real plucked guitar string.

It works because a circular delay line mirrors the physics of a vibrating string. On a guitar, a pluck sends a wave racing to the bridge and back once per period; a circular buffer of N samples repeats every N samples, tuning its fundamental frequency to the sample rate divided by N (about 436 samples for A2 at 48 kHz). The initial noise burst injects energy at every frequency simultaneously, just like a sharp pluck, and circulating around the loop reinforces only waves that fit an integer number of times around the ring, turning raw hiss into a full harmonic series in one trip. Finally, averaging two adjacent samples acts as a gentle lowpass filter that attenuates high frequencies more than low ones on every lap, causing upper harmonics to mellow and fade first, exactly the way internal friction damps a real steel or nylon string.

The left panel draws that circular delay line as a glowing ring, with the read/filter/write head pinned at twelve o'clock. Play a low note and watch the jagged noise burst smooth out into a clean, shrinking wave as it circles. Top right shows the live DFT of the harmonics currently circulating in the ring (with each odd harmonic's predicted 60 dB ring time printed above), and the bottom right plots output loudness in decibels, where exponential decay traces a crisp straight downward slope.

Inside the audio worklet, the entire string loop comes down to this per-sample update:

let y = (1 - v.wgt) * buf[r1] + v.wgt * buf[r2]; // wgt = ½: the plain average
if (ks.frac) {
  // all-pass filter: adds the fractional sample the integer loop is short by
  const ap = v.C * y + v.apx - v.C * v.apy;
  v.apx = y;
  v.apy = ap;
  y = ap;
}
y *= v.rho; // loop gain, set from the decay time
if (blend < 1 && rand() >= blend) y = -y; // the drum/bottle variant, below
buf[v.w] = y;

In its raw textbook form, the loop suffers from three practical headaches, all solved by David Jaffe and Julius Smith in a companion paper published right beside Karplus and Strong's original 1983 article. The controls below the keyboard expose each fix:

  • Pitch-dependent decay: a plain two-point average barely damps very low frequencies, so an unattenuated A2 fundamental would ring for roughly 40 minutes while A6 vanished in under a second. Multiplying each pass by a loop gain ρ just under 1 sets the fundamental's ring time directly via the decay time slider (0.3 to 30 seconds).
  • Over-damped treble: on brighter instruments like harpsichords or steel strings, the plain 50/50 average kills high harmonics too quickly. Raising decay stretch S biases the average toward the current sample, weakening the lowpass filter so upper partials ring longer (bright steel uses S = 6).
  • Quantized tuning: an integer buffer length N plus the filter's half-sample lag can land up to half a sample off the desired pitch. At A2 that is at most 2 cents, barely noticeable; up at C6, where the whole loop is only 46 samples long, half a sample throws the note up to 19 cents out of tune. Click fractional delay off and play high notes to see the tuning error in the readout; with it on, a one-pole all-pass filter supplies the missing fraction of a sample.

Two more sliders shape the pluck itself. pick position runs the initial noise through a comb filter that cancels every kth harmonic for a pick at 1/k of the string length, reproducing the exact nodal notches from Part 1, while pluck pre-filters the noise to trade a crisp plectrum snap for a soft thumb.

Finally, blend b controls Karplus and Strong's quirky percussion extension. At b = 1 every pass keeps its sign and behaves like a string. Lower b and each sample randomly flips sign with probability 1 − b. Right at drum (b = ½), random inversions break up periodic repetition completely, turning the delay line into a resonant drum thud whose decay length depends on the note you hit. Push all the way to bottle (b = 0) and every sample inverts on every single lap, doubling the loop period: the pitch plunges an octave and retains only odd harmonics, sounding uncannily like blowing across a glass bottle.

Aside · Karplus, Strong, and waveguides

Kevin Karplus and Alex Strong published the algorithm in the Computer Music Journal in 1983, partly inspired by how ridiculously cheap it was to run: the core averaging loop needs only additions and a bit shift, and Strong got it running in real time on an 8-bit Intel 8080A. Julius Smith soon generalized the delay-line insight into digital waveguide synthesis, using paired delay lines to model traveling waves in both directions along strings and wind-instrument bores, technology Yamaha licensed from Stanford for its physical-modeling VL1 synthesizer in 1994.

Keep an eye on the VOICES box while you stack chords. How the figures share one engine

Every interactive figure on this page instantiates the same plain-JavaScript AudioWorklet processor on the browser's audio thread. The main thread sends patch updates (oscillators, filter, envelopes, LFO, FM operators, or string parameters) and note events over a MessagePort. Every five render quanta (640 samples, roughly 13 ms at 48 kHz), the worklet posts back each voice's envelope stages and levels, live filter cutoff, LFO phase, the newest string delay buffer, and short waveforms tapped after the oscillators, after the filter, and at the final output.

Before leaving the worklet, every figure passes through a −3 dBFS peak limiter and a soft clipper (followed by the site's master bus) so dense chords or runaway resonance stay comfortable on headphones. In environments without AudioWorklet support, the exact same processor file runs on the main thread and streams short audio buffers with about 0.1 s of extra latency.

EpilogueWhat carries over

Building all three engines inside one piece of code makes it hard to miss how tightly these seemingly rival traditions connect. Subtractive synthesis starts with an abundance of harmonics and shaves them back with filters; FM starts with pure sines and multiplies new sidebands outward; Karplus–Strong fills a loop with noise and lets physics filter it into a harmonic series. All three are simply different handles for steering the same underlying object: which frequencies exist at each millisecond of a note.

Time turns out to do just as much heavy lifting as static tone color. A frozen snapshot of our brass patch or FM bell barely hints at what it is, yet let the filter bloom slightly behind the volume or let the modulation index collapse as the note decays and your ear immediately hears horn or bronze. And moving those ideas into digital code adds its own craft: an edge that costs two transistors in analog hardware needs PolyBLEP math to avoid aliasing, a zero-delay feedback loop to keep resonance true, and an all-pass filter to tune a delay line to a fraction of a sample.

If I keep expanding this engine, I would love to add morphing wavetable and granular oscillators, couple the Karplus–Strong delay line to a resonant guitar body model, wire up all six DX7 operators with selectable algorithms, and pack custom patches into shareable URLs. Meanwhile, the spectral landscape at the top of the page is built straight out of the modules we just assembled: a two-oscillator subtractive patch from Step 3, shaped by the envelopes of Step 4 and nudged by a touch of vibrato from Step 5.
