Living Music · Part 2

The Synthesizer

Making sounds from scratch, one module at a time. The landscape above is the live spectrum of a small synth. Touch it to play: across is pitch, up opens the filter.

Scroll down to read ↓

Each ridge at the top of the page is one moment of sound split into frequencies, low on the left and high on the right, with the newest ridge in front. Tap or drag on it and a two-oscillator synth plays the nearest note of a C minor pentatonic scale between C2 and C6. The tall ridge under your finger is the note itself, and the ridges trailing off to the right are its harmonics. Move up and the filter opens, so more of those harmonics get through. Until you play, a silent stand-in performs a slow phrase and the ridges are computed from the patch instead of measured.

Part one took notes apart into stacks of sine waves. This part goes the other way. It builds a synthesizer up one piece at a time, from a single oscillator to FM bells and a plucked string, and every figure is playable. Use your computer keyboard (A to K are the white keys from C, W E T Y U O P the black keys, Z and X shift the octave), a MIDI keyboard (press the MIDI button under any of the on-screen keyboards), or the on-screen keys. Keys and MIDI go to the figure you touched last, or to the one most in view, and that figure gets a soft ring. Sound only starts after you press something, and each figure fades out when you scroll away from it.

Every sound on this page comes from one synth engine I wrote for it, running in an AudioWorklet (the browser's real-time audio thread). It has twelve voices, three ways of making sound, and it reports its internal state back about 75 times a second, so the figures draw what the engine is doing rather than a guess at it.

Step 1 · OscillatorsFour shapes

A synthesizer starts with an oscillator, something that repeats one cycle of a wave over and over. The rate sets the pitch and the shape sets the timbre. Part one showed that any repeating shape is a stack of harmonics. The classic shapes are worth knowing by their recipes, because the rest of this page is about changing those recipes.

  • Sine: one harmonic and nothing else. Pure and a little dull.
  • Triangle: only the odd harmonics, falling as 1/n², which is 12 dB per octave. Soft, like a flute.
  • Square: the odd harmonics again, but falling only as 1/n (6 dB per octave). Hollow and reedy.
  • Saw: every harmonic, at 1/n. The brightest and buzziest of the four.

Pick a shape and play. The left panel draws two cycles of the ideal shape, and while a note sounds the orange trace on top of it is the real signal, taken from the audio output. The bars are the recipe, in decibels relative to the first harmonic, and the white dots are the same harmonics measured from the output.

The fifth button is the square's generalisation, the pulse. It's high for a fraction d of each cycle and low for the rest, and its harmonics have amplitude |sin(πnd)|/n. At d = 50% that's the square. At 25% the sine term is zero for every fourth harmonic, so harmonics 4, 8, 12 and so on vanish. At 20% every fifth goes. Narrow pulses sound thin and nasal, and sweeping the width while a note plays is one of the oldest synth sounds there is (step 5 does it with an LFO).

The saw is the one synthesizer designers reach for first. It has every harmonic, so it can be carved into almost anything. That carving is the next three steps. First, though, there's a problem with making a saw in a computer at all.

Aside · Why these shapes

They're the shapes that are cheap to build out of analog circuits. A typical analog oscillator charges a capacitor with a constant current, which makes a straight ramp, and snaps it back to zero when it crosses a threshold: that's a saw. Compare the saw against a second voltage and you get a pulse whose width is set by that voltage. Other shaping circuits fold the saw into a triangle and round the triangle into something close to a sine.

Step 2 · Going digitalAliasing

The obvious way to make a saw in code is to keep a phase t that climbs from 0 to 1 once per cycle and output 2t − 1. That's the "naive" button below. It sounds fine for low notes and gets nasty as you go up. The reason is the jump. An ideal saw drops instantly, and an instant drop has harmonics that go on forever. Audio sampled at 48 kHz (a common rate; the figure uses your device's) can only hold frequencies up to half that, 24 kHz, called the Nyquist frequency. Anything above it doesn't disappear. It folds back down: a harmonic that should be at 25 kHz shows up at 23 kHz, one at 30 kHz at 18 kHz. These folded harmonics are aliases, and they land at frequencies that have nothing to do with the note.

The spectrogram shows a saw swept from A3 up to A7 and back down, eight seconds each way, computed with the engine's own oscillator code. Time runs left to right and frequency bottom to top, with the Nyquist frequency at the top edge. The real harmonics fan upward as the pitch climbs. With the naive oscillator the ones that hit the top bounce back down, and as the pitch keeps rising they move down, the wrong way. Press play to hear the sweep. The lower panel is the spectrum at a single pitch, A6 (1.76 kHz) until you play something: harmonics in colour, aliases in red.

At A6 only 13 harmonics fit under 24 kHz, and the naive saw's loudest alias below 5 kHz is 28 dB below the note, easily loud enough to hear as a whine that doesn't belong. There are two ways to fix it, and the other two buttons are those fixes.

Additive builds the saw from its recipe, adding up sines only up to the Nyquist frequency. There's nothing to fold because nothing goes past the top. It's exact, but it costs one step per harmonic per sample: 109 harmonics at A3. In the figure the aliases drop to about −93 dB, which is the floor of the measurement.

PolyBLEP keeps the cheap naive saw and patches the jump. The trouble is concentrated in the one or two samples around each drop, so you subtract a small polynomial there that turns the instant step into a band-limited one. This is the version the engine uses everywhere else on the page: 

// t: phase in [0, 1); dt: phase step per sample (frequency / sample rate)
function polyblep(t, dt) {
  if (t < dt) {
    const x = t / dt;
    return x + x - x * x - 1;
  }
  if (t > 1 - dt) {
    const x = (t - 1) / dt;
    return x * x + x + x + 1;
  }
  return 0;
}

saw = 2 * t - 1 - polyblep(t, dt);

That brings A6's loudest alias down to −70 dB, quiet enough to be masked by the note, for a few multiplies per sample whatever the pitch. The square gets the same treatment at both of its edges. The triangle has no jumps, only corners, and gets the integrated version of the same patch (a "polyBLAMP").

Why the aliases fall as the note rises

A sampled signal can't tell a frequency f from 48,000 − f (or from f + 48,000). Each harmonic that should be above 24 kHz shows up mirrored about that line, at 48 kHz minus its true frequency. Raise the note and the true frequency goes up, so the mirrored one goes down. The 14th harmonic of A6 belongs at 24.64 kHz and appears at 23.36 kHz; the 20th belongs at 35.2 kHz and appears at 12.8 kHz.

The naive square looks a little different in the spectrogram from the naive saw because it has only odd harmonics, so it has half as many aliases.

Aside · Analog shapes, digitally

Making alias-free versions of analog waveforms was a research problem through the 1990s. Tim Stilson and Julius Smith at Stanford published band-limited impulse trains (BLIT) in 1996, and the step-correction idea grew out of that line of work. The polynomial shortcut used here was described by Vesa Välimäki and Antti Huovilainen in a 2007 survey of antialiasing oscillators. Most software synths do something in this family, or use tables of pre-band-limited waves.

Step 3 · Subtractive synthesisThe resonant filter

Part one ended with a voice built from a buzz and four filters. That's the idea behind most of the synthesizers ever made: start with a bright oscillator and take away what you don't want. This is subtractive synthesis, and the tool for taking away is a lowpass filter. It passes everything below its cutoff frequency and turns down everything above it.

The filter in the engine is modelled on Robert Moog's transistor ladder: four simple one-pole lowpass stages in a row, each cutting 6 dB per octave above the cutoff, so the whole chain cuts 24 dB per octave. The buttons tap the ladder after one, two, three or four stages. Then there's resonance: some of the output is fed back to the input, upside down. Around the cutoff the four stages delay the signal by half a cycle, so there the feedback adds instead of cancels, and a peak grows. With no feedback (k = 0) the ladder is already 12 dB down at the cutoff. At k = 2 the peak is back to 0 dB, at k = 3 it's 8 dB up, and at k = 4 the loop gain reaches one: the filter rings at its cutoff with no input at all.

Drag the glowing handle. Sideways moves the cutoff (40 Hz to 16 kHz) and up raises the resonance. The orange curve is the filter's response, the faint bars are the saw's harmonics going in, and the bright bars are what comes out, the input times the response. While you play, the thin white line is the measured spectrum of what you're hearing, and it should sit on the bright bars.

A few things to try. Sweep the cutoff slowly on a held low note with the resonance around 3, and the peak picks out one harmonic after another: that's the classic filter sweep. Switch the source to noise and push the resonance near 4, and the noise turns into a pitched whistle at the cutoff. And compare the slopes: 6 dB per octave barely changes a saw, while 24 dB per octave turns it nearly into a sine a couple of octaves above the cutoff.

How the engine runs the ladder

The feedback loop is the hard part in a digital filter: the output you need to feed back depends on the input you're computing. Naive versions add a sample of delay to the loop, which detunes the peak and makes the resonance misbehave at high cutoffs. The engine solves the loop for the current sample instead, using Vadim Zavalishin's "topology-preserving" form of each stage. One stage is three lines: 

// G = g / (1 + g), where g = tan(π · cutoff / sample rate)
let t = (u - v.s1) * G;
const y1 = t + v.s1;
v.s1 = y1 + t;

A tanh on the input keeps self-oscillation from growing without bound, which is also roughly what the transistors do. One more detail comes from the real circuit: resonance costs bass. The feedback cancels low frequencies too, and at k = 4 the ladder's low end is 14 dB down. The engine gives some of that back, multiplying the output by 1 + k/2, so at k = 4 the bass is only 4.4 dB down.

Aside · Moog and Buchla

Robert Moog filed the patent for his ladder filter in October 1966 and it was granted in 1969. It went into the modular systems he'd been building since 1964 and then into the Minimoog of 1970, and it's a large part of why "Moog" still means a particular kind of bass. Wendy Carlos's Switched-On Bach (1968), recorded on a Moog modular, introduced a lot of people to the sound.

On the other coast, Morton Subotnick and Ramon Sender of the San Francisco Tape Music Center commissioned Don Buchla in 1963 to build an instrument for live electronic music. His 100 series used touch-sensitive metal plates instead of a keyboard, and his designs leaned on shaping the oscillators themselves rather than filtering them. People still divide synthesis into the "East Coast" and "West Coast" schools after these two.

Step 4 · Shape in timeEnvelopes

A held saw through a fixed filter is the same sound from start to finish, and real instruments never are. A piano note is loudest and brightest the moment the hammer lands. A trumpet note swells and gets brighter as the player pushes. Synthesizers handle this with envelopes, control signals that rise and fall each time a key goes down. The standard one has four parts:

  • Attack: how long it takes to rise to full when the key goes down.
  • Decay: how long it takes to fall from there to the sustain level.
  • Sustain: the level it holds for as long as the key stays down.
  • Release: how long it takes to fade out after the key comes up.

The figure has two. The top one sets the volume. The bottom one pushes the filter's cutoff up by up to the "envelope → cutoff" amount (0 to 6 octaves) and lets it settle back. Drag the corners to change the times and the sustain level, or start from a preset. While you hold a key, a dot per voice rides each curve at the level the engine reports, and the strip at the bottom draws the output over the last three seconds or so, tinted by how open the filter is.

Brass is the one to study. The volume rises in 60 ms, but the filter envelope takes 90 ms and lifts the cutoff three octaves, from 320 Hz to 2.56 kHz, so the note gets brighter as it gets louder and then settles. "Pluck" has no sustain at all. Both envelopes fall straight to zero, and the filter closes faster than the volume fades, so the note goes dull as it dies, the way a struck string does.

The curves aren't straight lines. The attack is a capacitor-style charge aimed at 1.5 that stops when it reaches 1, so it arrives in the attack time and bends like the analog circuits did. Decay and release are exponentials, and their times are the time to fall 60 dB, which is most of the way to silence. That matches how hearing works: a sound that falls by the same number of decibels every second sounds like a steady fade, and an exponential does that.

Aside · Where ADSR came from

The four-stage envelope is usually credited to the composer Vladimir Ussachevsky, who ran the Columbia–Princeton Electronic Music Center and asked Moog for an envelope with those controls in the mid-1960s. It became the default on almost every synthesizer since.

Step 5 · ModulationLFOs

Envelopes move once per note. A low-frequency oscillator, or LFO, moves continuously: it's an oscillator like the first one, but slow, and instead of being heard it's patched into something else. The figure's LFO (left) is wired to four destinations (right), and each lane draws the value that destination is getting right now. Raise a depth to connect it.

  • Pitch gives vibrato. Five or six cycles a second at ±25 cents is a singer's.
  • Cutoff gives a wah, the filter opening and closing.
  • Volume gives tremolo.
  • Pulse width sweeps the square through narrower pulses and back, which makes a single oscillator sound like several slightly out of tune.

The "random" shape picks a new value at the start of every cycle and holds it, called sample and hold. On the cutoff it gives the bubbling sound of a lot of 1970s science fiction. If you're using a MIDI keyboard, the mod wheel adds up to 30 cents of vibrato in every figure.

Now press "audio rate". It's the vibrato setting pushed to an extreme: the LFO runs at 110 Hz and swings the pitch by ±900 cents. You can't hear a wobble that fast. Instead the spectrum underneath fills with new partials that weren't in the square at all, and the note turns metallic. Somewhere above about 20 Hz modulation stops being a movement you hear and becomes part of the timbre. That's the next step.

Step 6 · Frequency modulationFM: bells from two sines

Take two sine oscillators. One, the carrier, is the one you hear. The other, the modulator, pushes the carrier's phase back and forth at an audio rate: 

y(t) = sin(2π·fc·t + I · sin(2π·fm·t))

The result is a spectrum of lines at the carrier frequency plus and minus whole multiples of the modulator frequency, fc ± n·fm, called sidebands. The height of the nth pair is Jn(I), a Bessel function of the modulation index I, which is how hard the modulator pushes. At I = 0 there's only the carrier. As I grows, energy spreads out into more sidebands, roughly I + 1 on each side. The Bessel functions wiggle, so individual lines come and go: at I = 2.405 the carrier itself vanishes.

The figure shows the line spectrum on the left (worked out from the Bessel functions, with sidebands that fall below 0 Hz folded back up, in pink), the waveform under it, and J₀ to J₅ on the right with the index as a cursor you can drag. The horizontal axis of the spectrum is in multiples of the note you're playing.

The ratio between carrier and modulator decides which partials you get. Every line is at f·(c + n·m) for a note of frequency f, so with whole-number ratios they all land on harmonics of one frequency. At 1:1 that's every harmonic of the note, a bright saw-like spectrum. At 1:2 the lines fall on odd harmonics, like a square. At 1:1.4 they land on multiples of f/5, and with no common spacing the ear can lock onto, the result sounds like a bell or a gong.

The other half of FM is change over time. The modulator has its own envelope, so the index moves through each note, and while a note sounds the cursor on the Bessel plot follows it. On the bell, the index starts at 10 and decays over the note, so the spectrum starts dense and thins toward a pure tone as it rings, the way a struck bell does. On the brass, the index rises and falls with the volume, so loud means bright. The presets follow the instrument examples in John Chowning's 1973 paper: the bell at 1:1.4, brass at 1:1 with a peak index of 5, and a wood drum at 80:55 whose index of 25 collapses in a few tens of milliseconds. The clarinet (3:2) and bassoon (5:1) are woodwind-like settings in the same spirit.

Aside · Chowning, Stanford and Yamaha

John Chowning came across FM at Stanford in 1967, playing with vibrato that was faster and deeper than any singer's, and published the theory in 1973. Stanford licensed it to Yamaha, which took exclusive rights in 1975 and spent years making it cheap enough to sell. The result was the DX7 in 1983: six sine operators that could be wired in 32 arrangements, called algorithms. It became one of the best-selling synthesizers ever made, and its electric piano is all over the pop records of the 1980s. The patent became one of Stanford's highest earning licenses.

Strictly, the formula above is phase modulation, and so is what the DX7 computes. For sine modulators the spectrum is the same as true frequency modulation, and modulating the phase keeps the pitch steady when the index changes. This engine does the same.

Step 7 · Physical modellingA string in a delay line

Everything so far builds a sound out of its recipe. Physical modelling builds the thing that makes the sound and lets the physics produce the recipe. The simplest and most surprising example is the Karplus–Strong plucked string. Fill a short buffer with random numbers. Read it out in a loop, and every time a sample goes past, replace it with the average of itself and its neighbour. That's the whole algorithm, and it sounds like a plucked string.

It works because the buffer behaves like the string. A pulse on a real string travels to the bridge and back once per period, so a buffer of N samples played in a loop repeats every N samples, and the pitch is the sample rate divided by N: 436 samples for A2 at 48 kHz. The random fill is the pluck, a burst with every frequency in it. The loop only keeps frequencies that fit a whole number of times around it, so after one trip the noise has become a harmonic series. The averaging is a gentle lowpass filter, so on every trip the high harmonics lose a little more than the low ones, and the note mellows as it dies, the way a real string's does.

The figure draws the delay line as a ring, with the sample about to be read at the top. Pluck a low note and watch it start jagged and relax into a smooth, shrinking loop. The bars are the harmonics in the ring right now, with the time each one takes to fall 60 dB printed above it. The strip at the bottom is the output level in decibels, where an exponential decay is a straight line.

This is the heart of it in the engine, run once per sample for each string:

let y = (1 - v.wgt) * buf[r1] + v.wgt * buf[r2]; // wgt = ½: the plain average
if (ks.frac) {
  // all-pass filter: the fraction of a sample the loop is short by
  const ap = v.C * y + v.apx - v.C * v.apy;
  v.apx = y;
  v.apy = ap;
  y = ap;
}
y *= v.rho; // loop gain, set from the decay time
if (blend < 1 && rand() >= blend) y = -y; // the drum variant, below
buf[v.w] = y;

The plain algorithm has three problems, and David Jaffe and Julius Smith fixed all of them in a paper published alongside the original. The sliders are their fixes.

  • The decay depends wildly on pitch. The average loses very little at low frequencies, so in the plain loop the fundamental of A2 would take about 40 minutes to fall 60 dB, and A6's under a second. A loop gain ρ just below 1 sets the decay directly: the slider gives the fundamental's ring time, 0.3 to 30 seconds.
  • The highs die too fast for some instruments. The decay stretch S weights the average toward the newer sample, which filters less and lets the upper harmonics ring longer. "Bright steel" uses S = 6.
  • Whole samples aren't fine enough for tuning. The loop is N samples plus half a sample of delay from the average, and N has to be a whole number, so the loop can come out up to half a sample too long or too short. At A2 that's at most 2 cents. At C6, where the loop is only 46 samples, it can be 19. Turn the fractional delay off and the readout shows the error for the note you play. With it on, an all-pass filter adds the missing fraction of a sample.

Pick position works like it does on a guitar. Plucking a string at a point can't excite any harmonic that has a node there, so plucking at 1/k of the length removes every kth harmonic. The engine does that by combing the initial noise. The pluck slider runs the noise through a lowpass first, for a softer pick.

The blend b is Karplus and Strong's own drum variation. At b = 1 the loop is the string. Below 1, each sample's sign is flipped at random with probability 1 − b. At ½ the loop no longer holds a pitch and you get a drum-like thud whose decay the loop length sets. At 0 every sample flips on every trip, so the period doubles. The note drops an octave and keeps only odd harmonics, like blowing across a bottle.

Aside · Karplus, Strong, and waveguides

Kevin Karplus and Alex Strong published the algorithm in the Computer Music Journal in 1983. Part of its appeal was cost: it needs only additions and a shift, and Strong had it running in real time on an Intel 8080A. Julius Smith went on to generalise the idea into digital waveguide synthesis, where delay lines model waves travelling in both directions along strings and tubes, and Yamaha licensed that from Stanford for the VL1 in 1994.

Step 8 · Everything at onceThe whole instrument

The last figure puts it all together. The ten presets cover all three engines: subtractive (bass, brass, strings, lead, whistle), FM (electric piano, bell, FM bass) and the string (guitar, harp). The diagram is the signal path of whichever preset is loaded, and it's live. Each module draws what it's doing at that moment: the oscillator mix, the filter's response at the cutoff the engine is actually using, the envelopes with a dot per voice, the operators' live modulation index, the string's delay line. Cables light up with the signal going through them, and the spectrogram underneath is the output.

A few presets use things from earlier steps in combination. "Bass" is one voice with glide, a saw and a square an octave below it, and a resonant filter snapped open by a fast filter envelope. "Whistle" has no oscillators at all: white noise through the filter with the resonance just under self-oscillation, and with full keyboard tracking the cutoff follows the note, so the ringing peak plays the melody. The electric piano is FM with two pairs of operators: a 1:1 pair for the body of the note, and a pair with the modulator at 14 times the note frequency and a very short index envelope, which gives the metallic tine at the start of each note.

The sliders work across engines. Brightness moves the filter cutoff by up to three octaves either way on the subtractive presets, scales the FM modulators' index on the FM ones, and hardens or softens the pluck on the strings. Attack and release scale every envelope in the preset by between ⅛ and 8 times. The effects come after the voices: a chorus, a ping-pong delay, and the page's shared reverb.

The voices panel shows the engine's voice allocation. Each preset has a fixed number of voices, and when you play more notes than that, the engine takes over the quietest voice that's already been released, or, if every voice is still held, the quietest of those. The mono presets use a single voice with last-note priority: hold one key, press another, let go of the second, and the first comes back, gliding if the preset has glide.

How the figures share one engine

The engine is a single AudioWorklet processor, a plain JavaScript file the browser runs on its audio thread, and every figure makes its own instance. A figure sends it a patch (oscillators, filter, envelopes, LFO, operators, string settings) and note messages. Every five blocks of 128 samples, about 13 ms at 48 kHz, the engine posts back each voice's envelope stages and levels, the filter cutoff it's using, the LFO's phase, the newest string's delay line, and short scopes of the signal after the oscillators, after the filter, and at the output. That's what the figures draw.

The output passes through a peak limiter at −3 dBFS and a soft clipper before it leaves the engine, and then through the site's master chain, so stacking chords or sitting at full resonance can't produce painful levels. Where AudioWorklet isn't available, the same file runs on the main thread and streams its output in short buffers, with about 0.1 s more delay.

EpilogueWhat carries over

A few things I took away from building it:

  • There are two directions. Subtractive synthesis starts with too much and removes it. FM starts with almost nothing and multiplies it. Both end up controlling the same thing, which harmonics are present and how loud.
  • Sounds live in time. Most of what makes a patch recognisable is how its spectrum changes over the note: the filter envelope on the brass, the decaying index on the bell, the highs dying first on the string.
  • Digital needs band-limiting. A shape that's trivial in a circuit can sound broken in code, and the fix is about what happens in the one or two samples around a jump.
  • Models give you behaviour for free. Nobody told the delay line to make the high harmonics die first. It follows from what the loop does.

Things I'd try next: wavetable and granular oscillators, a full waveguide string with a resonant body, all six DX7 operators and their 32 algorithms, and saving and sharing patches as links.
