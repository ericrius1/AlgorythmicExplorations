Living Music · Part 6

Major and Minor

Why the two most common chords sound the way they do, how chords pull toward home, and the geometry of moving between them. The lattice above is a map of all 24 major and minor chords. Tap it to hear a walk across it.

Part five picked seven of the twelve notes and made one of them home. This part stacks them. Stand on any note of a scale, skip one, take the next, skip one, take the next, and you have three notes a third apart. That's a triad. In C major, standing on C gives C–E–G and standing on D gives D–F–A. Do it on all seven notes and you get three major triads, three minor ones and one diminished: the whole chord vocabulary of the key.

The lattice at the top of the page holds every major and minor triad. Each triangle is one chord, and two triangles that share an edge share two notes. The glowing path is a walk from triangle to triangle that changes one note at a time. By the end of this article you'll know why the map is laid out that way, why the warm triangles sound different from the cool ones, and why a walk across it never sounds like a jump.

Step 1 · Stacked thirdsThree notes from one ladder

Here is the microscope from part three, pointed at a chord. Each row is one note of the chord drawn as its first eight harmonics on a log-frequency axis. Bar height is loudness: harmonic k is 1/k as loud as the first, which is also the tone the figure plays. When a harmonic of one note lands near a harmonic of another, the collision lane along the top marks the spot. White means the two coincide, amber means they're a few hertz apart and beat slowly, and red means they're close enough to grind.

In C major, the third harmonic of C (785 Hz) lands on the second harmonic of G (784 Hz), and the fifth harmonic of C (1,308 Hz) lands near the fourth harmonic of E (1,319 Hz). They line up because the three notes are, very nearly, harmonics 4, 5 and 6 of a single lower note: C4, E4 and G4 sit almost on the 4th, 5th and 6th harmonics of C2. The bottom row shows that note, the chord's implied root, with its own harmonics drawn as a faint comb behind everything. Most of the chord's partials land on the comb's teeth. A major triad is close to a slice of one harmonic series, the pattern part one showed the ear fusing into a single pitch.

The "very nearly" comes from part four. Equal temperament makes the major third 400 cents wide instead of the 386 of a pure 5:4, so E's harmonics miss C's by a little, and the pair at 1,308 and 1,319 Hz beats about ten times a second. The fifth is only 2 cents narrow, so its pair beats less than once a second. Switch the figure to just tuning and every amber mark turns white.

Now pick minor. C–E♭–G uses the same two thirds stacked the other way up, a minor third under a major one. The fifth still lines up, but the three notes only fit a single harmonic series at 10:12:15, and the implied root drops to 26 Hz: an A♭ more than three octaves down, a note that isn't in the chord. The ear gets no strong single fundamental to hang the chord on, and its root is less clear. Helmholtz made a version of this argument in 1863: some of the faint extra tones a minor triad produces in the ear belong to A♭ rather than to C minor, which he blamed for the "veiled" sound of minor chords.

Roughness, the measure part three built from Plomp and Levelt's curve, can't separate the two. The band at the top of the figure computes it for five triads on the same root. On C4: major 0.29, minor 0.31, augmented 0.29, diminished 0.40, sus4 0.32. Diminished is rough because of its tritone. Augmented, which most listeners hear as the least stable of the four triads, comes out as smooth as major. Whatever makes major and minor sound different, it isn't mostly roughness.

Listening experiments point the same way. Josh McDermott, Andriana Lehr and Andrew Oxenham measured individual listeners' liking for consonant chords, for harmonic over inharmonic tones, and for steady over beating tones. The people who most preferred consonant chords were the ones who most preferred harmonic spectra. How much they disliked beating didn't predict it.

Aside · Rameau and the sounding body

Jean-Philippe Rameau's Treatise on Harmony (1722) put the triad and its root at the centre of music theory. In Génération harmonique (1737) he grounded the major triad in the corps sonore, the sounding body: any vibrating string sounds its own harmonics, and among them are a twelfth and a major seventeenth above the fundamental, a major triad spread over three octaves. Minor wouldn't come out of the overtones the same way. He tried several explanations over the following decades and never settled on one.

Why a chord keeps its root when you rearrange it

Play C–E–G, then move the C up an octave to get E–G–C. The lowest note changed, but it still sounds like C major. The microscope shows why. E4, G4 and C5 are harmonics 5, 6 and 8 of C2, and G3, C4 and E4 are harmonics 3, 4 and 5 of C2. Moving a note by an octave doubles or halves its frequency, which moves it to another tooth of the same comb, so the implied root doesn't move. Musicians call these rearrangements inversions and treat them as the same chord.

Step 2 · Chord spaceWhy these two

Counting transpositions as the same chord, there are 19 types of three-note chord. Here they all are. Any three notes cut the octave into three gaps that add up to 12 semitones: C–E–G is 4 up to E, 3 up to G, and 5 more back up to C. The triangle plots every such split. Its corners are one note tripled, its edges are two-note chords (one gap is zero), and its centre is 4 + 4 + 4, the augmented triad, the only chord that splits the octave evenly. Every other type appears three times, once for each note you could start counting from.

Moving one note up a semitone widens the gap below it and narrows the gap above it, so neighbouring dots are one semitone apart. When you tap a dot, the figure plays that chord type at whichever transposition is the smallest move from the chord you just heard. The white lines point to the current chord's neighbours, and each one sounds like a single note sliding.

Major and minor are the six dots right around the centre, alternating. After the augmented triad itself, they're the most even three-note chords there are. Dmitri Tymoczko made a lot of this in a 2006 paper in Science: chords that nearly divide the octave evenly can reach many other chords by small moves, which is part of why so much music is built from them. The colour is roughness, averaged over the three ways of voicing each chord, and by that measure major and minor tie for the smoothest type (0.28), with the augmented triad close behind (0.29). The rough dots are the clusters near the corners.

Press augmented → major or minor. Lower any note of an augmented triad by a semitone and you get a major triad. Raise any note and you get a minor one. Norman Cook has argued that this asymmetry matters more than roughness for how the two sound: the augmented triad is tense because its two intervals are equal, and the two ways out of it go in opposite directions.

The dashed lines are mirrors. A chord on a mirror line has two equal gaps, so turning it upside down gives the same chord back. Major and minor sit on opposite sides: flip C–E–G upside down and the gaps 4 then 3 become 3 then 4, a minor triad. Hugo Riemann built a whole theory of harmony, "harmonic dualism", around that mirror.

Now press circle the augmented triad. Walking around the six dots one semitone at a time plays C, C minor, A♭, A♭ minor, E, E minor and back to C. That sequence comes back in step four.

Step 3 · FunctionTension and release

On its own, a chord is a sound. In a key, chords also have jobs. Build a triad on each note of C major and you get I (C), ii (Dm), iii (Em), IV (F), V (G), vi (Am) and vii° (B°). Theory books group them by what they tend to do next. Tonic chords (I, plus vi and iii, which share two notes with it) feel like home. Predominant chords (IV and ii) lean away. Dominant chords (V and vii°) push back toward home. The classic order is tonic, predominant, dominant, tonic, and a great deal of music runs on that loop.

The pull of V comes from two notes. Add a seventh to G major and you get G7: G–B–D–F. B and F are a tritone apart, and each sits a semitone from a note of C major: B just under C, F just over E. When G7 goes to C, B steps up to C and F steps down to E while G stays put. Two half steps and a held note.

In the figure each voice is a thread through pitch. The bass takes the roots. The three upper voices use a minimum-motion search: at each change, every voice moves to the nearest note of the new chord, with every chord tone covered. (For V7 the upper voices take B, F and G and leave out D, the usual way to fit a seventh chord into four parts.) Level threads are notes the two chords share. The numbers on the bends are how far a voice moved, in semitones. Play I–IV–V7–I and watch the last change: +1, −1, and a held G.

The two bars under each chord are computed. The red one is Fred Lerdahl's distance from the tonic chord, from Tonal Pitch Space (2001). It counts the steps around the circle of fifths between the two roots, plus every note the new chord adds at the root, fifth and triad levels of the key. From I it gives 5 for IV and V, 7 for vi and iii, and 8 for ii and vii°. Lerdahl and Carol Krumhansl later tested a tension model built on this distance against listeners' tension ratings, and it followed them closely. The blue bar is roughness, for the four notes as voiced. V7 is high on both: as far from home as V, and rougher than any other chord in the loop.

Try deceptive. It's the same I–IV–V7, but V7 goes to vi instead of I. B still rises to C and F still falls to E, so the upper voices do what they promised, but the bass steps up from G to A instead of going to C. The cadence lands somewhere other than home, and the music has a reason to keep going.

I–V–vi–IV never makes the V–I move at all: V goes to vi, and IV returns to I without passing through V. So it can repeat for a whole song without sounding finished. It's the loop under "Let It Be" and "No Woman, No Cry", and the Axis of Awesome's comedy medley "4 Chords" strings together dozens more songs that use it. Pachelbel is the progression from his Canon, I–V–vi–iii–IV–I–IV–V, which falls a fourth and rises a step, over and over.

The minimum-motion search, as coded

For three voices and a three-note chord there are 3³ = 27 ways to assign voices to notes. The search throws out any assignment that leaves a chord tone missing, puts each voice on the nearest octave of its note inside F3–D5, and scores the result: total semitones moved, plus 6 for each pair of voices that land on the same note and 3 for each pair that cross. Lowest score wins. That's all of it, and it reproduces a good share of the voice-leading rules in an eighteenth-century harmony textbook: hold common tones, prefer steps to leaps, keep voices apart.

Step 4 · Voice leading as geometryThe Tonnetz

One map shows all of this at once. Put the twelve notes on a triangular grid so that moving right goes up a perfect fifth, moving up and right goes up a major third, and moving down and right goes up a minor third. Every small triangle is then a major or minor triad: the ones pointing up are major (warm), the ones pointing down are minor (cool). Leonhard Euler drew an early version of this grid in 1739, and nineteenth-century theorists, Hugo Riemann among them, built on it. It's called the Tonnetz, German for "tone network".

Triangles that share an edge share two notes, so stepping across an edge changes one note. A triangle has three edges, which gives three moves:

  • P (parallel) swaps C major for C minor. The third moves a semitone.
  • L (leading-tone exchange) swaps C major for E minor. C moves down a semitone to B.
  • R (relative) swaps C major for A minor. G moves up a whole tone to A.

These are the neo-Riemannian transformations, worked out by David Lewin, Brian Hyer and Richard Cohn in the 1980s and 90s. Press P, L or R to step, or tap any triangle to jump to it. The spark shows the note that moved.

The cycle buttons repeat a pair of moves. P and L alternated give C, C minor, A♭, A♭ minor, E, E minor and back to C, the circle around the augmented triad from step two, now laid out on the map. Cohn called these hexatonic cycles, because the six chords use only six notes between them. P and R alternated give an eight-chord octatonic cycle (C, Cm, E♭, E♭m, F♯, F♯m, A, Am), and L and R alternated walk through all 24 major and minor triads before coming home.

Composers used these moves long before anyone named them. The second movement of Beethoven's Ninth has a long passage that runs down an L–R chain, and later nineteenth-century composers used P and L to slide between keys that share almost no notes. Cohn connects the chord directly across a hexatonic cycle (C major against A♭ minor) with the uncanny in nineteenth-century music, and the same moves turn up all over film scores, which Frank Lehman catalogues in Hollywood Harmony (2018).

The walk at the top of the page wanders this map with P, L and R moves, sometimes running a cycle. It ends up in distant keys without ever sounding like a jump, because each step moves one voice by one or two semitones and holds the other two.

Aside · The map is a doughnut

Walk twelve steps right on the Tonnetz and you've gone up twelve fifths, which in equal temperament lands back on the note you started from. Walk three steps up and right and three major thirds bring you home too. So the plane repeats in both directions, and the figure is really the surface of a torus unrolled flat. In just intonation it wouldn't close: twelve pure fifths overshoot by the Pythagorean comma from part four, and the map would go on forever.

EpilogueWhat carries over

  • A major triad is almost one harmonic series. Its notes are harmonics 4, 5 and 6 of the root two octaves down. Minor only fits at 10:12:15, on a root that isn't in the chord.
  • Roughness doesn't explain major and minor. It ties them, and it rates the augmented triad about as smooth. Harmonicity and three-note structure do better.
  • In a key, chords have jobs. V7 pulls toward I because two of its notes are a semitone from I's notes, a tritone apart.
  • Voice leading is distance. Major and minor sit next to each other in chord space, and P, L and R are the smallest moves between them.

Things I'd try next: add a fourth note, which turns the triangle into a tetrahedron of seventh chords with the diminished seventh at its centre; a Tonnetz in just intonation that never wraps around; and a progression explorer that suggests the next chord from a corpus of real songs instead of the function cycle.
