Living Music · Part 6

Major and Minor

How three notes lock together into a chord, why moving one note by a single semitone flips major into minor, and the geometry that connects all twenty-four triads. Tap the lattice above to hear a walk across it.

Play C, E, and G together, then drop the middle note one semitone to E♭. Both chords have the exact same ingredients: one major third, one minor third, and an outer perfect fifth. Yet reversing the order of those two thirds completely changes the character of the sound. Why should such a tiny rearrangement feel so different, and how do chords like these chain together to create the sensation of leaving home and coming back?

In part five we picked seven notes out of twelve and turned one of them into a tonal center. Now we stack them. Start on any degree of a scale, skip a note, take the next, skip another, and take the next: you get three notes stacked in thirds, a triad. Starting on C in C major gives C–E–G; starting on D gives D–F–A. Run through all seven degrees and the scale hands you three major triads, three minor ones, and one diminished triad, covering the basic harmonic vocabulary of the key. The triangular grid at the top of the page lays out all twenty-four major and minor triads so that any two triangles sharing an edge also share two notes. Walking across an edge changes just one pitch at a time, which is why the glowing path above can drift into remote keys without ever feeling abrupt.

Step 1 · Stacked thirdsThree notes from one ladder

To see what actually happens inside a triad, we can aim the harmonic microscope from part three at three notes at once. Each row below plots one note's first eight harmonics on a logarithmic frequency axis, with bar height proportional to loudness (harmonic k has amplitude 1/k, matching the synthesized tone you hear when you strike the chord). Whenever a partial from one note lands close to a partial from another, the collision lane along the top marks the spot: white dots for an exact match, amber for slow beating a few hertz apart, and red where partials sit close enough to grind against each other.

Look at C major first. The third harmonic of C4 (785 Hz) lands right next to the second harmonic of G4 (784 Hz), and the fifth harmonic of C4 (1,308 Hz) lands near the fourth harmonic of E4 (1,319 Hz). That alignment happens because C4, E4, and G4 sit very close to harmonics 4, 5, and 6 of a single lower pitch, C2 at 65.4 Hz. The bottom row marks that implied root and casts its own harmonic series upward as a faint comb behind the whole graph. Most of the triad's partials snap onto the teeth of that comb, so a major chord behaves a lot like a slice carved out of one vibrating string's overtone series, the same acoustic cue part one showed the ear fusing into a single pitch.

The tiny misses come from part four's compromise. Equal temperament widens the major third from a pure 5:4 (386 cents) to 400 cents, so E's fourth harmonic overshoots C's fifth by roughly 10 Hz and produces a steady shimmer. The fifth is only 2 cents narrow, so its collision beats less than once a second. Click tuning: equal to toggle into just intonation and every amber collision snaps cleanly to white.

Switch the quality to minor and watch the implied root plummet. C–E♭–G puts the minor third on the bottom and the major third on top. The outer fifth still aligns cleanly, but the three frequencies only share a common overtone ladder way up at 10:12:15, pushing their implied fundamental down to 26.2 Hz, an A♭ more than three octaves below C4 that doesn't even appear in the chord. Without a strong low fundamental anchoring all three notes, the root feels less pinned down. Hermann von Helmholtz noticed a related effect back in 1863: the faint combination tones generated inside the inner ear by a minor triad line up with A♭ rather than C minor, which he credited for the darker, slightly "veiled" quality of minor harmony.

Sensory roughness alone, computed from Plomp and Levelt's curve in part three, barely distinguishes the two. The strip across the top of the figure runs that calculation across five triads built on C4: major scores 0.29, minor 0.31, augmented 0.29, sus4 0.32, and diminished jumps to 0.40 because of its tritone. Notice that augmented, which most people hear as tense and unstable, comes out just as smooth as major. Whatever drives the felt contrast between major, minor, and augmented, roughness is only a small piece of it.

Perceptual studies point toward harmonicity instead. In 2010, Josh McDermott, Andriana Lehr, and Andrew Oxenham tested how strongly individual listeners preferred consonant chords, harmonic over inharmonic spectra, and steady tones over beating ones. Preference for consonant chords tracked preference for clean harmonic spectra closely, whereas sensitivity to beating barely predicted it at all.

Aside · Rameau and the sounding body

Jean-Philippe Rameau's Treatise on Harmony (1722) put the triad and its root at the center of Western music theory. Fifteen years later, in Génération harmonique (1737), he anchored the major triad in the corps sonore ("sounding body"): any plucked string radiates harmonics that include a twelfth and a major seventeenth above the fundamental, forming a major triad stretched over three octaves. The minor triad refused to fall out of the overtone series the same way, and Rameau spent decades trying out different derivations for it without ever fully convincing himself.

Why a chord keeps its root when you rearrange it

Play C4–E4–G4, then move the C up an octave to get E4–G4–C5. Even though E is now on the bottom, the chord still reads unmistakably as C major. The harmonic comb explains why: E4, G4, and C5 sit on harmonics 5, 6, and 8 of C2, and G3, C4, and E4 sit on harmonics 3, 4, and 5 of that same C2. Shifting a note by an octave doubles or halves its frequency, moving it to another tooth on the same comb while leaving the implied root right where it was. Musicians call these octave rearrangements inversions and treat them as voicings of the same underlying chord.

Step 2 · Chord spaceWhy these two

If we treat transpositions as equivalent, every possible three-note chord in twelve-tone tuning boils down to just 19 chord types. Any three pitch classes slice the 12-semitone octave into three gaps that add up to 12: C–E–G climbs 4 semitones to E, 3 to G, and 5 more back up to C. The triangle below plots every way to partition 12 into three numbers. The three corners represent triple unisons (12 + 0 + 0), the edges hold two-note chords where one gap collapses to zero, and dead center sits 4 + 4 + 4, the augmented triad, the sole chord that divides the octave into three equal pieces. Every other chord type appears three times around the center, once for each cyclic ordering of its gaps.

Nudging one voice up by a semitone stretches the gap beneath it by 1 and shrinks the gap above it by 1, which moves you directly to an adjacent dot on the grid. Whenever you tap a dot below, the figure picks whichever transposition of that chord type requires the smallest total finger movement from whatever you just played. Those white spokes connect the current chord to its one-semitone neighbors, letting you hear distance on the map as a single note sliding.

Right around the center, alternating like petals around the augmented triad, sit the six dots for major and minor. After the augmented triad itself, they are the most evenly spaced three-note chords possible in twelve-tone tuning. Music theorist Dmitri Tymoczko analyzed this geometry in a 2006 Science paper: chords that come close to dividing the octave evenly sit at the crossroads of chord space, able to reach a huge number of other chords through tiny voice movements. Color here tracks roughness averaged across each chord's three close-position voicings. Major and minor tie for the smoothest interior spot (0.28), augmented sits right behind them (0.29), and roughness flares up toward the tight semitone clusters tucked into the corners.

Try the augmented → major or minor button to hear the symmetry break in both directions. Lower any note of an augmented triad by a semitone and it relaxes into a major triad; raise any note by a semitone and it turns into a minor triad. Psychologist Norman Cook argued that this three-tone tension matters more than pairwise roughness: two stacked equal intervals leave the ear suspended, and resolving upward versus downward tilts the chord into opposite emotional territory.

The three faint dashed lines bisecting the triangle are axes of mirror symmetry. Any chord lying directly on a dashed line has two equal gaps (like sus4's 5 + 2 + 5, diminished's 3 + 3 + 6, or augmented's 4 + 4 + 4), so flipping its intervals upside down hands back the same chord. Major and minor sit as mirror images across those lines: invert C–E–G (gaps 4 then 3) and you get gaps 3 then 4, a minor triad. Nineteenth-century theorist Hugo Riemann built an entire system called "harmonic dualism" out of that reflection.

Pressing circle the augmented triad walks step by step around those six central dots, moving one voice by a single semitone each time: C, C minor, A♭, A♭ minor, E, E minor, and right back to C. Hold onto that six-chord loop, because it reappears from a completely different angle in step four.

Step 3 · FunctionTension and release

Inside a key, chords take on distinct roles beyond their individual color. Building triads on each step of C major gives I (C), ii (Dm), iii (Em), IV (F), V (G), vi (Am), and vii° (B°). Harmony textbooks group these seven chords into three functional families based on where they tend to lead. Tonic chords (I, along with vi and iii, which each share two notes with I) sound settled and home-like. Predominant chords (IV and ii) lean away from home and build momentum. Dominant chords (V and vii°) lean hard back toward resolution. The circuit tonic → predominant → dominant → tonic drives an enormous fraction of Western music.

Most of the dominant's gravitational pull comes down to two notes. Stack one more third onto G major to make G7 (G–B–D–F) and you introduce a tritone between B and F. Each of those two notes sits just one semitone away from a stable note of C major: B rubs right under C, while F leans right over E. When G7 resolves to C, B rises a half step to C, F drops a half step to E, and G holds steady across both chords.

In the progression explorer below, each of the four voices is drawn as a glowing ribbon moving through pitch. The amber bass takes the chord roots, while the three upper voices are chosen automatically by a minimum-motion search that sends every voice to the nearest tone of the next chord while covering the full triad. (On V7, the upper three voices take B, F, and G and omit D, the classic four-part shortcut that keeps the crucial tritone intact.) Flat lines show common tones held between chords, and the signed numbers mark semitone steps. Tap I–IV–V7–I under LOOP and watch the final change: +1, −1, and a flat line on G.

Beneath each chord chip sit two computed bars. The red bar (δ from I) is Fred Lerdahl's chord distance from Tonal Pitch Space (2001): it adds the number of steps around the key's circle of fifths between the two roots to the count of newly introduced pitch classes across the root, fifth, and triad levels. Relative to I, that gives 5 for IV and V, 7 for iii and vi, and 8 for ii and vii°. When Lerdahl and Carol Krumhansl tested a tension model built on this distance against real listeners' continuous tension ratings, the curves tracked closely. The blue bar below it measures roughness for the four notes as voiced. V7 spikes on both metrics at once, combining high harmonic distance with the sharpest roughness in the key.

Click deceptive to hear how composers play with that expectation. It runs the same I–IV–V7 setup, then sends V7 to vi instead of I. In the upper voices, B still climbs to C and F still falls to E, fulfilling half the promise, while the bass steps up from G to A instead of landing on C. The phrase dodges finality and buys itself room to keep moving.

Looping I–V–vi–IV skips the V–I cadence entirely: V moves to vi, and IV glides back to I without passing through a dominant. Because it never slams the door shut, a song can ride those four bars indefinitely, as "Let It Be", "No Woman, No Cry", and dozens of pop hits in the Axis of Awesome's "4 Chords" medley all do. And Pachelbel runs the Canon sequence (I–V–vi–iii–IV–I–IV–V), cycling through a descending-fourth, ascending-step pattern that feels endlessly self-propelling.

The minimum-motion search, as coded

With three upper voices and a three-note chord, there are 3³ = 27 possible voice-to-note assignments. The solver discards any assignment that drops a chord tone, snaps each voice to the closest octave of its target pitch class inside F3–D5, and computes a simple cost: total semitones moved across all three voices, plus a penalty of 6 for any unison and 3 for any pair of voices that cross. Picking the lowest score reproduces most of the voice-leading habits taught in eighteenth-century harmony courses: hold shared notes, favor small steps over leaps, and keep voices cleanly separated.

Step 4 · Voice leading as geometryThe Tonnetz

All of these chord relationships snap together on a single two-dimensional map. Arrange the twelve pitch classes on a triangular lattice so that moving right climbs a perfect fifth, moving up-right climbs a major third, and moving down-right climbs a minor third. Every unit triangle on that grid automatically becomes a triad: upward-pointing triangles (warm amber) are major, and downward-pointing triangles (cool blue) are minor. Leonhard Euler sketched an early version of this grid in 1739, and nineteenth-century German theorists, Hugo Riemann foremost among them, turned it into the Tonnetz, literally "tone network".

Because adjacent triangles share an edge, flipping across any edge keeps two notes fixed and moves only the third corner. Every triad has three edges, giving three elementary moves:

  • P (parallel) flips across the root–fifth edge, swapping C major for C minor by nudging the third down a semitone.
  • L (leading-tone exchange) flips across the third–fifth edge, swapping C major for E minor by lowering C a semitone to B.
  • R (relative) flips across the root–third edge, swapping C major for A minor by raising G a whole tone to A.

David Lewin, Brian Hyer, and Richard Cohn formalized this toolkit as neo-Riemannian theory in the 1980s and 1990s. Use the P, L, and R buttons below to step edge by edge, or tap any triangle directly to jump there; the white spark traces the single voice that moves.

Alternating any two of these flips generates closed orbits across the lattice. Press P–L cycle and the lattice steps through C, Cm, A♭, A♭m, E, Em, and back to C: the exact six-chord ring around the augmented triad from step two, now unfolded onto the grid. Cohn named these hexatonic cycles because all six triads draw from a pool of just six pitch classes. P–R cycle traces an eight-chord octatonic loop (C, Cm, E♭, E♭m, F♯, F♯m, A, Am), and L–R chain winds through all twenty-four major and minor triads before returning to where it started.

Composers were mining these paths long before theorists gave them letters. The scherzo of Beethoven's Ninth Symphony drops straight down a long L–R chain, and late Romantic writers leaned heavily on P and L to glide between distant keys that share almost no diatonic scale tones. Cohn tied the opposite poles of a hexatonic cycle (like C major paired directly with A♭ minor) to moments of eerie strangeness in nineteenth-century scores, and Frank Lehman catalogs hundreds of the same moves in modern movie soundtracks in Hollywood Harmony (2018).

The hero lattice at the top of the page runs a slow walk built entirely out of P, L, and R steps, slipping occasionally into a hexatonic loop or an L–R run. Even when it wanders far from its starting key, every transition stays silky because two notes always anchor the ear while the third shifts by a semitone or whole step.

Aside · The map is a doughnut

Walk twelve steps east on the Tonnetz and you have climbed twelve perfect fifths, which in equal temperament lands right back on your starting pitch class. Walk three steps northeast and three major thirds close the octave as well. Because the lattice repeats periodically along both axes, this flat drawing is really the surface of a torus unrolled onto a plane. In pure just intonation it would never close: twelve pure fifths overshoot seven octaves by part four's Pythagorean comma, stretching the grid out into an infinite plane.

EpiloguePutting the pieces together

Stepping back from the four figures, the contrast between major and minor turns out to have roots in both physics and geometry. Under the microscope, a major triad sits snugly on harmonics 4, 5, and 6 of an implied root two octaves down, whereas minor only matches a single harmonic ladder up at 10:12:15 on a phantom pitch far below the chord. Sensory roughness alone barely splits them (and even puts the tense augmented triad in the same smooth bracket), while three-note chord space shows major and minor hugging the center as the most evenly spaced non-augmented triads in twelve-tone tuning. Inside a key, chords take on jobs driven by voice leading, especially V7's tritone resolving by opposite half steps onto I, and on the Tonnetz all of that smooth voice leading turns into literal proximity across shared triangle edges.

There are plenty of natural extensions from here: adding a fourth note turns chord space into a three-dimensional tetrahedron of seventh chords with the diminished seventh at its center; untempering the Tonnetz into just intonation opens up an infinite unwrapping lattice; and swapping the textbook function wheel for transition probabilities learned from a corpus of real songs would turn the loop explorer into a surprisingly opinionated co-writer.
