Living Music · Part 3

Consonance and Roughness

Two tones drawing a harmonograph across a landscape of dissonance. Drag along the top curve to hear intervals lock in or grind apart.

Scroll down to read ↓

Anyone who has ever tuned a guitar by ear knows a strange acoustic transition. Pluck two strings tuned a hair apart and the combined tone breathes in and out in slow, countable swells. Turn the tuning peg a little farther and those swells speed up until they blur into a harsh, gritty buzz. Keep turning, and past a certain gap the grit clears away into two clean, separate notes.

Up at the top of the page, a virtual harmonograph turns that exact acoustic story into a drawing. One tone at 220 Hz drives the glowing pen sideways while a second tone drives it vertically, fading out like a dying pendulum. Across the top runs a curve measuring how rough the pair sounds at every interval from unison to the octave. Tap the canvas (or hit sound) and drag along that curve; letting go snaps onto any nearby named interval, and left alone it wanders through twelve intervals on its own. Wherever the curve plunges into a deep valley, the tangled weave suddenly freezes into a tidy, repeating knot, and the sound snaps smooth. In Part 1 a single note came apart into a stack of sine harmonics. Now two notes play at once, and it turns out that those hidden harmonics are what carve out nearly every valley in Western harmony.

Step 1 · Close frequenciesBeats and roughness

Begin with the barest possible case: two pure sine waves separated by just a few hertz. Because one cycles slightly faster than the other, their peaks drift steadily in and out of alignment. When peaks line up, they reinforce each other and the tone swells; half a beat later, peak meets trough, they cancel, and the tone dips. That pulsing happens |f₂ − f₁| times every second, once for every full cycle the higher sine gains on the lower one: 

sin(2π·220·t) + sin(2π·223·t) = 2·cos(2π·1.5·t)·sin(2π·221.5·t)
// a 221.5 Hz tone whose loudness swells 3 times a second

The figure opens on that exact pair, A3 (220 Hz) alongside 223 Hz. Click play both. On the left, a 20 ms window shows the blue and orange sines sliding past one another as their bright white sum grows and collapses. On the right, a one-second envelope traces the three distinct swells. Now drag the second tone, above the first slider slowly across its range from 0.1 to 160 Hz.

Below about 10 Hz you hear gentle, countable throbs, the exact cue a guitarist listens for when matching two strings. Push past 10 Hz and your ear loses track of individual pulses; the wobble fuses into a coarse, rasping sensation called roughness. Near A3, roughness peaks around 22 Hz apart, then gradually relaxes until, past roughly 60 Hz, the grit vanishes and two independent, smooth tones emerge.

The curve drawn across the bottom plots that trajectory directly from listening experiments Reinier Plomp and Willem Levelt published in 1965. Its shape is dictated by the mechanics of your inner ear. Inside the cochlea, incoming sound waves separate by frequency along the basilar membrane, and each patch of sensory hair cells responds to a local neighborhood of frequencies known as a critical band. When two tones land inside the same critical band, they physically shake the same patch of membrane at once, and the rapid interference between them registers as roughness. Plomp and Levelt found that roughness peaks when two tones sit roughly a quarter of a critical band apart.

Click through the register buttons (A2 through A5) to see how pitch register shifts the picture. Critical bands stay relatively wide down in the bass and grow narrower, as a fraction of pitch, higher up. At A5 (880 Hz), the peak roughness gap of 34 Hz spans only about two-thirds of a semitone. Down at A2 (110 Hz), a 20 Hz peak spans nearly a minor third, and two sines keep grinding against each other until they are almost a fifth apart. That single quirk of cochlear anatomy explains why a tight chord played low on the piano turns muddy while the exact same notes two octaves up sound crystalline, and why arrangers naturally space bass voices wide.

Aside · Tuning by counting

Professional piano tuners treat beating as a stopwatch. When laying down a temperament across the middle of a piano, they rely on precise beat counts between upper harmonics of two strings, adjusting the tuning pin until the pulse rate hits a known target for each interval. On a modern equal-tempered piano, a properly tuned middle-register fifth still beats slowly, just shy of once per second.

Step 2 · Simple ratiosPatterns that repeat

Once two pure sine waves sit more than a critical band apart, they stop beating against each other completely. Yet in music, a fifth sounds settled while a tritone feels tense. Before bringing harmonics back in, look at what happens to the geometry of the combined waveform itself. Below, tone A (220 Hz) drives a dot horizontally and tone B drives it vertically over the last 36 ms of motion, tracing a Lissajous figure beside their time-domain sum.

Click through the ratio presets beneath the slider. Whenever the two frequencies form a ratio of small integers, the glowing trace closes neatly on itself and holds still. At 3:2 (a perfect fifth, 220 and 330 Hz), the upper sine completes three cycles in the exact time the lower one finishes two, so the combined waveform repeats every 9.09 ms (110 times per second, exactly an octave below the lower tone). Ratios with larger integers take far longer to realign: 45:32 (one version of the tritone) technically repeats too, yet only once every 145 ms (around 7 times per second), far too sluggish for the ear to hear as a shared pitch, leaving the knot perpetually dense and restless.

Nudge the frequency ratio slider just slightly off 1.5000 and watch the 3:2 knot begin to tumble slowly in space. Its rotation speed matches the exact rate at which the two waves slip out of alignment: 

// near p:q, harmonic q of the upper tone and harmonic p of the lower one almost meet
const slip = Math.abs(near.q * f2 - near.p * F1);

That slip rate is a beat frequency in disguise: specifically, the beat between the third harmonic of 220 Hz (660 Hz) and the second harmonic of the upper tone, which coincide perfectly at an exact 3:2 fifth. Pure sine waves carry zero overtones, so listening here you will barely hear a difference between an exact fifth and one nudged slightly off center. Real instruments, however, are packed with those harmonics, and the moment they come back in, that slip becomes unmistakably audible.

Aside · Mirrors on tuning forks

French physicist Jules Lissajous invented these figures optically in 1857. He glued a tiny mirror onto one tine of each of two tuning forks mounted at right angles, then bounced a narrow beam of light off the first mirror, onto the second, and across to a projection screen. Because any tiny mistuning made the luminous knot rotate slowly at the exact slip rate, Lissajous figures served for decades as a precision laboratory tool for calibrating frequency standards.

Step 3 · Harmonics collideWhere the harmonics land

Real musical tones (strings, reeds, brass, voices) sound a full harmonic stack at 1, 2, 3, and higher integer multiples of their fundamental. Play two such notes simultaneously and you have dozens of sine-wave pairs interacting at once across the cochlea. Every harmonic from the first note lands near some harmonic from the second, and each pair either aligns cleanly, sits far enough apart to stay smooth, or falls inside a critical band and grates according to the Plomp–Levelt curve from Step 1.

Below, both harmonic stacks sit face to face on a logarithmic frequency axis: tone A in blue pointing upward, tone B in orange pointing downward, starting on a fifth. Pairs that line up within a few cents flare crisp white (at 3:2, B's 2nd partial locks right onto A's 3rd, and B's 4th locks onto A's 6th). Pairs close enough to beat pulse angry red. The graph underneath totals up the Plomp–Levelt roughness of every cross-tone partial pair at every interval from unison to just past the octave. Tap play and drag horizontally across the curve to sweep tone B.

Every valley label underneath the curve is detected automatically from the curve's own local minima and matched to the nearest simple ratio. With the default seven harmonics per tone, the dips fall squarely on 6:5, 5:4, 4:3, 7:5, 3:2, 5:3, 7:4, and 2:1, with the octave and fifth plunging deepest of all. Out of one empirical cochlear roughness curve and straightforward addition falls nearly the entire traditional hierarchy of musical consonance. Notice the two septimal valleys containing a 7 (7:5 and 7:4): they appear because harmonic 7 is present in the stack. Standard Western notation skips them, though barbershop quartets famously lean their dominant sevenths down toward the silky 7:4 ratio because no piano key ties them down.

Slide harmonics per tone down to 1 (pure sine) to watch the valleys evaporate: with pure sines, only a single broad roughness hump near unison remains. Step up to 2 and the octave valley snaps into view; step to 3 and the perfect fifth carves itself out. Each additional harmonic adds new coincidences and digs fresh valleys. Then flip between low (C3), middle (A3), and high (A4): down at C3, the minor and major third valleys flatten to roughly half their depth at A4, echoing the low-register muddiness from Step 1. 

// Plomp–Levelt roughness of two partials, in Sethares' parameterization
export function roughness(f1: number, a1: number, f2: number, a2: number): number {
  const lo = Math.min(f1, f2);
  const s = 0.24 / (0.0207 * lo + 18.96);
  const x = s * Math.abs(f2 - f1);
  return Math.min(a1, a2) * (Math.exp(-3.51 * x) - Math.exp(-5.75 * x));
}
Deep dive: the model behind the curve

Electrical engineer and music theorist William Sethares fit Plomp and Levelt's listening data with the difference of two exponentials above. Roughness starts at zero for unison, rises sharply to a peak at x ≈ 0.22 (which evaluates to 22 Hz at 220 Hz), and tails off smoothly as the separation widens. The factor s rescales the frequency gap by the local critical bandwidth at the lower partial, and each pair is weighted by the quieter of its two amplitudes. Here, partial n has amplitude 0.88n−1, and the total curve sums roughness over every cross-tone pair (within-tone pairs remain constant regardless of interval, so they add only a flat offset).

This curve captures sensory roughness alone. It knows nothing about harmonic fusion at the octave, melodic context, tension and resolution across chord progressions, or a listener's cultural habits. All of those shape real musical consonance too. Even so, it is striking how much of the classical ranking of intervals drops straight out of cochlear interference.

Step 4 · Change the sound, change the scaleTimbre moves the valleys

If consonance valleys form wherever two notes' partials line up, a bold prediction follows: change the partial spacing inside the timbre itself, and the consonant intervals ought to move right along with them. William Sethares tested this systematically in the 1990s. The curve below runs the exact same roughness calculation, except the buttons now swap both tones to inharmonic timbres whose overtones abandon the integer series.

The figure starts on stretched, one of Sethares' favorite demonstrations. Every partial is stretched outward as though an octave had a ratio of 2.1 instead of 2, putting partial n at 2.1log₂n. Glance at the curve: the deepest valley has migrated from 2.000 over to 2.100, and the fifth has drifted from 1.500 to 1.543. Play the pair right at a normal 2.000 octave (1200¢) and it grates like a badly mistuned chord; slide a hair right to 2.100 (1284¢) and the roughness collapses to 4%, locking cleanly into place. For this stretched timbre, the pseudo-octave of 2.1 sounds completely in tune while the textbook 2:1 octave clashes.

Try the remaining timbres to see how far that link goes:

  • stiff string: physical metal strings resist bending slightly, which pushes upper partials progressively sharp according to fn = n·f·√(1 + Bn²) (shown here with B = 0.002, typical of piano treble wire). That gentle stretch pulls the octave valley roughly 20 cents sharp, which is the physical reason piano tuners deliberately stretch octaves wide toward the top of the keyboard.
  • metal bar: a uniform bar suspended freely at both ends, like a glockenspiel or vibraphone bar, vibrates at strongly inharmonic ratios of 1, 2.76, 5.40, 8.93, and 13.3. With just five widely spaced partials, roughness stays fairly tame everywhere past a whole tone, showing shallow dips near 1.49, 1.65, and 1.96 while the standard 2:1 octave loses its special status altogether.
  • 10-TET spectrum: each harmonic has been nudged onto the nearest pitch of a scale dividing the octave into 10 equal steps of 120 cents (instead of 12). Look at the orange tick marks along the curve floor: the valleys snap straight onto the 10-TET grid (2\10, 3\10, 4\10, 5\10, 6\10, or 240, 360, 480, 600, and 720 cents). A ten-note equal scale that sounds weirdly sour on a piano suddenly sounds coherent and settled when paired with a matching spectrum.
Aside · Gamelan

Indonesian Javanese gamelan ensembles are dominated by struck bronze bars and bossed gongs whose overtones are naturally inharmonic, and their traditional scales diverge sharply from Western intervals. When Sethares measured the spectra of real gamelan instruments and plotted their dissonance curves, the minima fell surprisingly close to the steps of the five-note slendro scale. That alignment stops short of proving scales evolved solely from acoustics, yet it reveals a tight mutual fit between an instrument's physics and the tuning system built around it, much like the partnership harmonic strings and pipes share with Western intervals.

EpilogueConsonance is a fit

Looking back across these four steps, musical consonance stops feeling like numerology and starts looking like geometry inside the ear. Two frequencies separated by a few hertz beat slowly; widen them into the tens of hertz and they crowd the same cochlear critical band, producing roughness (especially across wide bass bands). On real harmonic instruments, simple integer ratios like 3:2 and 4:3 sound open and settled because they bring whole ladders of overtones into alignment instead of leaving them grinding against one another. And the clincher from Sethares' experiments is that those preferred intervals belong to the partnership between ear and timbre: swap out integer harmonics for stretched partials or vibrating metal bars, and the smooth valleys pack up and move.

There are plenty of directions to push this further: generalizing the roughness landscape to three simultaneous tones so you can see major, minor, and diminished triads as topographical basins; importing spectra recorded from real church bells; or running Sethares' inverse trick to synthesize a custom timbre on the fly that makes an exotic seven-step equal scale sound smoothly in tune.

Before going there, though, Western instruments run headfirst into an arithmetic headache. Every deep valley in the harmonic dissonance curve sits on an exact small-integer ratio, yet a fixed keyboard has to play those ratios starting from any key. Stack twelve pure 3:2 fifths end to end and you overshoot seven pure 2:1 octaves by about a quarter of a semitone. Wrestling with that unavoidable gap is how the modern piano ended up with its twelve slightly compromised notes.
