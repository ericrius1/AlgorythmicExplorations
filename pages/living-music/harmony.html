Living Music · Part 3

Consonance and Roughness

Why some pairs of notes sound smooth and others rough. Two tones are drawing the figure above. Drag along the curve to change the interval between them.

Scroll down to read ↓

The figure at the top is a harmonograph, a pen moved by two vibrations at once. One tone at 220 Hz pushes it sideways, the second tone pushes it up and down, and the trace fades as it goes, like a pendulum drawing. The curve across the top is how rough the two tones sound together for every interval from unison to the octave. Tap to turn the sound on and drag along that curve. When you let go, it snaps to the nearest named interval if one is close. Left alone, it tours a dozen intervals, holding each one for a few seconds.

Notice that the low points of the curve are where the drawing closes into a still, tidy knot, and that those are the intervals that sound smooth. This article is about why. Part one took a single note apart into a pitch, a loudness, and a recipe of harmonics. This part plays two notes at once, and it turns out that the recipe of harmonics decides most of what happens.

Step 1 · Close frequenciesBeats and roughness

Start with the simplest pair: two sine waves at almost the same frequency. They drift in and out of step with each other. When they're in step their peaks add and the sound gets louder. Half a beat later they're opposite, they cancel, and it gets quiet. The sound swells and fades |f₂ − f₁| times a second, because that's how often the faster wave gains a whole cycle on the slower one. 

sin(2π·220·t) + sin(2π·223·t) = 2·cos(2π·1.5·t)·sin(2π·221.5·t)
// a 221.5 Hz tone whose loudness swells 3 times a second

The figure starts there, at A3 (220 Hz) plus 3 Hz. Press play. The left panel shows 20 ms of both waves and their sum sliding in and out of step. The right panel shows the last second of the sum, so you can count the three swells. Then drag the slider (0.1 to 160 Hz above the first tone) slowly to the right.

Below about 10 Hz you hear a slow wobble that you can count, the sound a guitarist listens for when tuning two strings to each other. Past that, the wobble gets too fast to follow and turns into a gritty, unpleasant texture called roughness. At A3 it's worst at about 22 Hz apart. Keep going and the roughness clears up until, past about 60 Hz, you hear two separate, smooth notes.

The curve under the waves is that progression as a graph, from listening tests that Reinier Plomp and Willem Levelt ran in 1965. They found that roughness depends on where the two tones fall in the inner ear. The cochlea sorts sound by frequency along its length, and each spot responds to a band of nearby frequencies called a critical band. Two tones inside the same band shake the same patch of the membrane, and the fast beating between them is what you hear as roughness. Plomp and Levelt found roughness was worst at about a quarter of a critical band apart.

The register buttons show the consequence. Critical bands are wide at low frequencies and narrower, relative to the pitch, at high ones. At A5 the roughest gap is 34 Hz, about two thirds of a semitone. At A2 it's 20 Hz, which down there is almost a minor third, and two sines at A2 stay rough until they're nearly a fifth apart. This is why a close chord low on a piano sounds muddy while the same chord two octaves up sounds clear, and why bass parts tend to be written in wide intervals.

Aside · Tuning by counting

Piano tuners use beats as a measuring tool. To set a temperament they don't judge whether an interval sounds in tune. They play it and count beats between the harmonics of the two strings, and adjust until the beat rate matches a number they already know for that interval. A tuned fifth in the middle of the piano beats a little under once per second.

Step 2 · Simple ratiosPatterns that repeat

Far apart, two sines don't beat, so something else has to separate a smooth interval from a tense one. The next figure shows the other half of the story. One tone drives a dot sideways and the other drives it up and down, the same idea as the hero, drawn from the last 36 ms of the real signals. The pattern it traces is a Lissajous figure.

When the two frequencies form a ratio of small whole numbers, the path closes on itself and stands still. At 3:2 (a perfect fifth, 220 and 330 Hz), the faster tone does three cycles while the slower one does two, and then the whole pattern starts over. The panel on the right marks it: the sum of the two waves repeats every 9.09 ms, 110 times a second, an octave below the lower tone. The ratios with small numbers repeat quickly and draw simple knots. 45:32 (one version of the tritone) also repeats in the end, but only every 145 ms, about 7 times a second. That's too slow to hear as a pitch, and the figure never settles into a simple shape.

Drag the slider a little away from 3:2 and the knot starts turning. It turns at the rate the pattern slips, which the figure computes like this: 

// near p:q, harmonic q of the upper tone and harmonic p of the lower one almost meet
const slip = Math.abs(near.q * f2 - near.p * F1);

That's a beat rate. It's the beat between the third harmonic of 220 Hz and the second harmonic of the upper tone, two frequencies that coincide at an exact fifth. Pure sine waves don't have those harmonics, so you'll hear very little difference between an in-tune and an out-of-tune fifth here. Real instruments do have them, and that's where the next figure comes in.

Aside · Mirrors on tuning forks

Jules Lissajous drew these figures in 1857 without electronics. He glued a small mirror to a tine of each of two tuning forks, set at right angles, and bounced a beam of light off one mirror, then the other, onto a screen. The figure's slow turn showed how far one fork was from an exact ratio with the other, and for decades the method was a standard way to compare frequencies accurately.

Step 3 · Harmonics collideWhere the harmonics land

Part one showed that a real note isn't one sine. It's a stack of harmonics at 1, 2, 3… times its frequency. So when two real notes play together, every harmonic of one is close to some harmonic of the other, and every one of those pairs beats or doesn't according to step 1. Consonance is mostly a matter of how many of those pairs land close enough to be rough.

The figure below shows both stacks on a log-frequency axis, tone A in blue pointing up and tone B in orange pointing down, starting at a fifth. Pairs that land on top of each other flare white: at 3:2, B's 2nd harmonic sits on A's 3rd and B's 4th on A's 6th. Pairs that land close enough to beat glow red. The graph underneath adds up the roughness of every pair for every interval from unison to just past the octave. Drag along it to move tone B.

The valleys are found in the curve itself and labelled by the nearest simple ratio. With the default seven harmonics per tone they fall on 6:5, 5:4, 4:3, 7:5, 3:2, 5:3, 7:4, and 2:1, and the deepest are the octave and the fifth. That's close to the ranking of intervals in any harmony textbook, worked out from one psychoacoustic curve and some addition. The ratios with a 7 in them (7:5 and 7:4) show up because harmonic 7 is in the stack. Western tuning doesn't use them, though barbershop quartets are often said to tune their seventh chords toward 7:4, and no piano key plays them.

The harmonics slider shows where the valleys come from. Drag it down to 1 and you're back to pure sines: the curve has one hump near unison and no valleys at all. With 2 harmonics the octave appears. With 3 the fifth appears. Each harmonic you add brings in new coincidences and new valleys. Then try the register buttons. At C3 the thirds' valleys are about half as deep as at A4, the same low-register muddiness as in step 1. 

// Plomp–Levelt roughness of two partials, in Sethares' parameterization
export function roughness(f1: number, a1: number, f2: number, a2: number): number {
  const lo = Math.min(f1, f2);
  const s = 0.24 / (0.0207 * lo + 18.96);
  const x = s * Math.abs(f2 - f1);
  return Math.min(a1, a2) * (Math.exp(-3.51 * x) - Math.exp(-5.75 * x));
}
Deep dive: the model behind the curve

William Sethares fit Plomp and Levelt's data with the difference of two exponentials above. The roughness of a pair is zero at unison, rises to a peak, and decays as the pair separates. The factor s scales the frequency gap by the width of the critical band at the lower frequency, so the peak always sits at x ≈ 0.22, which works out to 22 Hz apart at 220 Hz. Each pair is weighted by the quieter of its two amplitudes. The figures give harmonic n an amplitude of 0.88n−1 and sum the roughness of every pair, one partial from each tone. (Pairs inside one tone don't change with the interval, so they're left out.)

It's a model of sensory roughness and nothing else. It doesn't know about the way two notes at an octave fuse into one sound, about how chords move from one to the next, or about what a listener is used to hearing. Those matter too. What's surprising is how much of the traditional ranking of intervals falls out of roughness alone.

Step 4 · Change the sound, change the scaleTimbre moves the valleys

If the valleys come from where the harmonics land, then a sound with different harmonics should have different valleys. Sethares followed that idea through in the 1990s. The next figure is the same dissonance curve, with buttons that swap both tones for an inharmonic sound, one whose partials aren't whole-number multiples of the lowest one.

It starts on "stretched", one of Sethares' own examples. Its partials are spaced as if an octave were a ratio of 2.1 instead of 2, so partial n sits at 2.1log₂n. Look at the curve: the deepest valley has moved from 2.0 to 2.1, and the 3:2 valley has moved to 1.543. Play the two tones at 2.000 and they sound rough, an octave that's out of tune. Play them at 2.100 and they're smooth. With this timbre, a stretched octave is the consonant one.

The other buttons:

  • Stiff string. Real strings are slightly stiff, which pushes each partial a little sharp: fn = n·f·√(1 + Bn²), here with B = 0.002. The octave valley moves to about 20 cents wide. Piano tuners stretch their octaves for the same reason.
  • Metal bar. A uniform bar free at both ends, like a glockenspiel bar, has partials at 1, 2.76, 5.40, 8.93 and 13.3 times the lowest. With only five partials spread that far apart, the curve is low almost everywhere past a whole tone, with shallow dips near 1.49, 1.65 and 1.96. The octave isn't special any more.
  • 10-TET spectrum. Each harmonic moved to the nearest step of a scale that divides the octave into 10 equal steps instead of 12. The valleys move onto steps of that scale (240, 360, 480, 600, 720 cents and so on). A ten-note scale that sounds out of tune with ordinary instruments can sound in tune with a timbre built to match it.
Aside · Gamelan

The metallophones of a Javanese gamelan are bars and gongs, so their partials are inharmonic, and their tunings don't follow Western ratios. Sethares measured the spectra of gamelan instruments and found that the dissonance curves they produce have minima close to steps of the five-note slendro scale. That doesn't prove the scales came from the timbres. It does show the two fit each other, which is the same relationship harmonic timbres have with Western intervals.

EpilogueConsonance is a fit

  • Close frequencies beat. Slow beats wobble, beats in the tens of hertz sound rough, and the size of the rough zone is set by the ear's critical bands, which are widest in the bass.
  • Simple ratios line up harmonics. At 3:2 the harmonics of two notes coincide instead of beating, and small numbers mean many coincidences.
  • The consonant intervals depend on the timbre. Harmonic timbres produce the familiar intervals. Change the partials and the smooth intervals move with them.

Things I'd try next: the same curve for three notes, so you can compare major and minor chords by roughness; a "bell" timbre measured from a real recording; and a figure that builds a custom timbre for whatever scale you give it, the way Sethares did, so a seven-step equal scale sounds in tune.

One hard problem is left over. Every valley in the curve sits on a ratio of small whole numbers, and a keyboard has to pick a fixed set of notes that plays those ratios from any starting note. Stack twelve perfect fifths and you land almost, but not quite, on seven octaves. The gap is about a quarter of a semitone, and dealing with it is how the piano got its twelve notes.
