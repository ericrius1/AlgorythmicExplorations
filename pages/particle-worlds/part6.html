Particle Worlds · Part 6

The Fluid Instrument

A 3D fluid toy built on part five's Poisson solve, with every number tuned by eye for how it feels to stir. Wave your cursor through it, or press ✋ hands to reach in with your webcam.

Scroll down to read ↓

For five parts the series has been chasing physical models: orbits, galaxies, sloshing water, accreting dust, and the cosmic web. This finale throws away physical units and builds an instrument instead. Every parameter in the glowing cloud above (drag, buoyancy, trail length, color stops) was picked purely by eye until it felt satisfying to swirl around.

Visual effects artists call this art direction, and it turns out almost all of the numerical machinery from the earlier essays stays useful when your goal is expressive motion. Under the paint sits a real 3D fluid solver driven by the exact same Poisson equation just solved for cosmology, paired with curl noise, hundreds of thousands of lightweight tracers, and an optional webcam hand tracker running straight on your GPU.

Step 1 · Incompressible flowAdvection and pressure projection

What immediately signals "fluid" to the eye is the way smoke and water get out of your way. Wave your palm through incense smoke and the smoke refuses to compress into a lump ahead of your hand; it slips sideways, curls around your wrist, and rolls up into twin vortices behind it. That refusal to pile up or thin out is incompressibility, and enforcing it brings back a familiar equation.

Part five solved ∇²φ = 4πGρ to turn mass density into gravitational potential. Fluids use the exact same differential equation with pressure in place of gravity. After you stir a velocity field u, add wind, or let it coast forward, the resulting field picks up regions where flow converges into a sink or bursts out of a source. Incompressible flow requires the divergence ∇·u to be zero everywhere, so the solver finds a scalar pressure field p whose gradient cancels out every source and sink: 

∇²p = ∇·u        // find the pressure pushing back against pile-ups
u ← u − ∇p       // subtract its gradient to restore zero divergence

Step through one projection below to watch a burst of outflow collapse while a neighboring vortex sails through untouched:

The other half of the fluid step carries the velocity field along its own currents, a stage called advection. Pushing values forward from each cell along its velocity vector tends to overshoot and explode whenever speeds get high. Instead, the solver traces backward: stand at each grid cell, ask where the fluid sitting here came from one timestep earlier, and sample the old velocity field at that departure point: 

u_new(x) = u_old(x − u(x)·dt)   // semi-Lagrangian: look up where the fluid came from

Sampling at that departure point trilinearly blends the eight surrounding grid cells. Because a weighted average can never exceed the largest value going into it, the velocity field can never blow up, regardless of timestep size or how wildly you whip the cursor across the screen. The trade-off is a small amount of numerical smearing every frame, which softens crisp curls over time. That trade-off is fine for an interactive toy where stability matters far more than razor-sharp engineering accuracy.

Aside · Stable Fluids, 1999

Jos Stam popularized this exact loop, semi-Lagrangian advection followed by pressure projection, in his 1999 SIGGRAPH paper Stable Fluids. Because it stays unconditionally stable under arbitrary user input, it became the default blueprint for real-time smoke and fire in games and VFX, helped enormously by his 2003 GDC follow-up paper containing a clean, self-contained C implementation.

Why ten Jacobi sweeps is plenty when nobody is grading the math

The 3D playground runs on a 64³ grid (262,144 cells) every frame. Part five solved its Poisson equation exactly in one shot with an FFT, which relied on periodic wraparound boundaries. This fluid sits inside a walled cube, so it uses ten sweeps of Jacobi relaxation instead: on every sweep, each cell averages its six 3D neighbors, subtracts the scaled local divergence, and writes the updated pressure, starting fresh from zero each frame.

Ten sweeps is nowhere near mathematically converged. Each Jacobi iteration only communicates pressure about one cell outward, so broad divergence across half the box lingers into subsequent frames. In practice, whatever remains gets trimmed down again on the next frame, and a faint large-scale squishiness looks indistinguishable from a perfectly rigid solve while running in a fraction of a millisecond.

Step 2 · Ambient motionCurl noise

Left untouched, a projected fluid eventually coasts to a stop as drag and numerical smearing sap its vortices. To keep smoke breathing gently between gestures, effects pipelines inject a drifting ambient wind. A naive way to build wind is to evaluate a smooth random heightfield and push fluid along its slope. Switch between the two modes below to see why that immediately goes wrong:

In downhill (sinks) mode, every tracer races straight toward the nearest peak of the noise and gets trapped there. A gradient field is pure sources and sinks, the exact kind of compression the pressure solver is fighting to eliminate. Rotate that exact same 2D gradient by 90° and flow runs along the contour lines instead of climbing them, so fluid circulates in closed eddies with zero divergence by construction. In 2D, rotating by 90° is just swapping components and flipping one sign; in 3D, the counterpart is taking the curl of a three-channel noise potential: 

v = ∇ × ψ        // ψ: three channels of smooth 3D noise
∇·(∇ × ψ) = 0    // the divergence of any curl is identically zero

That vector field is curl noise: lively, turbulent motion that never pools and stays divergence-free before the pressure solver even runs. The playground adds a slowly drifting curl-noise force to the 64³ grid every frame, giving the volume a gentle breathing motion whenever nobody is stirring it. In the WGSL shader, each noise gradient is differentiated analytically from the same eight lattice-corner hashes used to interpolate the noise value, requiring three evaluations per cell instead of the eighteen noise lookups finite differences would demand.

Aside · Curl noise, 2007

Robert Bridson, Jim Hourihan, and Marcus Nordenstam introduced curl noise to graphics in a 2007 SIGGRAPH paper, showing how to modulate the underlying potential near solid obstacles so flow wraps cleanly around geometry too. Because it produces swirling, turbulent velocity fields cheaply without solving any equations, it quickly became a staple across film and game effects.

Step 3 · Tracer particlesWeightless paint carried by the grid

In parts one through five, the particles were the physical actors: they carried mass, exerted forces on one another, and drove the entire simulation. Here their job is purely visual. Each particle is a massless tracer riding the 64³ velocity grid: on every frame it samples the local velocity via trilinear interpolation (the 3D twin of part five's gather pass) and steps forward. Nothing gets deposited back onto the grid, so the fluid behaves identically whether you slide particles down to 50,000 or all the way up to 1,000,000 (the playground below starts at 240,000).

Each tracer lives for a few seconds before respawning. In ink and nebula, a bit over half of dying tracers respawn right around an active stirrer so streams appear to pour straight off your cursor or fingertips, while the remaining fraction refills the rest of the cube. A tiny random drift added to each particle's velocity keeps ribbons soft and feathery instead of pinching into harsh single-pixel threads.

Three rendering choices give those dots their painterly look. First, each particle renders as a velocity-stretched capsule (part two's camera-facing billboard extended backward along its velocity vector), turning fast currents into bright filaments while calm eddies settle back into fine specks. Second, the renderer accumulates into a floating-point HDR trail buffer that multiplies by a decay factor every frame rather than clearing to black, working like a long-exposure photograph; the presets retain 84% to 90% of the prior frame, and the trails slider lets you push that retention anywhere from 50% to 97%.

Third, each color mode tweaks both the three-stop speed palette and the underlying grid dynamics. Ink maps slow-to-fast speed from deep cobalt through bright cyan to near-white. Embers shifts to dark crimson, orange, and pale gold, spawning most fresh particles from a warm coal bed near the floor while lifting dyed fluid upward with buoyancy. Nebula blends violet, magenta, and teal with a gentler speed cap, a soft pull back toward the center, and the lowest drag of the three so slow arcs linger in midair. Because every tracer only reads eight grid cells and ignores all other particles, updating a quarter million of them costs barely anything compared to the 64³ fluid passes steering them.

Step 4 · Hand trackingTwenty-one landmarks in the browser

A mouse cursor gives you a single point on a flat screen. Turning on your webcam hands the fluid two palms, two index tips, and pinch gestures using 7.7 MB of neural network weights running locally on WebGPU:

The tracker is micro-handpose, a compact WebGPU port of Google's MediaPipe hand pipeline. It locates palms in the camera frame and predicts twenty-one 3D landmarks per hand: four joints along every finger plus the wrist. Once a hand is locked in, subsequent frames track it from its prior bounding box without rerunning the full palm detector. Nothing loads until you click 📷 start hand tracking, and every frame of video stays inside your browser tab on your local GPU.

Inside the playground, each detected hand feeds up to three stirrers into the fluid grid. Your palm drives a wide Gaussian push whose strength scales up as you splay your fingers open. Your index fingertip drives a tighter, higher-emission brush for sketching crisp ribbons. Finally, the tracker measures how closely your thumb and index tips meet, normalized by your palm size so the gesture behaves the same whether you sit close to the laptop or lean back: 

hand_scale = |wrist − middle_finger_base|     // stays steady as fingers bend
pinch = clamp(1 − |thumb_tip − index_tip| / (1.05 · hand_scale), 0, 1)

Whenever pinch crosses 0.45, a third stirrer switches on at the midpoint between thumb and index finger, pulling nearby fluid inward while spinning it around the camera's view axis. Tuning those radii, thresholds, and swirl weights was pure trial and error: waving at the screen, squinting at the wake, and adjusting numbers until pinching actually felt like grabbing a whirlpool.

Step 5 · All togetherThe playground

Here is the full instrument. Move your cursor across the canvas to stir the volume, and click-drag to orbit around the 3D box (pinch on a trackpad or hold ⌘/Ctrl while scrolling to zoom, so regular scrolling still moves down the page). Clicking ink, embers, or nebula smoothly crossfades the palette and fluid constants without resetting the currents already in motion. Press ✋ enable hands if you want to reach in with your webcam: wave to push the volume, trace streaks with your index finger, and pinch to spin up a vortex.

Try drawing a slow figure-eight in nebula and then lifting your hand away: with drag dialed way down, the closed loop keeps turning on its own for several seconds. Leave the box untouched for two and a half seconds and a gentle invisible stirrer takes over along a 3D Lissajous path so the fluid always stays alive while you watch.

EpilogueSix parts, one toolkit

Even though this final playground drops physical units and lets the eye overrule conservation laws, nearly every piece inside it came straight out of the five essays before it. One GPU thread per particle reading shared state and writing only its own slot arrived in part one; camera-facing billboards and the orbit camera came from part two; local neighborhood updates on a grid echoed part three's short-range forces; running distinct passes together in one frame followed part four; and reading forces off a mesh after a Poisson solve came directly from part five, with fluid pressure stepping in for gravitational potential. If you wanted to push the solver further, replacing those ten Jacobi sweeps with a multigrid V-cycle would clear out large-scale divergence cleanly, and swapping plain semi-Lagrangian advection for MacCormack or BFECC advection would sharpen the swirling ribbons.

That wraps up Particle Worlds. The series started back in part one with two dots falling toward each other under one line of Newtonian gravity, and almost every hurdle since turned out to be a question of data structures: a pointer-free mass pyramid, an octree bent over a planetarium dome, a counting sort that lines up physical neighbors in GPU memory, a spatial hash that removes the walls, a Fourier transform that diagonalizes a differential equation into division, and finally all of those ideas folded into something you can reach into and play with.

Back to the start: Part one, Two Falling Dots. One force formula, a slingshot, and the first O(n²) wall.
