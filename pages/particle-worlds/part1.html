Particle Worlds · Part 1

Two Falling Dots

Taking a live gravity simulator from two dots in JavaScript up to 300,000 bodies on WebGPU, fixing whichever bottleneck breaks next.

Scroll down to read ↓

If you try to simulate 300,000 gravitating stars the straightforward way, every star pulls on every other star on every tick: 300,000², or 90 billion pairwise force calculations per step. Even in tight JavaScript, a single frame at that scale would stall a browser tab for more than a minute. Yet the 40,000-body galaxy spinning above is running live on your graphics card in slow motion, and the final demo down the page runs the exact same solver up to 300,000 bodies at interactive frame rates.

Getting from two dots to a full disk of stars takes six steps. Each version runs right in the page until it hits a wall, and each fix directly addresses whatever stopped the one before it.

Step 1 · One forceThe formula

Every piece of physics on this page comes down to a single equation. Everything after this section is about evaluating it fast enough to watch. 

// acceleration of body i, caused by body j
d   = position[j] - position[i]
a_i += G * mass[j] * d / (|d|² + ε²)^(3/2)

That is Newton's law of universal gravitation with one extra constant tucked into the denominator: ε², known as softening. Under exact inverse-square gravity, the pull climbs toward infinity as two point masses pass close together. On a computer taking discrete time steps, a close flyby delivers one gigantic impulse during a single step and slingshots the body right out of the simulation at absurd speed. Adding ε² caps the pull once the separation drops near ε, behaving as if each point mass were smeared into a tiny cloud of radius ε.

Aside · Where softening comes from

Astronomers wrote down this exact potential decades before digital computers existed. In 1911, Henry Plummer fitted a density profile to globular star clusters whose gravitational field reduces cleanly to this softened expression. Each softened particle in an n-body code is effectively a tiny Plummer sphere.

You can feel what ε does in the sandbox below. Press anywhere in the dark space, drag to set a launch velocity while watching the dashed orbit preview, and release. Then drag softening ε down to 0.01 and aim a body straight through the star: it whips back out much faster than it fell in. That numerical slingshot is what softening tames.

One small choice in the time-stepping loop also makes a surprising difference, and it comes for free. When advancing each body, update its velocity from the current acceleration first, then move its position using that updated velocity (semi-implicit Euler). Reversing those two lines so the old velocity moves the body first leaks energy into every orbit, causing tight planets to spiral outward. Click the order: velocity first button to switch to position first and watch the inner planet unwind after just a few laps.

Why the order matters

Updating velocity before position makes the integrator symplectic. Even though each individual step still carries a small position error, the map preserves area in phase space, a core geometric invariant of Hamiltonian mechanics. Instead of pushing orbits steadily outward, the errors nudge the ellipse slightly sideways so total energy oscillates around the true value rather than drifting off to infinity.

Its close cousin, leapfrog integration, has been the workhorse of astrophysical and molecular dynamics codes since the 1960s for the exact same reason.

A star with a handful of planets stays cheap because almost all of the pull comes from one central object. Things get serious once every body in a cloud has to feel every other body.

Step 2 · All pairsEveryone pulls on everyone

A real disk of stars has no single anchor holding it in place; the stars bind one another through their own mutual attraction. Once three or more bodies are involved, closed-form orbits vanish, and the only way to see how a system evolves is to march it forward step by step.

Aside · Chaos, and a simulation made of light bulbs

In the 1890s, competing for a prize offered by King Oscar II of Sweden, Henri Poincaré proved that even three gravitating bodies can behave chaotically: tiny changes in starting conditions grow so fast that no formula can predict their long-term paths.

The very first n-body galaxy simulation happened without an electronic computer at all. In 1941, Erik Holmberg arranged 74 light bulbs on a lab table as two miniature "galaxies" of 37 bulbs each. Because light intensity falls off as 1/r² just like gravity, he measured the illumination hitting each spot with a photocell, converted the reading into a displacement, shifted every bulb by hand, and repeated. His tabletop galaxies swung past each other and pulled out tidal tails that digital computers would only reproduce decades later.

On a computer, summing every pairwise interaction is just two nested loops:

for (let i = 0; i < count; i++) {
  let ax = 0, ay = 0;
  for (let j = 0; j < count; j++) {
    const dx = x[j] - x[i], dy = y[j] - y[i];
    const r2 = dx*dx + dy*dy + eps2;
    const inv = G * mass[j] / (r2 * Math.sqrt(r2));
    ax += dx * inv;  ay += dy * inv;
  }
  vx[i] += ax * dt;  vy[i] += ay * dt;
}

That double loop is exact (up to softening and time step) and serves as the ground truth for checking every faster method later. Push the bodies slider to the right below and watch the millisecond readout climb. Because work grows as the square of the body count, doubling the particles quadruples the math; by 4,000 bodies you are already doing 16 million force evaluations every single step.

Somewhere along that slider your browser starts dropping frames, even though that inner loop is about as lean as JavaScript gets. An O(n²) loop eventually swamps any constant-factor optimization. Pushing much further calls for both massively parallel hardware and a smarter algorithm, starting with the chip already sitting inside your computer.

Step 3 · ParallelMove it to the GPU

Take another look at the outer loop over i. Computing the acceleration on body 0 depends only on the positions from the start of the step, completely independent of body 1 or body 50,000. That independence is exactly what GPUs thrive on: thousands of threads executing identical arithmetic side by side.

Moving to a WebGPU compute shader replaces the outer loop with a launch of one thread per body, while each thread still runs the inner sum over all other bodies.

There is an immediate catch with GPU hardware: memory bandwidth runs dry long before raw arithmetic does. If 100,000 threads each stream 100,000 positions straight from global VRAM, the memory bus chokes immediately. Instead, threads team up in workgroups of 256. Each thread fetches one body into fast on-chip shared memory to assemble a 256-body tile, everyone waits at a barrier, every thread sums forces against all 256 bodies in that tile, and then the group moves on to the next tile. 

var<workgroup> tile: array<vec3f, 256>;  // fast on-chip memory

for (var t = 0u; t < numTiles; t++) {
  tile[local_id] = load(bodies[t * 256u + local_id]); // each thread fetches one
  workgroupBarrier();                                  // wait for the whole tile
  for (var k = 0u; k < 256u; k++) {
    acc += force(tile[k], myPosition);                 // everyone uses all 256
  }
  workgroupBarrier();
}

Every body now crosses global memory once per workgroup rather than once per thread, trimming memory traffic by a factor of 256. This tiled n-body kernel from GPU Gems 3 has become a classic first compute shader for good reason.

Aside · When gravity got its own chips

In the 1990s, astrophysicists at the University of Tokyo wanted fast pairwise gravity so badly that they designed custom silicon for it. Their GRAPE ("GRAvity PipE") boards hard-wired Newton's force law straight into the circuits, and in 1995 GRAPE-4 became the first computer to cross one teraflop, solely evaluating gravitational pulls. Today's shader runs the same workload on commodity graphics chips driven down in price by thirty years of video games.

The slider now starts at 30,000 bodies and stretches all the way to 120,000, pushing the wall dozens of times farther out than the CPU could reach. Even so, the quadratic curve is still waiting: at 120,000 bodies the GPU is grinding through 14.4 billion pair forces on every single tick. To reach hundreds of thousands of bodies cleanly, we have to compute far fewer forces in the first place.

Step 4 · ApproximationGroup the far-away bodies

All trillion stars in the Andromeda galaxy are tugging on you right now, yet from two and a half million light-years away you could replace the whole galaxy with a single heavy point at its center of mass and barely notice the difference. Whenever a cluster of bodies is small compared to its distance from you, its individual positions blur into one combined pull.

In 1986, Josh Barnes and Piet Hut turned that observation into a tree algorithm. Pack the space into a quadtree: divide the square into four quadrants, subdivide any quadrant holding more than one body into four again, and repeat down the hierarchy. Each cell stores the total mass and center of mass of everything inside it. To find the acceleration on a body, walk down from the root and test each cell against a single opening angle threshold, θ: 

// is this cell small enough, seen from here, to count as one point?
if (cellWidth² < θ² × distance²) {
  acc += forceFrom(cell.totalMass, cell.centerOfMass);  // one term for the whole cell
} else {
  descend into the four children;                       // too close, look closer
}

The parameter θ controls the trade-off between accuracy and speed by setting how small a cell's width-to-distance ratio has to be before it collapses into a single point mass. Nearby cells fail the test and split open all the way down to individual bodies, while the opposite side of the galaxy folds into a handful of large lumps. A sum that used to take n terms shrinks to roughly log n.

At the default θ = 0.8, the walk typically evaluates around 50 to 70 terms instead of 3,000 (under 2% of the work) and lands within a couple percent of the exact force. Tightening θ to 0.3 cuts the error below 1% while still avoiding roughly nine tenths of the pairwise work.

Aside · A three-page paper and its relatives

Barnes and Hut's original Nature paper is only three pages long, stitching together two ideas that already existed separately: the quadtree introduced by Raphael Finkel and Jon Bentley in 1974 for spatial databases, and the classical multipole trick of summarizing distant mass at its center.

A year later, Leslie Greengard and Vladimir Rokhlin published the Fast Multipole Method, which pushes the scaling all the way down to O(n) with strict error bounds by also grouping target bodies together. That core strategy of exact near-field math paired with hierarchical far-field summaries turns up everywhere from multigrid solvers to wavelet compression.

Moving that tree walk onto the GPU seems like the obvious final leap, except one piece of the algorithm resists parallelization: building the tree itself.

Step 5 · The bottleneckA tree with nothing to build

A classic pointer-based Barnes–Hut quadtree is constructed one body at a time. You walk down to the leaf where a body belongs, and if another body is already sitting there, you allocate four child nodes and push both bodies deeper. Because every insertion depends on the splits left behind by earlier insertions, construction is inherently sequential, and since every body moves every step, you have to rebuild the entire tree from scratch every single frame.

Keeping the tree build on the CPU while running forces on the GPU means copying every body back over the bus, building the quadtree in single-threaded JavaScript, and uploading the whole tree back to VRAM on every tick. Past roughly 100,000 bodies, that CPU round trip eats more time than the actual gravity calculation.

You can wrestle a pointer-based build onto the GPU with locks or Morton-code radix sorts, and production engines often do. A simpler alternative changes the shape of the tree so there are no nodes to allocate or link at all.

Make the quadtree complete: subdivide every cell uniformly to the same finest resolution, whether it holds stars or empty space. Once every level is a full grid, the tree topology turns into index arithmetic. The four children of cell (level, ix, iy) always sit at (level+1, 2ix+dx, 2iy+dy), and every level packs tightly into one flat buffer. If you have ever worked with texture mipmaps, you have seen this layout before: it is a mipmap of mass.

Aside · Mipmaps, 1983

Lance Williams introduced mipmaps in 1983 (taking the name from the Latin multum in parvo, "much in little") to keep distant textures from shimmering into aliasing noise. Graphics hardware eventually gained dedicated silicon for them. The exact same hierarchy doubles as a gravity accelerator because both problems boil down to pre-averaging fine detail you are too far away to resolve.

Drag the pyramid level slider below to step up and down the hierarchy on a live 4,000-body disk. Level 7 bins the disk into a 128×128 grid; each coarser level merges 2×2 blocks into a single parent cell, all the way up to level 0 holding the entire galaxy. Color encodes total mass and the pink dots mark each cell's center of mass, giving the Barnes–Hut walk the exact same data it expects from a pointer tree.

Best of all, populating a complete pyramid is completely parallel and takes two stages:

Scatter. Launch one thread per body. Each thread hashes its body into a finest-level cell and atomically accumulates its mass and weighted local offset: 

// one thread per body, in any order, nobody waits
atomicAdd(&grid[cell],     u32(round(m * FP_SCALE)));                     // Σ mass
atomicAdd(&grid[cell + 1], bitcast<u32>(i32(round(m * dx * FP_SCALE)))); // Σ m·dx
atomicAdd(&grid[cell + 2], bitcast<u32>(i32(round(m * dy * FP_SCALE)))); // Σ m·dy

Reduce. March upward one level at a time, launching one thread per parent cell to sum its four children with zero write conflicts. Building a full 1024×1024 pyramid takes ten tiny reduction passes and finishes in roughly a millisecond, replacing a serial tree build with one atomic scatter and a mipmap chain.

Fixed-point atomics: three tricks in three lines

WGSL offers atomic addition only on 32-bit integers, so the scatter converts floats to fixed point by multiplying by a scale factor and rounding before the atomicAdd, then dividing back out afterward. The scale factor is chosen conservatively so that even piling every body's mass into one cell cannot overflow 32 bits.

Storing dx and dy relative to each cell's center bounds both offsets to within half a cell width, letting all 32 bits go toward fractional precision instead of spanning the whole galaxy.

Negative offsets pass through unsigned atomics by bitcasting signed two's-complement integers into u32. Modular unsigned addition yields the exact same bit pattern as signed addition, so everything cancels cleanly, and each cell's center of mass comes right back out as Σ(m·dx) / Σm.

Step 6 · GPU-residentThe whole pipeline on the GPU

Putting every piece together turns one simulation step into a fixed sequence of GPU compute dispatches (fifteen at the 1024×1024 cap), with zero CPU readbacks: 

clear    → zero the grid's atomic accumulators
bounds   → where is everything? (parallel min/max)
scatter  → every body adds its mass to its finest cell   (the atomics above)
resolve  → fixed point back to mass + center of mass
reduce   → build the pyramid, one level per pass         (10 passes at 1024²)
force    → every body walks the implicit tree            (Barnes–Hut, no pointers)
           …and moves itself, in place

The bounds pass shrinks or stretches the root box around the current particle positions every step so drifting or ejected stars never escape the grid. Meanwhile, the force pass updates each body's position and velocity in place inside a single buffer: because no thread ever inspects another body's slot directly and gets all outside gravity exclusively through the pyramid, there is no race condition and no second body buffer to ping-pong.

Atomic min and max on floats

Finding the bounding box on the GPU requires atomic min and max on floating-point coordinates, which WGSL lacks just like float atomic adds. Fortunately, IEEE 754 floats can be remapped bijectively onto order-preserving unsigned integers: flip just the sign bit for positive numbers, and flip all 32 bits for negative numbers. Ordinary integer atomicMin and atomicMax then find the true extremes, and applying the inverse bit-flip restores the exact float bounds.

Aside · Why galaxies need a halo

A thin, cold rotating disk held together strictly by its own gravity is violently unstable. In 1973, Jeremiah Ostriker and Jim Peebles ran n-body simulations of a few hundred particles and watched their flat disks repeatedly buckle into rotating bars. Because real spiral galaxies stay flat for billions of years, they argued that visible disks must sit embedded inside a much heavier invisible halo, providing one of the foundational arguments for dark matter. Our demo uses a simple cored halo pull, v²·r / (r² + core²), which yields the flat rotation curve astronomers observe in real spirals.

Starting a disk that doesn't fly apart

To begin on clean circular orbits, each body needs an orbital speed matching the inward pull at its starting radius. The textbook spherical shortcut, v² = G·M(<r)/r, assumes mass outside radius r cancels out. Inside a flat disk that assumption fails because outer rings actually pull outward on inner stars; using the spherical formula near the center launched bodies more than twice too fast and blew a hollow crater out of the core within a second.

Instead, the seeding routine samples the actual softened radial acceleration across 48 radii, adds the halo term, and assigns each body the circular velocity that balances that measured force, plus a small random velocity dispersion so the disk has realistic texture.

Aside · How the real universe gets simulated

In 2005, the Millennium Run evolved ten billion dark-matter particles from shortly after the Big Bang to the present day. Its simulation code, GADGET-2, paired a Barnes–Hut tree for short- and medium-range gravity with a Fourier grid method for the long-range field (which part five explores in detail). The clusters are vastly bigger and runs take months, yet the underlying recipe is identical: soften close encounters, summarize distant mass hierarchically, and keep particle data parked right beside the compute units.

EpilogueWhat carries over

Four habits from this gravity solver end up driving every simulation in the rest of this series. First, identify which bottleneck you are actually hitting: throwing a GPU at an O(n²) loop moves the wall, whereas changing the algorithm to O(n log n) breaks through it. Second, treat accuracy as a tunable knob; letting θ trade a couple percent of force error for a fifty-fold reduction in work makes real-time interaction possible. Third, remember that data layout dictates parallelism: a pointer quadtree and a mass pyramid store the same physical quantities, yet flattening the hierarchy into a uniform grid lets the GPU build it in a millisecond without locks. Finally, avoid shuttling buffers across the CPU-GPU bus whenever the whole loop can stay resident in VRAM.

From here, there are several natural upgrades worth exploring: ordering particles along a space-filling Morton curve so neighboring GPU threads walk the same branches of the tree together, swapping the fixed pyramid for a parallel adaptive tree (like an LBVH) when matter collapses into extreme clumps, and lifting the whole solver into three dimensions.
