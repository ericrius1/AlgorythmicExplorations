Particle Worlds · Part 4

Worlds from Dust

20,000 dust grains orbiting a star, pulled together by part one's gravity pyramid and knocked into clumps by part three's contact grid. Drag your cursor through the ring to stir it up.

Scroll down to read ↓

Drop a cloud of dust into empty space with gravity turned on and you might expect it to collapse into a rock. It won't. Every grain accelerates toward the center, rushes straight past it, and climbs right back out the far side. Without something to bump into and bleed off kinetic energy, a gravitational cloud just breathes in and out forever.

Making dust actually build something takes two different kinds of bookkeeping at once. Part one built a mass pyramid for the far field, where distant stars only matter in bulk. Part three built a sorted grid for the near field, where liquid particles only care about whatever is right against them. Run both structures over the same particles every substep and you get dust that attracts from across the disk, collides on contact, and sticks into growing knots. That is what is happening in the ring at the top of the page: 20,000 grains on nearly circular orbits around a star, curdling into spiral streaks and tightening into moonlets in a simplified 2D sketch of planetesimal formation.

Step 1 · No surfacesWhy gravity alone stays ghostly

Back in part one, softening was added to gravity so two bodies passing right over each other wouldn't divide by zero and fling off to infinity. Softening also sneaks in a physical assumption: particles have no solid surface. Once two bodies get inside the softening radius, the pull tapers off and they glide straight through each other like ghosts. For a galaxy, that's completely faithful. Stars are tiny next to the gulf between them, so when two whole galaxies merge, direct stellar collisions almost never happen. Astronomers call a system like that collisionless.

A dusty nursery around a young star behaves very differently. Watch what happens to a cold, gently spinning disk of 20,000 dust grains when gravity runs by itself, and then click both to turn contact forces on:

With only gravity active, the disk falls inward, crowds through the middle, and expands right back out. Gravity is conservative: every bit of kinetic energy gained on the way down goes straight into climbing back up the potential well on the other side. No matter how long you wait, the cloud stays a loose swarm because it has no way to shed the energy of its own collapse. Switching to both adds solid contacts with damping, and the swarm finally packs down into a dense spinning body. To make those contacts work out in open space, though, part three's grid needs one big upgrade first.

Step 2 · Open spaceTaking the grid out of its walls

Part three's contact grid relied on a fixed tank. Every cell got an array index from its coordinates inside that tank, and the walls guaranteed the array stayed finite. That worked cleanly for water sloshing inside a box, since the tank was part of the scene anyway. A collapsing dust cloud or an orbiting ring sits in open space, where close encounters occasionally kick a stray grain far out into the dark. Covering open space with a flat grid forces an ugly choice: allocate millions of empty cells for space nobody visits, or draw a tight box and watch escaping grains fall off the edge of the array.

Spatial hashing keeps the fixed-size cells and throws away the walls. Each cell is still just a pair of integer coordinates, column and row, anywhere across the entire plane. Instead of indexing directly into a 2D array, the code hashes those two integers into a fixed table of buckets: 

fn bucketOf(c: vec2i) -> u32 {
  let h = (u32(c.x) * 0x9E3779B1u) ^ (u32(c.y) * 0x85EBCA77u);
  return h & (TABLE - 1u);   // TABLE is a power of two
}

For the default 20,000 grains, the table holds 65,536 buckets, scaling up to 262,144 and 1,048,576 when you push the grain slider higher. Everything else from part three's sorting pipeline carries straight over: count grains per bucket, prefix-sum the counts into bucket offsets, and scatter the particles into contiguous runs.

Folding an infinite plane into a finite table creates one side effect: distant, unrelated cells occasionally land in the same bucket. When a grain checks its 3×3 neighborhood, it pulls in a few impostors from elsewhere in the disk. That turns out to be harmless because the contact loop already checks the exact distance to every candidate. Real touching neighbors pass that distance test and distant strangers fail it immediately. The only trap is duplicate buckets inside the 3×3 window itself: if two of the nine nearby cells hash to the same bucket, searching that bucket twice would double-count every contact inside it, so the kernel skips any bucket it has already seen.

You can see those hash collisions directly below. It's the same 3,500-disk pile from part three on a 24×24 grid (576 cells), intentionally squeezed into a tiny 128-bucket table so collisions are everywhere. Move your cursor over the pile to aim the yellow 3×3 query window; the red cells are far-off cells sharing one of its buckets, and the red disks are the impostors the distance check throws away. The query window stays where you leave it when you move down to drag the hash table buckets slider, so you can watch the red thin out as the table grows.

Aside · Hashing, 1953

In January 1953, IBM researcher Hans Peter Luhn circulated an internal memo proposing that records be filed into numbered buckets calculated directly from their keys, with colliding records chained together inside the bucket. Arnold Dumey published the first open paper on the technique in 1956, and Knuth later traced priority back to Luhn's memo. Luhn's name also lives on the check-digit formula at the end of credit card numbers, which he patented in 1960.

Step 3 · One bufferTwo data structures over the same memory

Once the grid is unbounded, wiring gravity and contacts together runs straight into a subtle memory bug. Part three's counting sort permutes the particle buffer every substep so particles in the same bucket sit side by side in memory. After one shuffle, grain #4,071 is a completely different grain. Part one's galaxy solver stored each body's mass in a separate array looked up by index. Hook the two solvers together naively and the particles move around while the mass array sits still, handing every grain someone else's mass four times a frame.

You could sort the mass array alongside the positions and velocities, turning it into a key–value sort with one more buffer to shuffle. Instead, this page changes the problem: every dust grain gets the same mass. Uniform grains are plenty for a dust disk, and if every particle weighs the same, permuting the buffer has zero effect on gravity. The mass array disappears completely, and the pyramid simply counts how many grains fall into each cell. The total mass of the dust stays fixed while the grains slider controls how finely that mass is sliced up.

With uniform masses, both structures run back to back over one ping-ponged particle buffer:

sort     → count, scan ×3, scatter                     // part three's hash sort: 5 dispatches
pyramid  → clear, bounds, scatter, resolve, reduce ×8  // part one's pyramid: 12 dispatches
force    → tree walk + 9 cells, integrate              // combined kernel: 1 dispatch

That works out to 18 compute dispatches per substep, running four substeps per frame. Scroll through the diagram below to see what the force kernel asks of each structure for a single grain in the ring:

Keeping the two structures separate lets each one work at its own natural scale. The gravity pyramid has a 256×256 base and refits its root bounding box to the whole cloud every substep so long-range pulls always cover everything. The hash grid keeps its cells fixed to a single grain diameter, matching the tiny range where two grains can actually touch. They meet only inside the final force shader, which walks the pyramid for gravity, checks nine hashed buckets for contacts, sums the two accelerations, and integrates. Because the force pass runs on the freshly sorted buffer, a grain's physical contact neighbors are also sitting right next to it in GPU memory.

The central star doesn't even live in the particle buffer; it's one analytic GM/r² term added inside the force kernel, much like the dome in part two was an analytic surface rather than extra geometry. There is also a gentle confining pull past a fixed outer radius so a single ejected grain can't fly off to infinity and stretch the pyramid's root box into uselessly coarse cells.

Aside · How research codes do it

Planetary scientists build their production codes on the exact same pairing. In 2000, Derek Richardson and colleagues adapted pkdgrav, a parallel cosmology tree code, to track around a million colliding planetesimals in 3D. Their integrator is far more accurate and their collision model handles bounces, mergers, and shattering, yet every timestep still boils down to a tree walk for gravity paired with a local neighbor search for contacts.

Step 4 · DissipationCollisions that bleed energy

Even with both data structures wired up, one physical ingredient is still doing the real heavy lifting. Watch the collapsing cloud again and compare all three physics modes, gravity only, contacts only, and both, along with the stickiness slider:

In gravity only mode, the cloud rebounds endlessly like before. In contacts only mode, it behaves like loose marbles in zero gravity: the initial spin flings the whole disk outward because nothing binds it together. Turn on both and the grains pile onto one another, shed their infall speed, and settle into a compact, slowly rotating planetesimal.

The piece that lets it settle is a single line inside the contact force. Just like part three's sand, each overlapping pair interacts through a spring–dashpot: a stiff spring pushes overlapping grains apart, and a dashpot (like a shock absorber moving through thick oil) damps their relative speed along the contact normal: 

acc += nrm * (dia - r) * P.stiffness;   // spring: push apart by the overlap
let vn = dot(vel - parts[k].zw, nrm);   // relative speed along the contact normal
acc -= nrm * vn * P.damping;            // dashpot: bleed off part of that speed

That damping term makes every bump inelastic, draining kinetic energy out of the collapse (in real dust, that energy radiates away as heat). The stickiness slider directly controls P.damping from 0 to 80, starting at 30. Because the dashpot opposes separation as well as approach, higher damping makes grains cling together once they meet. Drag stickiness all the way down to zero and the collisions become elastic: gravity plus perfectly bouncy spheres still conserves energy, turning the planetesimal back into a jittering swarm that refuses to pack down. Stars in a galaxy lack this energy sink, so galaxies stay puffy; colliding dust grains have it, which is why rocky worlds can condense at all.

Aside · Newton's pendulums and Safronov's monograph

Isaac Newton measured bounciness for the Principia by swinging pairs of pendulums made of different materials into each other and recording what fraction of their closing speed survived impact: around 5/9 for tightly wound wool, and around 15/16 for glass. Today that ratio is called the coefficient of restitution, and turning up stickiness turns it down.

The modern picture of planets assembling from dust—grains settling, colliding, sticking, and larger bodies sweeping up their neighborhoods—was worked out largely by Viktor Safronov in Moscow during the 1950s and 1960s. He published it as a Russian monograph in 1969, and NASA's 1972 English translation brought his theory to Western planetary science, where it became the foundation of planetesimal growth models.

Step 5 · The diskGravity against shear in an orbiting ring

A nearly stationary cloud is planet formation on easy mode, since every grain wants to fall toward the same point. Real planets grow inside a rotating disk where angular momentum fights collapse at every radius. Below is the hero simulation with all its controls exposed, including a grains slider that runs from 10,000 up to 150,000.

Inside an orbiting ring, shear works relentlessly against clumping. Grains closer to the star move faster than grains farther out, so any little overdensity gets stretched into a tilted arc as its inner edge races ahead of its outer edge. Watch the first few seconds after pressing re-seed: instead of condensing into neat round dots right away, the ring breaks into tight trailing spiral streaks where self-gravity pulls dust together while orbital shear smears it out. Whenever a streak grows dense enough, inelastic collisions cool its interior faster than shear can rip it apart, and a bound moonlet snaps together. Once formed, each moonlet clears material along its lane, scattering some grains and absorbing others.

Aside · Self-gravity wakes in Saturn's rings

Planetary scientists refer to this late stage, where a few dominant bodies sweep up smaller neighbors, as oligarchic growth, a term coined in Eiichiro Kokubo and Shigeru Ida's 1998 simulations. Their runs tracked up to 4,000 planetesimals using dedicated gravity hardware from the same Tokyo GRAPE project featured in part one. On a modern laptop GPU, the slider above handles 150,000.

Saturn's A ring performs a live version of this tug-of-war right in the solar system's backyard. Its ice particles keep trying to clump under self-gravity while orbital shear keeps pulling the clumps apart. The Cassini spacecraft spotted both sides of the balance: fleeting diagonal bands called self-gravity wakes, looking much like the streaks above, alongside intact moonlets roughly a hundred meters across that carve propeller-shaped wakes through the surrounding ring.

Plenty of real astrophysics is missing here. Real protoplanetary disks are 3D and filled with gas, which drags grains inward and herds them into clumps through the streaming instability. High-speed impacts can also shatter bodies instead of growing them. And to make clumping happen in seconds rather than millennia, the dust ring here is wildly massive compared to its star (in simulation units, total dust mass is 0.12 against a star of 0.066), cranking self-gravity way up. Even with those shortcuts, the core engine has the same anatomy as research accretion codes: a tree for the far field, a spatial hash for the near field, and dissipative contacts in between.

EpilogueHow two structures stay out of each other's way

The most surprising thing about building this page is how little the two halves had to know about each other. The gravity pyramid and the spatial hash never share a cell size, never share an indexing scheme, and never even communicate; one answers "what does everything far away add up to?" while the other answers "what is pressing against this grain right now?", and the force kernel simply sums the two vectors. Getting them to coexist cleanly came down to removing a conflict in the data itself: giving every grain the same mass made the sort's shuffling invisible to gravity, deleting an entire bookkeeping pass in the process. And on the physics side, all the machinery only produced solid clumps once a single damping line let collisions shed energy.

There are plenty of obvious next steps if you wanted to push this closer to a real planetary disk. Both the pyramid and the spatial hash generalize straight to 3D, just as part two turned the quadtree into an octree. Allowing grains to actually merge into heavier bodies would bring varying masses back, which means tracking a mass field through a key–value radix sort and compacting merged particles out of the buffer (both built on the prefix scan from part three). Adding gas drag is one extra force term, and real accretion over thousands of orbits calls for longer or multirate timesteps instead of squeezing millions of years into ten seconds.
