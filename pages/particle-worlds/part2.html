Particle Worlds · Part 2

Gravity on a Dome

Lifting the 2D gravity pyramid into three dimensions, rendering 200,000 glowing bodies without a depth sort, and bending gravity over a dome with two springs.

Scroll down to read ↓

Adding a third coordinate to part one's 2D gravity solver looks trivial in the equations: Newton's force law barely notices a z component. What actually blows up is memory. A flat 1024×1024 grid takes 16 MB of VRAM; extending that same resolution into a cube would demand 16 GB, blowing straight past what a browser tab can hold. Drawing overlapping glowing particles in 3D also introduces a classic rendering headache: transparent geometry normally requires sorting every particle back-to-front on every frame.

Solving those two problems lifts the pointer-free solver cleanly into open 3D space. Once it is running, turning open space into the live hemisphere overhead takes only a few lines of spring math. Those 20,000 bodies are attracting one another straight through 3D space at two steps per frame (with gravity halved and damping turned off so the streams keep circulating instead of collapsing into clumps). Drag anywhere on the dome to orbit, and pinch or hold ⌘/Ctrl while scrolling to zoom.

Step 1 · Into 3DQuadtree to octree

Structurally, turning the implicit quadtree into an octree changes almost nothing. Each parent cell splits into eight sub-cubes instead of four squares: 

// 2D: children of (level, ix, iy), four of them
(level+1, 2ix+dx, 2iy+dy)                  dx, dy ∈ {0, 1}

// 3D: children of (level, ix, iy, iz), eight of them
(level+1, 2ix+dx, 2iy+dy, 2iz+dz)          dx, dy, dz ∈ {0, 1}

Child indices are still pure arithmetic, every level still lives as a flat slice of one GPU buffer, and the Barnes–Hut opening test cellWidth² < θ² × distance² stays untouched because lengths work the same way in any dimension.

The real constraint is the cubic growth of the grid. Part one topped out at a 1024² finest level, around a million cells and 16 MB of accumulators. The 3D equivalent, 1024³, would mean a billion cells and roughly 16 GB of memory. To stay comfortable inside a browser tab, the octree tops out at seven levels: 128³, about two million cells and 34 MB. Below that ceiling, the solver picks its depth dynamically from the particle count via ceil(log₂(n) / 3) clamped to between 4 and 7, keeping roughly one finest-level voxel per body.

In practice, the demos on this page stay well below that cap. At the default 20,000 bodies the finest level is 32³, and pushing the slider to its maximum of 200,000 steps up to 64³. You would need more than 2¹⁸ = 262,144 bodies before the solver allocates a 128³ grid.

Stopping at a coarser bottom grid trades away some near-field sharpness, since any other bodies sharing a nearby finest-level voxel collapse into that voxel's center of mass. At 20,000 bodies, those bottom voxels start out roughly 0.06 units across, almost identical to our softening radius of 0.05, so the grid blurs close pulls by about the same amount softening already does. In a diffuse galaxy where individual flybys barely register, that trade-off is practically invisible; for tight planetary systems with close gravitational slingshots, you would want exact leaf lists at the bottom.

The bookkeeping that changes from 2D to 3D

Level offsets. All levels above level l contain 1 + 8 + 64 + … + 8l−1 nodes, which sums to (8l − 1)/7, compared with (4l − 1)/3 in 2D (both denominators follow 2d − 1). That also means the entire coarse pyramid above the finest grid adds only about one-seventh extra memory in 3D, down from one-third in 2D.

The spare word gets filled. Part one allocated four atomic words per cell so indexing stayed a power-of-two shift, leaving the fourth word idle after storing mass, Σm·dx, and Σm·dy. In 3D, Σm·dz drops straight into that fourth slot: 

atomicAdd(&grid[c],     u32(round(m * FP_SCALE)));  // Σ mass
atomicAdd(&grid[c + 1], …(m * frac.x * FP_SCALE));  // Σ m·dx
atomicAdd(&grid[c + 2], …(m * frac.y * FP_SCALE));  // Σ m·dy
atomicAdd(&grid[c + 3], …(m * frac.z * FP_SCALE));  // Σ m·dz, the word 2D left empty

Bounds and traversal stack. The bounds pass tracks six atomic extremes (min and max for x, y, and z) instead of four. Each stack entry packs into a single 32-bit integer as level(4) | iz(9) | iy(9) | ix(9). Because opening a voxel pushes up to eight children instead of four, the stack grows deeper; a fixed 64-entry local array safely covers the seven-level cap, and at 32³ the walk never uses more than around 36 entries.

Mass packs into position.w. GPU storage buffers align cleanly to 16 bytes (four floats), so storing a 3D position already left a fourth component sitting open. Putting each body's mass in pos.w eliminates the separate mass buffer altogether: every particle is one vec4 of position/mass and one vec4 of velocity, 32 bytes total, fitting 200,000 bodies into roughly 6 MB.

Step 2 · RenderingDrawing without sorting

Before testing the octree, we need a way to look at 3D particles cleanly. Normally, rendering translucent sprites in 3D forces you to sort them by depth on every frame, because standard alpha blending depends on draw order: painting a closer translucent glow before a farther one produces the wrong color. Sorting hundreds of thousands of moving points every 16 milliseconds would require a full GPU sorting pipeline just for drawing.

Additive blending sidesteps the sort completely. When particles emit light onto an empty background rather than occluding what sits behind them, overlapping glows simply add together, and addition is commutative (a + b = b + a). Draw order stops mattering, so we can drop both the depth sort and the depth buffer. For a field of stars, that shortcut also matches reality pretty well: stars shine, and across galactic distances they almost never eclipse one another.

Aside · Fifty years of draw order

Deciding which surfaces cover which has been a central puzzle in computer graphics since the beginning. Early 1970s renderers relied on the painter's algorithm, sorting polygons back-to-front before rasterizing them. In 1974, Ed Catmull and Wolfgang Straßer independently described the z-buffer, storing per-pixel depth so opaque triangles could draw in any order; modern GPUs dedicate specialized hardware to it. Depth buffers only resolve keep-or-discard tests, though, so translucent layers still needed sorting, and order-independent transparency remains an active research area. Luminous particles are one of the happy cases where plain additive light works out of the box.

The vertex geometry stays minimal as well. Every body renders as a camera-facing quad with zero vertex buffers: the vertex shader generates all six corner offsets in code and fetches each particle's position and velocity by instance_index directly from the compute buffers. No data gets copied between simulation and display. The fragment shader draws a soft radial falloff colored by speed, shifting from cool blue for slow drift to bright orange inside fast collapsing knots. Accumulating into a 16-bit floating-point render target lets dense cores climb cleanly past 1.0 before a final tone-mapping pass compresses the brightness onto your display.

Step 3 · The octreeRebuilding the octree every step

The 3D solver follows the exact same dispatch sequence as the 2D pyramid, rebuilding the octree from scratch on every tick as particles move. At the default 32³ resolution, a full step takes ten compute dispatches: 

clear     → zero the grid's atomic words, reset the bounds
bounds    → min/max over every body                 (the root cell)
scatter   → each body adds into its cell's 4 words  (fixed-point atomics)
resolve   → fixed point → center of mass and mass   (finest level)
reduce ×5 → each level from its 8 children          (×6 at 64³)
force     → stack walk, θ test, integrate           (in place)

At 20,000 bodies, the 32³ finest grid clears roughly 131,000 atomic words and runs five reduction passes up to the root; at 200,000 bodies, the 64³ grid clears about a million words and runs six reductions. Just like in 2D, the force pass reads all external mass through the octree rather than peeking at other threads' entries, so it integrates every particle in place and hands the buffer straight to the renderer.

How a body avoids pulling on itself

Because every particle scatters its own mass into the grid, every ancestor voxel from the root down to the body's own cell contains that body's mass. If the walk ever accepted one of those ancestor cells as a single point mass, the body would end up attracting itself slightly. That really can happen when a wide cell's center of mass happens to sit far enough off to one side to pass the θ check.

Two small guards keep self-gravity out. At every coarse level, the walk always subdivides the cell containing the current body instead of summarizing it. Once the walk reaches the finest level and hits the body's own voxel, it subtracts its own mass and position back out of the cell's weighted sum and pulls only toward any remaining neighbors (skipping the cell entirely if the body is alone inside it). 

// coarse levels: never summarize the cell this body sits in
let shift = FINEST - lvl;
let mine = ix == (myIx >> shift) && iy == (myIy >> shift) && iz == (myIz >> shift);
if (!mine && w * w < theta2 * r2) { /* accept as one point */ } else { /* push 8 children */ }

// finest level, own cell: take this body back out
m = n.w - myMass;
if (m <= 1e-9) { continue; }             // alone in the cell
com = (n.xyz * n.w - p * myMass) / m;

The octree demo starts at 20,000 bodies (with sliders reaching 200,000 bodies, θ from 0.4 to 1.4, and up to 8 steps per frame), seeded as a slightly puffed-up rotating disk with circular velocities balanced against interior mass plus mild vertical scatter. Give it a few seconds and watch the familiar cold-disk instability from part one play out in three dimensions: without a dark matter halo holding it steady, the disk breaks into hot orange clumps that orbit and stretch into a ring.

Step 4 · ConstraintsThe dome

Everything above takes place in open 3D space. To make particles flow over a curved hemisphere like weather patterns wrapping around a planet, you could rewrite the integrator in spherical coordinates or project velocities onto tangent planes. A much simpler trick adds two spring terms right inside the force kernel: 

if (P.shellK > 0.0) {
  let r = length(p);
  let rhat = p / max(r, 1e-6);
  acc = acc - rhat * (r - P.shellR) * P.shellK;  // radial spring: stay on the shell
  if (p.z < 0.0) {
    acc.z = acc.z - p.z * P.shellK * 4.0;        // floor spring: stay above the equator
  }
}

Whenever a body drifts inside the shell radius, the radial spring nudges it outward; whenever it bulges outside, the spring reels it back in. If it slips below the equator plane (p.z < 0), a second upward spring kicks in at four times the stiffness so the rim acts like a firm boundary while the dome shell stays supple. Because each restoring acceleration grows proportionally to the constraint violation, this is known as a penalty force. It slots directly beside gravity in the acceleration sum, leaving the octree and integrator completely untouched.

Aside · Penalty methods, 1943

Richard Courant introduced penalty methods in 1943 as a way to turn hard mathematical constraints into smooth energy costs that grow steeper the farther a solution strays from the allowed surface. Structural engineers, numerical optimizers, and real-time game physics engines have leaned on the idea ever since.

The demo below starts with 20,000 bodies spread across the hemisphere at dome strength 6, paired with gentle velocity damping (0.999 per step) so the shell settles instead of ringing. Pull dome strength down to zero and the springs switch off: free 3D gravity collapses the entire shell inward into one glowing ball within seconds. Crank the slider back up and the springs drag that condensed knot back out onto the dome, where it skids across the surface as a single lump rather than re-expanding. Hit re-seed whenever you want a fresh hemisphere.

Even while particles hug the dome, their gravitational attraction still cuts straight through 3D space along chords rather than curving across the surface. The radial spring absorbs the inward component of those pulls, leaving the tangential component free to accelerate streams and eddies across the shell.

Aside · Archimedes seeds the dome

Scattering particles uniformly across a hemisphere takes a small trick: sampling latitude and longitude uniformly crowds points tightly around the north pole. Archimedes handed us the fix roughly 2,200 years ago with his hat-box theorem, which shows that a sphere and its surrounding cylinder have equal surface area between any two horizontal slices. Picking a uniform height z in [0, R] together with a uniform azimuth angle around the circle lands uniformly on the hemisphere using just two random numbers and zero rejection sampling.

Why a spring and not a hard projection

Snapping positions directly back onto the sphere after every step via p = normalize(p) * shellR looks simpler at first glance, yet hard projection edits positions behind the integrator's back. You then have to strip radial velocity manually or watch bodies endlessly hammer against the projection, injecting or erasing energy outside the force balance. Keeping the constraint as a penalty spring lets damping and gravity interact with it naturally inside the same symplectic step.

Stiffness is the trade-off. Push a penalty spring too stiff relative to the time step and an explicit integrator overshoots its own oscillation and explodes, which is why the dome pairs moderate stiffness with light damping. When rigid walls really matter, solvers step up to Lagrange multipliers or Position Based Dynamics.

Notice also that length(p) − shellR is simply the signed distance to the sphere, and rhat is its gradient. Dropping in any other signed distance function, such as a torus or a terrain surface, immediately lets gravity flow over that shape with the exact same lines of shader code.

EpilogueWhat changes in 3D

Moving this solver into 3D mostly turned out to be a memory budget exercise: the algorithms transferred directly with 4s becoming 8s and squares becoming cubes, while capping the octree depth at 128³ kept VRAM sane and relied on softening to absorb the coarser bottom cells. Along the way, three practical GPU tricks carried a lot of weight: additive blending eliminated depth sorting because light addition ignores draw order, reading physics buffers straight by instance index saved a frame copy, and expressing the dome as a penalty force constrained the whole simulation without making the octree or integrator aware of geometry. Part three reuses that exact penalty-spring idea for the walls of a fluid tank.

For even tighter performance in 3D, sorting particles along a 3D Morton curve would help neighboring GPU threads walk similar octree branches together, and swapping the uniform octree for a parallel LBVH would keep fine detail when matter packs into tiny regions. Before optimizing gravity any further, though, it is time to change the physics itself: in part three, particles stop pulling from afar and start colliding up close, which flips the entire data-structure problem upside down.
