Particle Worlds · Part 5

Gravity in Frequency Space

589,824 matter samples condensing into a cosmic web on a 512×512 mesh, with gravity solved by a GPU Fourier transform. Sweep your cursor through it to stir.

Scroll down to read ↓

Zoom out far enough and an entire galaxy shrinks to a speck of dust. The 589,824 luminous dots above stand for chunks of dark and ordinary matter spread across a patch of the early universe. At the beginning of the loop, that patch is almost completely smooth, marked only by faint density ripples. Gravity amplifies those ripples into dense knots, stretches slender filaments between the knots, and empties out vast dark voids in between. Galaxy surveys map out this exact lacy network, known as the cosmic web, across hundreds of millions of light-years of real sky.

Simulating that with the earlier tools runs into trouble immediately. Part one's mass pyramid earns its speedup by lumping distant clusters together, and a nearly uniform early universe doesn't have clusters to lump yet. Part three's sorted grid only works when forces cut off at a short radius, whereas gravity reaches across the entire box. Cosmologists handle this with a third strategy altogether: deposit matter onto a regular mesh, transform it into sine waves with an FFT, and solve gravity in frequency space.

Step 1 · Periodic boundariesA universe with no edges

When you simulate an isolated galaxy, you can draw a box around it and leave empty space outside. The universe has no outside: wherever you stand, statistically the same soup of matter extends in every direction. Cosmological simulations capture that by making the simulation box periodic. Drift out through the right wall and you re-enter on the left; sit near one edge and you feel the pull of mass just across the opposite boundary. Where part four tore the walls off the grid, cosmology glues opposite walls together into a wraparound torus.

Wraparound space is deeply inconvenient for a tree solver. On a torus, every particle pulls on every other particle plus all of its infinite periodic copies, and summing that infinite lattice converges painfully slowly. Tree codes work around it using Ewald summation, a 1921 method borrowed from crystal physics, which adds real complexity and runtime cost. Even worse, when matter is almost uniform, enormous pulls from opposite sides nearly cancel out, leaving a tiny net force. A tree's approximation error is small compared to each individual pull, yet large compared to that delicate remainder.

Switching from pairs of bodies to a continuous field sidesteps both issues. Let mass density ρ be a scalar grid across the box and let φ be the gravitational potential on the same grid. They obey Poisson's equation: 

∇²φ = 4πG ρ        // density sets the curvature of the potential

Here ∇² is the Laplacian, the sum of second derivatives along each axis. In 2D it adds east–west curvature to north–south curvature, measuring how far the value at a grid cell dips below or bulges above the average of its neighbors.

Once you know φ, gravity is just the downhill slope, F = −∇φ. Every particle reads its acceleration straight off a field that already accounts for every bit of mass in the box and all of its periodic images. The hard part is inverting ∇² to obtain φ from ρ. On a 512×512 mesh that means solving 262,144 coupled linear equations, with every cell linked to its neighbors, twice every frame. That looks daunting in position space, yet after a Fourier transform the solve shrinks to a single division per cell.

Step 2 · FourierAny shape as a sum of waves

Fourier's theorem says any periodic signal over a box, no matter how jagged, can be built by stacking sine and cosine waves of different frequencies. You can test that below on 256 samples, adding frequencies one at a time from lowest to highest. A smooth Gaussian bump snaps into shape after just a handful of waves. A sharp square pulse demands far more, and even with dozens of waves, crisp corners keep a stubborn little ripple right beside each jump. Random noise looks hopeless at first, then locks onto the target cleanly as the slider approaches the top: 256 spatial samples and 128 frequency bins (each carrying amplitude and phase, plus the mean and Nyquist mode) hold the exact same 256 numbers, so nothing gets lost in translation.

Aside · The memoir Lagrange blocked

Joseph Fourier had sailed to Egypt with Napoleon as secretary of the Institut d'Égypte, and by 1807 he was serving as prefect of the Isère department in Grenoble while studying heat conduction in his spare time. That year he submitted a memoir on heat flow built around trigonometric series to the Institut de France. The review committee, which included Joseph-Louis Lagrange, refused to publish it. Lagrange couldn't believe a sum of infinitely smooth sine waves could ever represent an arbitrary function with abrupt jumps.

Fourier was right in general, though jumps do hide a quirk. As you add more waves, the sum converges everywhere away from the step, yet right beside the jump it always overshoots by roughly 9% of the step height, the Gibbs phenomenon visible at the corners of the square wave above. Fourier's full work finally appeared in 1822 as Théorie analytique de la chaleur.

The practical takeaway for solving gravity is that a grid of densities and its list of wave amplitudes (its spectrum) are two interchangeable views of the exact same data. The Fourier transform translates between them, and some differential equations that are hopelessly tangled on the grid fall completely apart on the spectrum side.

Step 3 · Frequency spaceTurning Poisson's equation into division

Take one pure density wave, ρ(x) = sin(kx). Differentiate it once and you get k cos(kx); differentiate it a second time and you land right back on −k² sin(kx), the exact same wave multiplied by −k². Because sine waves keep their shape under the Laplacian, solving Poisson's equation for a single wave means dividing its amplitude by −k². And because the equation is linear, the solver can decompose any lumpy density into waves, divide each wave independently by its own −k², and add the pieces back together. Those 262,144 coupled grid equations collapse into 262,144 independent divisions.

Scroll through the stages on a 1D slice to see each frequency flip sign and shrink:

In pseudocode, the whole Poisson solver fits in five lines:

spectrum = fft2d(density);            // move to frequency space
for (each wave k) {
  spectrum[k] /= -dot(k, k);          // solve Poisson's equation per mode
}
potential = inverse_fft2d(spectrum);  // return to the mesh

Dividing by k² crushes short-wavelength ripples far harder than long ones, which is why gravitational potentials always come out much smoother than the clumpy mass distributions that generate them. Periodic wraparound boundaries appear automatically too, because every Fourier mode already repeats cleanly across the box. (The actual GPU shader also multiplies each mode by a gentle Gaussian in k to damp jagged grid-scale noise.)

The k = 0 mode, and why only departures from the mean pull

One wave needs special treatment: k = 0 holds the average density of the whole box, where dividing by k² = 0 would explode. The shader zeroes that mode out, which is equivalent to subtracting the mean density. Inside a periodic box, a completely uniform background pulls equally in every direction and cancels out, leaving only overdensities and underdensities to exert net forces.

James Jeans made that exact same move in 1902 while studying how a uniform gas cloud fragments under gravity. In a static, infinite universe dropping the background term is mathematically shaky, and Binney and Tremaine famously nicknamed it the "Jeans swindle." In an expanding universe it turns out to be exact: the uniform mean density drives the expansion of space itself, while the equations in expanding coordinates respond strictly to departures from the mean. Strictly speaking, the non-expanding demos on this page are happily riding the swindle.

Across this series, every speedup has come from changing how the data is organized before doing the math. Part one built a tree to summarize distant clusters; part three sorted particles so physical neighbors sat beside each other in memory; here the Fourier transform rewrites density in a basis where the Laplacian acts one mode at a time, which mathematicians call diagonalizing the operator.

Step 4 · The FFTFrom n² down to n log n

All of that magic only pays off if fft2d itself is fast. Computing a discrete Fourier transform straight from its definition sums over all n inputs for each of the n outputs, landing right back at O(n²). Doing a 512×512 transform by brute force would mean 262,144² complex multiply-adds (nearly 69 billion operations for a single pass), and the cosmology demo needs forward and inverse transforms twice per frame.

The fast Fourier transform breaks that bottleneck by divide-and-conquer. A transform of length n splits into two half-size transforms, one on the even-indexed samples and one on the odd-indexed samples, stitched back together with a single complex multiply per output. Repeating that split recursively drops the work to O(n log n). Below, the top diagram traces the resulting butterfly network for 16 points: log₂ 16 = 4 layers where every value pairs with a partner 8 slots away, then 4, then 2, then 1. It shares the exact layered fan-in shape of part three's prefix scan, replacing additions with complex rotations, so it maps onto parallel GPU workgroups just as cleanly.

Underneath the network, two log-scaled bars race a direct O(n²) DFT against an FFT right on your CPU as you slide points from 256 up to 1,048,576. Above 2,048 points the direct transform gets too slow to wait for, so the benchmark times it at 2,048 and scales by n².

Aside · Gauss beat everyone by 160 years

Around 1805, while computing the orbits of the newly discovered asteroids Pallas and Juno from astronomical observations, Carl Friedrich Gauss worked out the same even–odd splitting trick to speed up his trigonometric sums. He wrote the method down in Latin and never published it; it only appeared in 1866 in his posthumous collected works, where it sat unnoticed until Michael Heideman, Don Johnson, and Sidney Burrus uncovered the history in 1984.

Long before that rediscovery, James Cooley and John Tukey published the algorithm independently in 1965. Part of the motivation came from physicist Richard Garwin, who needed fast spectral analysis to spot Soviet underground nuclear tests in seismometer traces and nudged Cooley to implement Tukey's idea. Within a few years, the FFT reshaped everything from radar and audio compression to MRI scans.

Inside the GPU FFT kernel

The WebGPU shader implements a Stockham radix-2 FFT. Each workgroup loads one 512-element row into shared memory, where 256 threads execute one butterfly pair each across log₂ 512 = 9 stages separated by workgroup barriers, then writes the row back out. Stockham indexing keeps elements sorted after every stage, avoiding a separate bit-reversal permutation pass.

A 2D transform runs once across all rows and once down all columns, so a forward and inverse 2D FFT take four compute passes total (the "4 FFTs" listed in the demo readouts).

Step 5 · Particle-meshBridging particles and the mesh

The Poisson solver speaks in grid cells, while the simulated universe is made of moving particles. Connecting the two gives the classic particle-mesh (PM) loop: 

deposit:   spread each particle's mass onto the mesh    // particles → field
solve:     FFT → divide by −k² → inverse FFT            // density → potential
gradient:  difference neighboring cells                 // potential → force grid
gather:    interpolate the force back to each particle  // field → particles
kick and drift, wrap through periodic walls, repeat

The subtle part is how particles talk to the mesh during deposit and gather. The simplest rule, nearest grid point (NGP), dumps a particle's entire mass into whichever cell center lies closest. Drag a particle across a cell boundary under NGP and all of its mass teleports abruptly into the next cell, creating sudden force jerks. The standard upgrade is cloud-in-cell (CIC): treat each particle as a 1×1 cell-sized square centered on its position, and split its mass across the four overlapping grid cells in proportion to the overlap areas. As the particle glides smoothly across a border, its mass transfers smoothly from one cell to the next.

Gathering forces back onto the particles has to use those exact same four bilinear weights. Mixing one deposit scheme with a different gather scheme makes a particle exert a spurious net force on itself, breaking momentum conservation. Drag the four orange particles below and switch between CIC — split over 4 cells and NGP — nearest cell only to watch how the dashed 1×1 cloud distributes mass across borders:

Deposit, FFT, divide, inverse FFT, gather: no tree walks and no sorting. Below, that exact solver runs on a cold uniform blob of 65,536 particles starting at rest inside a periodic box (drawn tiled 3×3 so you can see the periodic neighbors collapsing in lockstep, resetting every 14 seconds). Moving the mesh slider between 64×64 and 512×512 leaves the particles untouched and changes only the grid solving gravity. On a 64×64 mesh the gravitational field is blurry and the collapse stays smooth; push it up to 512×512 and the exact same cloud shatters into crisp knots and fine filaments.

That slider lays bare the core trade-off of particle-mesh gravity: anything smaller than a single grid cell gets blurred out. The mesh spacing itself acts as the softening length. For simulating close binaries in a galaxy, that would be a dealbreaker, which is why part one invested in an adaptive tree that puts fine resolution wherever stars clump. For large-scale cosmology, where filaments and voids span the whole volume, giving every part of the box equal resolution in O(M log M) time is a great bargain.

Step 6 · Initial conditionsPrimordial seeds and expanding space

With the PM gravity solver running, turning it into a miniature cosmos takes two more ingredients.

Primordial ripples. Measurements of the cosmic microwave background by COBE, WMAP, and Planck reveal an infant universe smooth to roughly one part in 100,000. Those tiny fluctuations are random in phase, yet follow a specific statistical recipe, the power spectrum, that dictates how much variance sits at each wavelength. Generating matching initial conditions takes just three CPU FFTs at seed time: fill a grid with Gaussian white noise, FFT it, shape every Fourier mode to match the desired power spectrum, divide by k² to turn density ripples into a displacement field, and transform back. Rather than tossing particles down randomly (which would inject unwanted shot noise), the setup starts them on a clean regular lattice and nudge every particle along that displacement field with a matching initial momentum.

Aside · Zel'dovich's pancakes, 1970

This lattice-displacement recipe is Yakov Zel'dovich's 1970 approximation. While density fluctuations remain small, every wavelength grows at the same rate, so particles coast along straight trajectories determined by the initial gravitational field. A single push along that field captures the early growth accurately, and Zel'dovich used it to predict that collapsing regions would first flatten along one axis into thin sheets he called "pancakes."

Expanding space. In an expanding universe, gravity has to pull matter together while space stretches underneath it. Rather than growing the mesh every step, cosmologists switch to comoving coordinates that stretch right along with the expansion, keeping the simulation box fixed at size 1 while physical distances scale by the cosmic scale factor a(t). All of that expansion math collapses into a few powers of a multiplying the kick and drift steps, leaving the deposit, FFT, and gather shaders completely untouched.

The simulation clock advances in equal steps of ln a from a = 0.02 (redshift z = 49, when the universe was 2% of its present size) up to a = 1 (today). Because the run starts long after the microwave background was emitted, the initial ripples have already had time to grow beyond one part in 100,000, though they're still gentle. Around 1,000 substeps bring the box to today in roughly eight seconds at 60 fps, using a simplified matter-only (Einstein–de Sitter) cosmology without dark energy. Once a hits 1, the clock pauses while the web keeps churning and responding to your cursor.

Explore the parameters below. Clicking expansion: on / expansion: off shows the tug-of-war between gravity and Hubble expansion: turn expansion off and gravity goes unchecked, collapsing the box rapidly into a handful of big blobs; leave it on and space stretches fast enough that gravity can only gather matter along the ridges favored by the initial seeds, sculpting knots, filaments, and empty voids.

The ripple strength slider acts like cosmologists' σ₈, controlling the starting amplitude of the perturbations: lower it and structure emerges later and more delicately. The tilt n slider tunes the balance between long and short waves (P(k) ∝ kⁿ): negative values favor a few sweeping filaments, while positive values break the box into a fine foam of tiny clumps. Planck pinned down both numbers for the real universe (σ₈ ≈ 0.81 and primordial spectral tilt nₛ ≈ 0.965), two of the six parameters in the standard ΛCDM cosmological model. The particles slider steps from 65,536 up to 1,048,576, and big bang again draws a fresh random seed.

One important caveat: this universe lives in 2D, where Poisson's equation gives a 1/r pairwise force instead of 1/r². The comoving kick and drift coefficients are calibrated so small perturbations grow at the exact 3D Einstein–de Sitter linear rate, though once filaments go nonlinear the detailed collapse shapes diverge from 3D. Even so, the computational skeleton is the real thing. In the mid-1980s, Marc Davis, George Efstathiou, Carlos Frenk, and Simon White used 3D particle-mesh codes (upgraded with direct short-range pairs, a hybrid called P³M) with just 32,768 particles to demonstrate that cold dark matter, seeded with ripples like these, reproduces the observed clustering of galaxies.

EpilogueThree ways around n²

Over five parts, the series has knocked down the same O(n²) wall in three completely different ways. Part one's mass pyramid summarized space hierarchically so distant clusters could be read in O(log n) steps, putting resolution wherever matter piled up. Part three's counting sort rearranged memory so physically touching particles sat next to each other in RAM, answering neighbor queries in constant time after a prefix scan. And here, the FFT changed the representation itself, moving Poisson's equation into frequency space where every wave decouples into a single division at an O(M log M) cost.

Large production cosmology codes combine two of those ideas in one solver. TreePM, used in Volker Springel's GADGET-2 code to run the ten-billion-particle Millennium Simulation, splits gravity at a cutoff of a few grid cells: a periodic FFT mesh computes the long-range field cheaply across the whole box, while a local Barnes–Hut tree sharpens short-range pulls inside dense halos. Just as part four paired the tree with the hash sort, TreePM pairs the tree with the mesh. Pushing this page closer to that world would mean moving to 3D, nesting finer meshes inside collapsing halos (adaptive mesh refinement), and feeding in a measured ΛCDM power spectrum in place of the tilt slider.
