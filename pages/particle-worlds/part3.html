Particle Worlds · Part 3

Sorting Water

Switching from long-range gravity to collisions and fluids turns neighbor search on a GPU into a parallel sorting problem. Stir the 45,000 water particles above with your cursor.

Scroll down to read ↓

Drop a few thousand grains of sand into a box and check every pair to see if they collide, and more than 99% of your distance checks come back empty. Unlike the galaxies in part one and part two, where distant stars still add up to real pull, short-range forces drop cleanly to zero beyond a tiny cutoff radius. Sand grains, bouncing marbles, molecular bonds, and the 45,000 water particles sloshing in the tank above all share that rule: every parcel only pushes and pulls on its immediate neighbors, and tens of thousands of local pushes build up into pressure and waves.

Evaluating the actual contact or fluid force takes only a few lines of math once you know who sits beside you. Discovering those neighbors quickly on a GPU is the tricky part, and solving it without linked lists or locks leads straight to a parallel counting sort built on a prefix sum.

Step 1 · ContactsMostly checking for nothing

Begin with a box of rigid disks falling under downward gravity. Whenever two disks overlap, a stiff spring with light damping pushes them apart; whenever they are farther than one diameter apart, they ignore each other completely. Written the naive way, the loop looks almost identical to part one's all-pairs gravity code: 

for (let i = 0; i < count; i++) {
  for (let j = i + 1; j < count; j++) {
    if (distance(i, j) < 2 * radius) {   // almost always false
      applyContactForce(i, j);
    }
  }
}

Watch the readout under the simulation below. At the default 2,000 disks, JavaScript runs 1,999,000 distance checks every step, burning several milliseconds per frame. While the grains are raining down only about a thousand pairs are actually in contact, climbing to several thousand once the pile settles onto the floor. Either way, well under 1% of those two million checks produce any force at all.

Dragging your cursor through the pile stirs the grains near the pointer, still paying for two million pair checks underneath. A Barnes–Hut tree offers no help here either: summarizing distant regions is pointless when distant particles contribute exactly zero. The job calls for a spatial structure that answers "who sits right next to this particle?" and skips the rest of the box completely.

Step 2 · BucketsA uniform grid

A uniform grid handles local lookups cleanly. Divide the box into square cells at least as wide as the interaction diameter, and drop every particle into the cell containing its center. Any disk close enough to overlap particle i must live either in i's own cell or in one of the eight surrounding cells. Checking neighbors shrinks to inspecting a 3×3 block of nine cells, bringing total work down to O(n) as long as particles stay reasonably spread out.

Aside · An old idea with several names

Computational physicists arrived at local neighbor lists early on. In 1967, Loup Verlet simulated 864 liquid argon atoms in Physical Review by caching each atom's nearby neighbors across several steps, and grid-based cell lists became standard in molecular dynamics during the 1970s. Graphics and game programmers usually call the same structure a uniform grid, or spatial hashing when cell coordinates get hashed into a fixed table.

The figure below drops 3,500 disks into a 24×24 grid and runs the 3×3 lookup live wherever you hover (or at a parked spot near the bottom of the pile when your pointer is away). The gold outline marks the nine cells searched, and the highlighted yellow disks are the only candidates tested. Depending on how tightly packed that spot is, each query inspects roughly 70 to a few hundred particles instead of all 3,500, and the readout tracks both that local window and the full-step candidate pair count against the 6.1 million checks an all-pairs loop would burn through.

On a CPU, the first version you would write maps each particle to an integer cell ID, pushes its index into that cell's bucket array, and loops over the surrounding nine buckets: 

const buckets = Array.from({ length: grid * grid }, () => []);

for (let i = 0; i < particles.length; i++) {
  buckets[cellOf(particles[i].position)].push(i);
}

function testNeighbors(i) {
  const [cellX, cellY] = cellCoord(particles[i].position);
  for (let offsetY = -1; offsetY <= 1; offsetY++) {
    for (let offsetX = -1; offsetX <= 1; offsetX++) {
      const cell = cellAt(cellX + offsetX, cellY + offsetY);
      if (cell === undefined) continue;           // outside the box
      for (const j of buckets[cell]) {
        if (j > i) testPair(i, j);                // each pair once
      }
    }
  }
}

(The live demo above already uses the flat sorted array from the next section under the hood, though the bucket version captures the logic clearly.)

The 3×3 lookup itself translates straight to a compute shader. What breaks on a GPU is that tiny call to push. Dynamically appending to per-cell lists implies variable-length memory allocation, pointer chasing, and a write head that depends on who appended just ahead of you. Running thousands of GPU threads into shared lists serializes them right back down. Just as part one replaced pointer quadtrees with index arithmetic, a sort can replace per-cell lists on the GPU.

Step 3 · SortingCounting sort and the prefix sum

All the 3×3 neighbor loop really asks for is that every cell's particles sit together in memory alongside the index where each cell's slice begins. Sorting the particle array by cell ID delivers exactly that: every cell becomes one contiguous run inside a single flat buffer, with zero pointers.

Because cell IDs are small integers, they can be sorted in linear time without comparing any two particles against each other using counting sort. First, tally a histogram of how many particles fell into each cell. Second, compute the running sum of counts ahead of every cell so each cell knows where its slice starts. Third, drop every particle directly into its cell's reserved slice. 

counts[cell]++                       // 1. histogram    (one pass)
starts[c] = counts[0..c-1] summed    // 2. prefix sum   (the hard part)
slot = starts[cell] + claimed++      // 3. scatter      (one pass)

Written sequentially in JavaScript, the whole sort fits in a dozen lines. The WebGPU version keeps the exact same arrays and three stages, turning each loop into a parallel compute dispatch and replacing the two increments with hardware atomics: 

const counts = new Uint32Array(cellCount);
for (const p of particles) counts[cellOf(p)]++;          // histogram

const starts = new Uint32Array(cellCount);
for (let c = 1; c < cellCount; c++) {
  starts[c] = starts[c - 1] + counts[c - 1];             // exclusive scan
}

const cursor = starts.slice();
const sorted = new Array(particles.length);
for (const p of particles) {
  const cell = cellOf(p);
  sorted[cursor[cell]++] = p;                             // scatter
}

Stage two, converting per-cell counts into running totals, looks stubbornly serial at first glance. Walking left to right with a single accumulator suggests you cannot know cell 100's offset until cell 99 finishes.

In 1986, Daniel Hillis and Guy Steele showed how to run that prefix sum in parallel by doubling the reach on each round. In round one, every cell adds the value 1 slot to its left simultaneously, so every box holds a two-cell sum. In round two, every cell adds the value 2 slots to its left (which already sums two cells), giving four-cell totals. Round three reaches 4 slots left to cover eight cells. After log₂ n rounds (ten rounds for a thousand cells), every entry holds the inclusive sum of all cells up to itself, with every box updating together inside each round.

Shifting those inclusive totals one slot to the right and placing a zero at index 0 yields the exclusive start index for every cell's slice.

Aside · Census machines and the Connection Machine

Sorting by buckets predates electronic computers by decades. After the 1880 US census took nearly a decade to tabulate by hand, Herman Hollerith built electromechanical tabulators for the 1890 census that counted punched cards directly into category bins. Mechanical card sorters soon followed, dropping cards into pockets one digit column at a time from least significant digit up: physical radix sort. Hollerith's company later merged into what became IBM, and Harold Seward formalized both counting sort and radix sort for digital computers in his 1954 MIT master's thesis.

Parallel prefix sum grew out of hardware history too. Hillis and Steele wrote their algorithm for the Connection Machine, a massively parallel supercomputer with 65,536 one-bit processors (coincidentally the exact number of cells in the 256×256 GPU grid below). Peter Kogge and Harold Stone had already introduced the same doubling tree in 1973 for solving recurrences and designing fast carry-lookahead adders.

Parallel prefix sum (usually called a scan) reaches far past sorting. Guy Blelloch championed it as a core parallel primitive, showing how radix sort, stream compaction, and parallel memory allocation all reduce to scans. Anytime a loop carries a running total forward with an associative operator, a scan can collapse an O(n) chain into O(log n) parallel rounds.

The scan as three GPU dispatches

Scanning all 65,536 cells of the 256×256 grid breaks cleanly into three WebGPU dispatches. First, 256 workgroups of 256 threads each run an in-shared-memory scan over their own 256-cell block and write out their block totals. Second, a single workgroup scans those 256 block totals. Third, every cell adds its block's scanned offset back onto its local total. 

pass.setPipeline(this.pipes.scan_blocks);  pass.dispatchWorkgroups(BLOCKS);  // BLOCKS = 256
pass.setPipeline(this.pipes.scan_sums);    pass.dispatchWorkgroups(1);
pass.setPipeline(this.pipes.scan_add);     pass.dispatchWorkgroups(BLOCKS);

Inside the first two passes, each workgroup runs Hillis–Steele by ping-ponging between two shared-memory arrays so every round reads strictly from the completed output of the previous round: 

var<workgroup> sa: array<u32, 256>;
var<workgroup> sb: array<u32, 256>;

// inclusive scan of the 256 values staged in sa
fn scanShared(lid: u32) -> u32 {
  var fromA = true;
  var d = 1u;
  loop {
    if (d >= 256u) { break; }
    if (fromA) {
      var v = sa[lid];
      if (lid >= d) { v += sa[lid - d]; }
      sb[lid] = v;
    } else {
      var v = sb[lid];
      if (lid >= d) { v += sb[lid - d]; }
      sa[lid] = v;
    }
    workgroupBarrier();
    fromA = !fromA;
    d = d << 1u;
  }
  return sa[lid];  // 8 rounds, so the result lands back in sa
}

Subtracting each cell's own count from its inclusive total converts it into an exclusive start offset without needing a separate shift pass. That final pass also copies starts into a second cursor buffer that the scatter pass increments atomically as ticket numbers.

Step 4 · AssemblyThe whole sort on the GPU

Because particles move continuously, the entire counting sort runs fresh on every substep right ahead of the force calculation: 

clear    → zero the cell counters
count    → every particle atomically bumps its cell    (histogram)
scan     → starts[] from counts[]                      (3 dispatches)
scatter  → every particle copies itself to its slot    (one atomicAdd each)
forces   → every particle reads its 9 cells, pushes, integrates  (two passes for water)

Both the histogram and scatter shaders fit in a handful of lines of WGSL. Atomics guarantee unique slots within each cell without needing locks or caring about order inside a single cell: 

@compute @workgroup_size(256)
fn count(@builtin(global_invocation_id) gid: vec3u) {
  if (gid.x >= GP.count) { return; }
  atomicAdd(&counts[cellOf(partsIn[gid.x].xy)], 1u);
}

@compute @workgroup_size(256)
fn scatter(@builtin(global_invocation_id) gid: vec3u) {
  if (gid.x >= GP.count) { return; }
  let p = partsIn[gid.x];
  let slot = atomicAdd(&cursor[cellOf(p.xy)], 1u);
  sorted[slot] = p;
}

Notice that scatter copies each particle's full state vector directly into sorted rather than scattering bare indices. That extra write pays off immediately during the force pass: particles sitting close in 2D space now sit right next to one another in VRAM, turning neighbor loops into tight sequential cache reads. Each substep sorts from buffer A into buffer B, runs physics from B back into A, and repeats with zero CPU intervention.

Losing the box: spatial hashing

A dense 256×256 grid works well inside a bounded tank, even when many cells stay empty. To let particles roam over open space without walls, swap the flat cy * GRID + cx cell index for a spatial hash of (cx, cy) (multiply each coordinate by a large prime, XOR them, and mask to a fixed table size). The exact same histogram, scan, and scatter pipeline then runs over an unbounded domain using a fixed-size table.

Occasionally two distant cells hash into the same bucket, and the distance check inside the force pass harmlessly rejects those stray particles. Matthias Teschner and colleagues popularized this scheme for real-time deformable collisions in 2003, and part four puts it to work once particles leave the box.

Below is the full GPU counting-sort pipeline driving dry sand: 12,000 grains by default (scalable from 2,000 up to 30,000), re-sorted into 65,536 cells on every one of the five substeps per frame. Each grain has a diameter of one grid cell, so every possible collision sits inside its 3×3 neighborhood. Drag your cursor through the pile to plow trenches or hit re-pour to drop the sand again.

Push the particles slider up and notice how evenly performance holds. Because grain size stays constant, adding particles simply deepens the pile; counting, scattering, and force evaluation each scale linearly with particle count, while the three-pass scan always runs over the same 65,536 cells regardless of particle count.

Step 5 · WaterSmoothed particles

Dry sand grains only push when their hard rims touch. Water behaves differently: it resists compression throughout the entire volume, even deep inside a calm pool. To get fluid waves and splashing out of the same sorted grid, the force kernel switches to smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH). Instead of treating each particle as a tiny billiard ball, SPH treats it as a soft, overlapping kernel of fluid of radius h. Summing the kernel weights of nearby neighbors gives the local density at every particle; regions packed above rest density push outward, while sparse regions draw inward. Pressure turns out to be one more short-range pairwise force, dropping straight into the existing 3×3 cell search. 

// pass 1, density: how crowded is each particle?
ρ_i = Σ over neighbors j:  (1 − r/h)²

// pass 2, pressure force: push from crowded toward roomy
P_i  = k · (ρ_i − ρ_rest)
acc -= r̂ · (P_i + P_j)/2 · (1 − r/h)

Setting the smoothing radius h equal to one grid cell guarantees every contributing neighbor sits inside the 3×3 block. Thanks to the counting sort, iterating over neighbors is just nine compact integer loops: 

for (var oy = -1; oy <= 1; oy++) {
  for (var ox = -1; ox <= 1; ox++) {
    let c = cc + vec2i(ox, oy);
    if (c.x < 0 || c.y < 0 || c.x >= i32(SP.grid) || c.y >= i32(SP.grid)) { continue; }
    let ci = u32(c.y) * SP.grid + u32(c.x);
    let s = cellStart[ci];               // where this cell's run begins
    let n = cellCount[ci];               // and how long it is
    for (var k = s; k < s + n; k++) {
      let d = parts[k].xy - pi;
      let r2 = dot(d, d);
      if (r2 < h2) {
        let q = sqrt(r2) / h;
        let w = 1.0 - q;
        rho += w * w;
        rhoNear += w * w * w;            // for near pressure, see below
      }
    }
  }
}
Aside · Invented for stars

SPH began in astrophysics, when Leon Lucy and independently Robert Gingold and Joseph Monaghan published it in 1977 to model collapsing stars and gas clouds without fixed computational meshes. Monaghan adapted the method to free-surface water in 1994, and Matthias Müller, David Charypar, and Markus Gross brought interactive SPH fluids to real-time computer graphics in 2003. High-budget visual effects water today often uses hybrid particle-grid methods like FLIP, which still spend every frame binning particles back into a spatial grid.

What keeps the water from clumping

Two extra terms keep the fluid smooth and lively. First, a strictly repulsive short-range "near pressure" term (from Simon Clavet, Philippe Beaudoin, and Pierre Poulin's 2005 paper on viscoelastic fluids) stops surface tension artifacts from beading particles into unnatural clumps and strands. Second, a small dose of Monaghan's XSPH velocity averaging blends each particle slightly toward its neighbors' velocities, giving the water gentle physical viscosity.

Because every pairwise pressure push acts along the line joining two particles with equal and opposite magnitude, Newton's third law holds automatically and pressure forces conserve net momentum. The walls, floor, and ceiling of the tank are stiff penalty springs, using the exact same formulation as part two's dome.

The tank opens with a classic dam-break column of 50,000 water particles (adjustable from 10,000 to 80,000) crashing across the floor. Five times per frame, the GPU sorts every particle into 65,536 cells, evaluates local densities, and integrates pressure and viscosity forces. Because particle radius stays fixed, sliding particles up or down adds or removes volume of water rather than changing grid resolution.

EpilogueWhat carries over

Looking at the water pipeline as a whole reveals something striking about GPU simulation: five of the seven compute dispatches (count, three scan passes, and scatter) exist purely to rearrange data in memory, leaving only two passes for the actual fluid physics. On a parallel processor, replacing dynamic pointer lists with a flat sorted permutation gives you cache-friendly memory access, and parallel prefix sum is the workhorse that makes that rearrangement possible without serial bottlenecks.

Notice too how sharply the physics shaped the data structures across these first three parts. Gravity demanded a multi-scale pyramid because distant clusters still pull in aggregate, whereas sand and water demanded flat local buckets because anything past radius h drops to zero. Extending this grid to 3D folds one more coordinate into the cell index, and switching to multi-pass radix sort handles arbitrarily large keys. Even more interesting is running both structures over the same set of particles at once: part one's mass pyramid for long-range gravity combined with this part's grid for short-range collisions, which is how part four builds a disk of dust that accretes into moons.
