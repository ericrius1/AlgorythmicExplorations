Acoustic Spaces · Part 1

Rooms That Play Back

Every sound you hear indoors arrives buried in thousands of reflections from the walls, floor, and ceiling. Tap the plan above to clap in an empty hall and hear it from where the head is standing.

Series overview · Acoustic Spaces Acoustic Spaces treats architecture itself as an active acoustic system rather than a passive container for sound. Across two interactive essays, this series first builds a live physics-based room simulator here—modeling mirror-image reflections, octave-band wall absorption, Sabine reverberation, standing bass modes, binaural hearing, and recursive room resonance—and then, in Part 2, turns a room's own echo delays and dimensions into a playable instrument whose walls write the rhythm.

Living Music followed a note up to the moment it leaves a string or a pipe. The moment it enters the air, the room takes over. Before a clap finishes reaching your ears directly, copies of it are already bouncing off the walls, the floor, and the ceiling, each one arriving slightly later, slightly quieter, and with a little less treble than the last. Within a fraction of a second those copies number in the thousands, and your brain blends them together into an immediate sense of how big the space is and what its walls are made of.

Every figure on this page works from the same core model: a rectangular box with six surfaces, each assigned real-world absorption measurements across six octave bands from 125 Hz to 4 kHz. From those dimensions and materials, the code computes an impulse response (the room's complete acoustic reaction to a single sharp click) and passes live audio through it by convolution. Up top is an empty 12 × 7 × 4 meter hall with brick, window glass, wood panels, and plaster. The glowing ripples come from a separate 2D wave solver slowed down 65 times so your eyes can follow the wavefronts; as a ripple passes the listener, the direct path and the four first wall reflections light up in turn. The audio you hear runs at full speed through the room's binaural impulse response. Try dragging the head to a different corner and clapping again.

Step 1 · ReflectionsMirror rooms

When a sound wave hits a smooth, flat wall, it bounces at the same angle it came in, just like light off a mirror. For a listener standing in the room, that reflection appears to originate from a virtual copy of the sound source tucked behind the wall, at the exact same distance behind it as the real source sits in front. Reflect that virtual room across a second wall and you get a double bounce. Repeat the reflection across every wall and the floor plan tiles the plane with mirrored rooms, each holding one image source that corresponds to a single unique bounce path back to the listener.

Turning an image source into audio only takes two pieces of geometry: its straight-line distance to the listener, and the tally of how many times the path struck each wall. Distance sets the arrival delay (at 343 meters per second) and the spherical spreading loss, where pressure drops as one over distance. Each wall hit scales the pressure in each octave band by √(1 − α), where α is the fraction of energy that surface absorbs. Jont Allen and David Berkley laid out this rectangular-room method in 1979, and it remains the standard foundation for room simulators. Here is the band-gain loop straight from room.ts: 

// beta = √(1 − α) per surface and band; hits = bounces off each of the six surfaces
const gain = BANDS.map((_, b) => {
  let g = 1 / dist;
  for (let i = 0; i < 6; i++) if (hits[i]) g *= Math.pow(beta[i][b], hits[i]);
  return g * Math.exp(-0.5 * AIR_M[b] * dist); // plus a little air absorption
});

Below is a 6 × 4 meter room viewed from above, with floor and ceiling treated as completely absorbing so the geometry stays in two dimensions. The real room sits in the center, surrounded by its mirror copies. Each colored dot is an image source shaded by reflection order, and one second-order path starts out highlighted: the dashed line runs straight from the virtual source to the head, while the solid orange line folds that same segment back inside the real walls to show the actual bounces. You can drag either the source or the listener, click any dot or echogram bar to inspect its path, and hit clap to hear only these discrete reflections with no reverb tail added.

Slide reflection order down to 0 for a completely dry clap, then step up to 1 to hear the four distinct slaps off the walls, the kind of tight flutter you notice clapping in a small bare bedroom. Around order 6 the echoes start fusing into a metallic buzz, and by order 12 the room begins to feel recognizable. Counting individual images gets out of hand quickly, though. In this flat 2D slice the count grows with the square of elapsed time; in a real 3D room with floor and ceiling included, the number of image sources within reach after t seconds scales like 4π(ct)³ / 3V, meaning reflections arrive at a rate of roughly 4πc³t² / V per second. In the 336 m³ hall at the top of the page, that rate passes 4,000 arrivals a second after only 50 milliseconds. Tracking individual echoes past that point stops paying off, so the model switches from geometry to statistics.

Step 2 · The tailSabine's stopwatch

In 1895, Harvard's president asked Wallace Clement Sabine, a 27-year-old physics instructor, to rescue the lecture hall in the new Fogg Art Museum, where reverberation turned every speaker into mud. Rather than guessing, Sabine built a measurement setup: he blew an organ pipe tuned to 512 Hz, cut the air off, and used a chronograph to time how long the dying tone stayed audible. Late at night, once the streetcars and traffic outside had gone quiet, he hauled seat cushions across from Sanders Theatre, laid them out a few at a time, and timed the decay again. When he plotted the results, the decay time multiplied by total absorption (the bare room plus whatever cushions were on the floor) came out to a straight constant.

Out of those late nights came a surprisingly simple law. Given a room's volume V in cubic meters and its total absorption A = Σ S·α in square meters (each surface's area multiplied by its absorption coefficient), the reverberation time is 

T = 0.161 · V / A   // seconds, to fall by 60 dB

Sabine published the formula in 1900, the same year Boston's Symphony Hall opened under his acoustic supervision. Modern acoustics defines reverberation time, T60, as the time it takes sound energy to drop by 60 dB, down to one-millionth of its starting level. In 1930 Carl Eyring refined the math for heavily damped spaces: if you cover every surface with a perfect absorber (ᾱ = 1), Sabine's equation still predicts a small nonzero echo, whereas replacing A with −S·ln(1 − ᾱ) sends the decay cleanly to zero. The two curves sit nearly on top of each other in live rooms and split apart once a room gets very absorbent.

Completely empty, the 1,200 m³ hall rings for 6.7 seconds at 500 Hz. Putting people across just a quarter of the floor cuts that in half to 3.2 seconds, and a full house pulls it all the way down to 1.3 seconds by Sabine (1.1 by Eyring). People in clothes soak up far more sound than plaster and hardwood, which is why an orchestra hall sounds so bright and boomy during an empty afternoon rehearsal and settles down the moment the seats fill.

To synthesize the late tail without summing millions of image sources, the code finds the mixing time where arrival density hits 4,000 reflections per second (clamped between 45 and 250 ms) and hands off to decaying noise. It generates overlapping frames of 1,024 samples with random phases, matches each frequency band to the energy level the early reflections had reached by then, and lets every frequency decay at its own rate of 6.91 / T nepers per second in amplitude, which drops 60 dB in exactly T seconds. A cosine crossfade over the final 15% of the mixing time stitches the two halves together.

Step 3 · ColorSix bands, six clocks

Real materials rarely absorb low and high frequencies alike, so a single decay time only tells part of the story. Plush carpet barely registers a 125 Hz bass wave (absorbing 2%) while soaking up 65% of the energy at 4 kHz. Drywall nailed over wooden studs does the exact opposite: the panel flexes like a drumhead at low frequencies, pulling 29% out at 125 Hz while reflecting 96% at 1 kHz. Glazed tile bounces back 99% across most of the spectrum. In huge rooms, the air itself starts stealing high frequencies along long travel paths. Every room effectively runs six separate decay clocks at once, and the tilt between bass and treble is what makes a carpeted living room sound warm and muffled while a tiled stairwell rings harshly.

Below left, you can watch each band die out inside the impulse response; below right, the bars show Eyring's predicted T60 while the white horizontal ticks show the T30 measured back out of the finished audio between −5 and −35 dB. Since the tail is built directly from those Eyring times, the ticks should hug the tops of the bars as a sanity check that no filter or crossfade skewed the result.

Because every preset here is an empty rectangular box with no chairs, curtains, or open doorways, the decay times run longer than a furnished real-world room, while keeping the right spectral shape. In the tiled bathroom, 1 kHz rings for 3.8 seconds while 125 Hz drops to 1.1 seconds because the drywall ceiling drains the bass. The living room holds 500 Hz for 1.3 seconds and loses 4 kHz in 0.37. The vocal booth stays under a tenth of a second everywhere. The 42 × 26 × 18 meter concert hall, with a full audience across the floor, sits steadily around 1.9 seconds from 125 Hz through 2 kHz before rolling down to 1.5 seconds at 4 kHz, right in the classic orchestral sweet spot. Step 4 · BassThe room's own notes

Down in the bass, picturing sound as neat billiard-ball rays stops working. A 50 Hz wave is nearly 6.9 meters long, bigger than most bedrooms, so the wave fills the whole volume at once and the room starts behaving like a boxy organ pipe. Whenever a whole number of half-wavelengths fits cleanly between opposing walls along any combination of length, width, and height, the room rings at a natural resonance called a mode: 

f = (c / 2) · √( (nx/L)² + (ny/W)² + (nz/H)² )   // nx, ny, nz = 0, 1, 2, …

Modes with a single nonzero index bounce straight between two parallel walls (axial); two nonzero indices graze four surfaces (tangential); three involve all six surfaces (oblique). In the 5.2 × 3.8 × 2.6 meter drywall-and-wood room below, the lowest modes land at 33.0 Hz (1,0,0), 45.1 Hz (0,1,0), 55.9 Hz (1,1,0), and a doubled pair right at 66.0 Hz, because the 5.2 meter length is exactly twice the 2.6 meter ceiling height. Below 200 Hz, 67 modes pack into that small space.

Every mode sets up a fixed standing wave of loud pressure peaks and dead quiet valleys, so walking two steps across a room completely changes how much bass you hear. In the 1950s, Manfred Schroeder worked out the transition point above which individual modes overlap so densely that the room's response turns into a statistical jumble: roughly 2000 √(T / V) Hz. For this room, that crossover sits at 158 Hz. Because the noise-tail impulse responses in the earlier figures smooth over individual low-end resonances, this demo evaluates the modal sum directly, damping every mode using the room's 125 Hz decay time (0.32 s).

The color map plots the loudness of a steady bass tone at ear height (1.2 m) across the floor plan, spanning 30 dB from dark blue valleys to bright amber peaks. Underneath, the curve traces the frequency response at the listener's head, with vertical blue lines marking the axial modes. Put on headphones (laptop speakers roll off well above these frequencies), keep the tone near 64 Hz, and drag the head across the dark vertical valley to hear the volume plunge by nearly 20 dB within a single meter.

Notice the speaker starts tucked in the top-left corner. Because every rectangular mode has a cosine pressure maximum at the walls, every single mode peaks in all eight corners. Parking a speaker there drives the entire set at once, which is why shoving a subwoofer against a corner makes the bass swell and boom. Hit ▶ sweep 25–200 Hz and the room turns a perfectly flat test tone into a rollercoaster of swells and deep notches whenever the sweep crosses a silent node at your spot. Studio engineers obsess over listening position and thick corner bass traps specifically to tame those dips and peaks.

Step 5 · DirectionTwo ears

Every room model up to now has assumed a single microphone floating in space. Human hearing runs on two ears roughly 17.5 cm apart with a solid skull between them, and back in 1907 Lord Rayleigh figured out that listeners rely on two distinct physical cues to place a sound, a framework still called the duplex theory. Low frequencies have wavelengths far longer than a head, so they bend right around it; what tells your brain left from right is the tiny head start the closer ear gets. High frequencies have short wavelengths that the head physically blocks, casting an acoustic shadow that makes the far ear noticeably quieter.

Modeling the head as a sphere of radius a, a wave reaching the far ear travels a straight tangent segment and then creeps along the curved surface to the ear canal. That geometry gives Robert Woodworth's time-difference formula, (a / c)(θ + sin θ) for a source at angle θ off center. With a = 8.75 cm, the delay tops out at 656 µs for a sound directly to one side. To approximate the acoustic shadow, the code uses Richard Brown and Richard Duda's 1998 one-pole, one-zero spherical-head model: high frequencies pick up to a +6 dB boost on the side facing the source and lose up to 20 dB in the shadow behind the head. In the live demo below, each ear runs through a short delay and a high-shelf filter driven directly by those equations.

On headphones, the source orbits once every 14 seconds until you grab it or tap somewhere else on the circle. Switch cues to timing only and the treble stays equally bright in both ears; switch to level only with voice selected and the low vocal tones barely budge, because the head shadow really kicks in above 1 kHz. Turn on both and the sound steps outside the center of your skull to sit cleanly off to one side, far more convincingly than plain pan, which just nudges overall channel gain equally at all frequencies with zero delay. Keep an eye on the dashed circle marked same cues: a symmetrical sphere produces identical timing and shadow for a sound in front and its mirror image behind, and gives no clue about elevation. Real ears break that tie using the tiny ridges and folds of the outer ear (captured in a head-related transfer function) along with subtle unconscious turns of the head.

The impulse responses throughout this page put that same two-eared head inside every room. Each early image source arrives from its own 3D direction and gets its own interaural delay and head-shadow filter, while the late noise tail is synthesized with independent random noise for the left and right channels so the reverberation wraps all the way around you. Put headphones on and clap in the hall at the top of the page to hear that spatial envelope kick in.

Step 6 · FeedbackI am sitting in a room

In 1969, composer Alvin Lucier recorded himself reading a short paragraph aloud, played that tape back into the same room while recording it onto a second machine, and repeated the cycle over and over. Each round trip stamps the room's acoustic signature on top of the last. After a couple of dozen passes, the consonants and vowels dissolve completely, leaving behind a ghostly chord of steady pitches picked out by the room itself.

In frequency space, playing audio through a room multiplies its spectrum by the room's transfer function H(f). Repeating the loop n times scales the spectrum by |H|n, so even modest peaks pull exponentially ahead of everything else. The demo below runs 32 generations of FFT convolution at 24 kHz, rolling off below 70 Hz and above 7 kHz on each pass to mimic a real loudspeaker and microphone. Since Lucier's original tape can't ship in the browser, the input is a seven-syllable vowel phrase from the same formant synthesizer used earlier. Each horizontal row of the waterfall displays one generation's spectrum from top (generation 0) down to generation 32.

At generation 0 you can spot the wide glowing formant bands of the synthesized vowels. By generation 8 they are already fracturing into thin vertical streaks, and by generation 16 almost nothing remains except a handful of razor-sharp tones, marked by the green triangles at the bottom and named in the readout. Click ▶ 0 → 1 → 2 → 4 → 8 → 16 → 32 to hear the voice melt straight into pure resonance. Notice that the surviving notes depend both on what energy the voice supplied in the first place and on where the speaker and microphone happen to sit. Above the Schroeder frequency, a room's frequency response is a jagged thicket of interference peaks that reshuffle if you shift a microphone by a few centimeters. That happens in physical rooms and in this simulation's random-phase tail alike, meaning the chord you uncover belongs just as much to the exact pair of positions as it does to the architecture.

EpilogueWhat a room does

Put those pieces side by side and a surprisingly small toolkit covers most of how indoor spaces sound. Mirroring a box turns early reflections into plain distance and wall-hit bookkeeping; once arrivals cross a few thousand per second, Sabine and Eyring's absorption math lets a six-band decaying noise tail take over without giving the handoff away. Down below the Schroeder frequency, ray geometry gives way to standing waves where standing a meter to the left matters more than repainting the walls, and running every path through Woodworth's delay and a spherical head shadow finally pulls the sound out of the middle of your headphones and into a room around you.

Plenty of real-world acoustics still sits outside this box model. L-shaped rooms, domes, and sloped auditorium ceilings break clean mirror tiling and call for ray tracing or full wave solvers; rough plaster and bookshelves scatter sound sideways instead of bouncing it like glass; measured head-related transfer functions would separate front from back and add overhead height; and a truly unified solver would stitch a modal bass engine cleanly onto the noise tail right around the Schroeder frequency.
