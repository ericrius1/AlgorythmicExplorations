Acoustic Spaces · Part 1

Rooms That Play Back

A room answers every sound with thousands of echoes, and you can work them out. Tap the plan above to clap in an empty hall, and hear it from where the head is standing.

Living Music was about notes up to the moment they leave the instrument. This series is about what happens next. Before a sound reaches you it has hit the walls, the floor, the ceiling and the furniture, and each of those sends back a copy that's a little later, a little quieter and a little duller. The copies arrive by the thousand. You don't hear them as echoes. You hear them as the room.

Everything on this page runs on one model: a rectangular room with six surfaces, each made of a material whose absorption is known in six octave bands, from 125 Hz to 4 kHz. From that the page builds an impulse response, the room's complete answer to a single click, and then plays sounds through it by convolution. The plan at the top is an empty hall, 12 × 7 × 4 metres, with a brick wall, a glass wall, wood panels and plaster. The ripple is a separate wave simulation, slowed down 65 times so you can watch the sound spread and bounce. The dashed lines light up as the slowed wavefront reaches the head along the direct path and the four first reflections off the walls. What you hear is the impulse response, at the real speed of sound. Drag the head somewhere else and clap again.

Step 1 · ReflectionsMirror rooms

A flat wall reflects sound the way a mirror reflects light: the angle going in equals the angle coming out. That means the reflected sound reaches you as if it came from a copy of the source standing behind the wall, as far behind it as the real source is in front. Mirror the copy in a second wall and you get the path that bounces twice. Keep going and the whole plane fills with mirrored rooms, each holding one image source, and every image is one path from the source to you.

Each image needs only two numbers to become sound: how far it is, and which walls it bounced off on the way. Distance gives the delay (343 metres per second) and the spreading loss (pressure falls as one over distance). Each bounce multiplies the pressure by √(1 − α), where α is the fraction of energy the wall absorbs, per octave band. Jont Allen and David Berkley wrote this down for rectangular rooms in 1979, and it's still how most room simulators start. Here is the gain of one image in each band, from room.ts: 

// beta = √(1 − α) per surface and band; hits = bounces off each of the six surfaces
const gain = BANDS.map((_, b) => {
  let g = 1 / dist;
  for (let i = 0; i < 6; i++) if (hits[i]) g *= Math.pow(beta[i][b], hits[i]);
  return g * Math.exp(-0.5 * AIR_M[b] * dist); // and the air takes a little
});

The figure is a 6 × 4 metre room seen from above. To keep it flat I made the floor and ceiling fully absorbing, so only the four walls reflect. The real room is in the middle; the rest are its mirror images, with one dot per image source, coloured by how many bounces it stands for. One second-order path is selected to start with: the dashed line runs straight from its image to the listener, and the solid line is the same path folded back into the real room. Tap any dot, or any bar in the echogram underneath, to see its path. The clap plays only these paths, with no reverb added.

At order 0 you hear the dry clap. At order 1 you hear four distinct slaps, which is what a small hard room sounds like if you listen for it. By order 6 or so they run together into a short buzz, and by 12 it starts to sound like a space. The count grows fast. In this flat room it grows with the square of the time; in a real 3D room the number of images within reach after t seconds is about 4π(ct)³ / 3V, so they arrive at a rate of 4πc³t² / V per second. In the hall at the top (336 m³) that passes 4,000 a second after about 50 milliseconds. Beyond that, counting stops being useful, and the next step replaces the count with a statistic.

Step 2 · The tailSabine's stopwatch

In 1895 Harvard's president asked Wallace Clement Sabine, a 27-year-old physics instructor, to do something about the lecture room in the new Fogg Art Museum, where nobody could understand the speakers. Sabine decided to measure how long a sound lingered. He sounded an organ pipe at 512 Hz, cut it off, and timed with a chronograph how long it took to become inaudible. Then he borrowed the seat cushions from Sanders Theatre, laid them out in the room a few at a time, and timed it again, working at night when the street was quiet. The time multiplied by the total absorption, the room's own plus the cushions', came out constant.

That gave him a formula. With the room's volume V in cubic metres and its total absorption A = Σ S·α in square metres (each surface's area times its absorption coefficient), the reverberation time is 

T = 0.161 · V / A   // seconds, to fall by 60 dB

He published it in 1900, the year Boston's Symphony Hall opened with Sabine as its acoustician. Today the reverberation time is defined as the time for the sound to fall by 60 dB, to a millionth of its energy. In 1930 Carl Eyring pointed out that Sabine's formula overestimates the time in very dead rooms (cover every surface with something that absorbs everything and Sabine still predicts a small echo), and gave a version with −S·ln(1 − ᾱ) in place of A. The two agree when the room is live and part ways when it isn't.

The figure is a plaster hall with the proportions of a long lecture room, 2 : 1.4 : 1. Change its volume, from 60 to 20,000 m³, and cover its floor with a seated audience. The left panel is both formulas against coverage. The right panel is the decay that actually comes out of the impulse response the figure builds, measured the way acousticians do it, by integrating the energy backwards from the end (Manfred Schroeder's method, from 1965). Press the organ pipe to hear Sabine's test: the stopwatch starts when the pipe stops.

Empty, the 1,200 m³ hall rings for 6.7 seconds at 500 Hz. A quarter of the floor full of people halves it, to 3.2, and a full floor brings it down to 1.3 seconds (1.1 by Eyring). That's the whole lesson Sabine took from the cushions: reverberation time follows the absorption you put in the room, and audiences are most of it. It's also why a hall sounds different in rehearsal and in concert.

The tail in the impulse response is not a sum of image sources. Once the reflections are too dense to count (the mixing time, when they reach about 4,000 a second, kept between 45 and 250 ms), I switch to noise. The noise is made in frames of 1,024 samples with random phases. Each frequency starts at the level the image sources had reached by then and falls at its own band's rate: 6.9 / T nepers a second in amplitude, which is 60 dB in T seconds. The two parts overlap and crossfade over the last 15% of the mixing time.

Step 3 · ColourSix bands, six clocks

Sabine's formula gives one number, but rooms don't absorb all frequencies equally, so every band gets its own. Carpet hardly touches 125 Hz (it absorbs 2%) and soaks up 65% at 4 kHz. Drywall on studs does the opposite: the board flexes, and at 125 Hz it absorbs 29%. Glazed tile reflects 99% of everything. The air itself takes a share of the high frequencies over long distances, which matters in big halls. So a room has six clocks running at once, and the balance between them is most of its character: a carpeted room with drywall sounds boomy and dull; a tiled one sounds bright.

Pick a room, or set the walls, floor and ceiling yourself. The top row is the absorption of the three surfaces. Below left, each band's decay in the room's impulse response; below right, the reverberation time per band, from Eyring's formula (bars) and measured from the response between −5 and −35 dB (white ticks, the standard T30 measurement). They should agree, since the tail is built from those times. The check is that nothing between the formula and the speaker, no filter and no join, bends it.

Every room here is empty and a perfect box, so the times run long next to real rooms with furniture, doors and people in them. The shapes are right, though. The tiled bathroom rings for 3.8 seconds at 1 kHz but only 1.1 at 125 Hz, because the drywall ceiling eats the bass. The empty living room holds 500 Hz for 1.3 seconds and loses 4 kHz in 0.37. The vocal booth is under a tenth of a second everywhere. The concert hall, 42 × 26 × 18 metres with its floor full of people, stays close to 1.9 seconds from 125 Hz to 2 kHz and drops to 1.5 at 4 kHz, which is about what good halls are built for. The church holds the middle for 13 seconds. Try the voice in the booth and then in the church. The words are hard to follow in the church because each syllable is still sounding when the next one arrives.

Step 4 · BassThe room's own notes

At low frequencies the picture of rays bouncing off walls breaks down. A 50 Hz wave is 6.9 metres long, bigger than most rooms, and the room behaves like a box-shaped organ pipe. It has resonances, called modes, at the frequencies where a whole number of half-wavelengths fits between the walls in each direction: 

f = (c / 2) · √( (nx/L)² + (ny/W)² + (nz/H)² )   // nx, ny, nz = 0, 1, 2, …

A mode with one nonzero index bounces between two opposite walls (axial); two indices means it goes around four walls (tangential); three means all six (oblique). The room in the figure is 5.2 × 3.8 × 2.6 metres, with drywall walls and ceiling and a wood floor. Its lowest modes are 33.0 Hz (1,0,0), 45.1 Hz (0,1,0), 55.9 Hz (1,1,0) and a pair at 66.0 Hz, one along the length and one between floor and ceiling. Below 200 Hz there are 67 of them.

Each mode is a standing wave with fixed loud spots and silent lines, so the bass you hear depends on where you stand. Manfred Schroeder worked out in the 1950s where this stops mattering: above roughly 2000 √(T / V) Hz the modes overlap so much that the response turns into a dense, random-looking jumble, and the statistical picture from Step 2 takes over. For this room that's 158 Hz. The impulse responses on this page don't model what happens below it, since their tail is noise, so this figure computes the modes directly: a sum over all of them, each damped at the room's 125 Hz reverberation time (0.32 s).

The map shows how loud a bass tone from the speaker in the corner is at every point at ear height (1.2 m). The curve below is the response where the head is standing. Drag the head along the room at 64 Hz and the level changes by almost 20 dB within a metre of the dark line. You'll need headphones: laptop speakers can't play this low.

The speaker sits in a corner on purpose. Every mode has a pressure maximum in every corner, so a speaker there drives all of them, which is also why bass gets boomy when you push a subwoofer into one. The deep notches in the curve are frequencies where the listener is on a silent line of the strongest nearby mode. Sweep the note and you hear the room's bass as a series of swells and holes that have nothing to do with the music. Mixing engineers sit in a measured spot for this reason, and treat the room with bass traps, which are absorbers thick enough to work at these wavelengths.

Step 5 · DirectionTwo ears

So far the room has had one listener with one ear. You have two, about 17.5 cm apart with a head in between, and that's how you know where a sound comes from. In 1907 Lord Rayleigh described two separate cues, an idea now called the duplex theory. At low frequencies the wavelength is longer than the head, the sound bends around it easily, and what differs between the ears is the arrival time. At high frequencies the head casts an acoustic shadow, and what differs is the level.

For a round head of radius a the extra distance to the far ear is the straight part plus the arc around the head, which gives Robert Woodworth's formula for the time difference: (a / c)(θ + sin θ) for a source at angle θ off centre. With a = 8.75 cm it peaks at 656 µs for a sound straight to one side. For the shadow I used Richard Brown and Richard Duda's spherical-head filter from 1998: one pole and one zero per ear, with the high frequencies boosted by up to 6 dB on the side facing the sound and cut by up to 20 dB in the shadow. In the live figure each ear gets a delay and a high-shelf filter set from those formulas.

The sound circles once every 14 seconds until you drag it. With timing only, the high frequencies are equally loud in both ears. With level only, nothing is delayed, and the low voice barely moves, because the shadow mostly acts above 1 kHz. With both, the sound should leave the middle of your head and sit off to the side. The plain pan only changes the balance between the channels, the same at every frequency, with no delay. Watch the dashed circle marked "same cues". A sphere gives identical timing and shadow for a sound in front and its mirror image behind, so this model can't tell them apart, and it can't hear height at all. Real ears separate them with the folds of the outer ear, which filter the sound differently from each direction (measured as a head-related transfer function), and with small head movements.

The impulse responses on this page use the same head. Every image source arrives from its own direction and gets its own time difference and shadow filter for each ear, and the late tail uses a separate noise for each ear, so the reverb surrounds you instead of sitting in the middle. That's why the hero sounds like standing in the hall, if you have headphones on.

Step 6 · FeedbackI am sitting in a room

In 1969 the composer Alvin Lucier recorded himself reading a short text, played the recording back into the same room and recorded it again, and kept going. Each pass adds the room's response once more, and after a few dozen passes the words are gone and what's left is a set of pitches: the frequencies the room favours. The text he reads describes the process. It turns out to be easy to compute.

Playing a sound into a room multiplies its spectrum by the room's frequency response H(f). Doing it n times multiplies it by |H|n, and the tallest peaks of H win by more each time. The figure does this 32 times by convolution, at 24 kHz to save work, rolling off below 70 Hz and above 7 kHz at each pass the way a loudspeaker and microphone would. The voice is the vowel synthesizer from the other figures, seven syllables with no consonants, since I don't have Lucier's recording to use. Each row of the waterfall is one generation's spectrum.

The formants of the voice are the broad bright bands at the top. By generation 8 they are breaking into narrow lines, and by 16 only the lines are left. The readout names the pitches that survive in each room. Which pitches they are depends on what was in the voice to begin with, since a room can only boost what it's given, and on where the speaker and the microphone stand. Above the Schroeder frequency the response of a room is a tangle of peaks whose positions change when you move a few centimetres. That's true of real rooms, and here it's true of the random tail, so the room's "notes" belong to the positions as much as to the room.

EpilogueWhat a room does

A few lessons from this part:

  • Reflections are mirrored sources. One rule, reflect the room, turns echoes into geometry: a delay, a distance and a list of walls.
  • After a few dozen milliseconds, count statistically. The images arrive too fast to matter one at a time, and a noise tail with the right decay per band sounds the same.
  • Absorption sets the time. Sabine's formula is still the first estimate for a live room, and Eyring's for a dead one.
  • Bass is a different problem. Below the Schroeder frequency the room is a resonator, and where you stand matters more than what the walls are made of.
  • Two ears make it a place. A time difference and a filter per ear move the room from between your ears to around you.

Things I'd try next: rooms that aren't boxes, which need ray tracing or a wave solver instead of mirrors; scattering, since real walls aren't perfect mirrors and a rough surface spreads its reflection out; measured head-related transfer functions instead of a sphere, to get front, back and height; and a low-frequency part built from the modes, joined to the noise tail at the Schroeder frequency.
