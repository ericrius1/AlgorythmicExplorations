Acoustic Spaces · Part 2

The Room Instrument

A room that writes its own music. The echoes of a drum set the rhythm, the walls set the accents, and you hear every note from where the head is standing.

The plan at the top is an instrument. Tap it to start. The orange ring is a drum that sends a pulse into the room once a bar. The pulse spreads, bounces off the walls, and every time a wavefront passes one of the coloured stones, that stone rings. Higher up the plan means a higher note. Drag the stones, the drum or the head. Tap the floor to add a stone (up to seven), and drag one out through a wall to take it away. Tap a wall to change what it's made of: tile, brick, wood, glass, curtains, acoustic tiles. When you have something you like, copy the link. The whole room is in it.

Part one built the pieces: image sources for the reflections, Sabine's reverberation per octave band, and a two-eared head. Here they run as one system, and the room stops being the place music is played in and starts deciding what gets played.

Step 1 · RhythmThe room is the sequencer

Every path from the drum to a stone is an image source, the same as in part one: the direct path, one for each wall, and (with "up to second" on) one for each pair of walls. Each path has a length and a loss. For the sequencer I give the pulse its own slow speed of sound, set so the room's diagonal takes 12 sixteenth notes whatever the size of the room. A path's length then becomes a time in steps, rounded to the nearest sixteenth and wrapped around the 16-step bar, so a path that takes 20 steps lands on step 4 of the next bar. Its loudness is the reflection loss at 1 kHz with a gentle fall-off with distance. Anything quieter than 0.12 is dropped. 

for (const im of ims) {
  const steps = im.dist / mPerStep();
  // reflection loss at 1 kHz (spreading removed), plus a gentle fall with distance
  const refl = im.gain[3] * im.dist;
  const vel = refl * Math.min(1, 1.3 / (1 + steps / 8));
  if (vel < 0.12) continue;
  const step = Math.round(steps) % STEPS;
  // …keep the loudest hit on each step
}

Here's a single stone in an 8 × 5 metre room with tiled walls, counting first reflections only. That makes five hits a bar at most: the direct path and one off each wall. The grid underneath is the bar the room wrote. Drag the stone and the hits slide around; put it near a wall and the direct hit and that wall's echo land close together. Then hang curtains.

With tile, each bounce keeps 99% of the energy, so the echoes are nearly as loud as the direct hit. Curtains absorb 72% at 1 kHz, which leaves about half the pressure after one bounce, and the echoes turn into ghost notes. With "up to second" on, eight more paths arrive, off two walls each, and the pattern gets busier. That's the design: hard rooms play dense patterns with even accents, soft rooms play sparse ones where the direct hit dominates.

I scaled the sequencer speed to the room because the real speed is useless for rhythm. At 343 metres per second a pulse crosses a living room in about 20 milliseconds, which would make every pattern a flam. Tying it to the diagonal means a bathroom and a church with the same proportions play the same rhythm, and what tells them apart is how they sound.

Step 2 · SoundTwo speeds of sound

The rings you see travel at the sequencer speed. What you hear travels at the real one. Each stone has its own convolver holding a binaural impulse response from the stone to the head, built the way part one built them: image sources up to the mixing time, each with its own delay and shadow for each ear, then a noise tail that decays at each band's reverberation time, with a separate noise per ear. A stone to your left sounds from the left, a far one sounds far, and a stone in the church rings for seconds.

Moving anything means new responses. A stone rebuilds only its own; the head, the room or a wall rebuilds all of them. The builds run at most one every 60 milliseconds so dragging stays smooth, and each new response fades in on a second convolver while the old one fades out, which takes under a tenth of a second and doesn't click. The notes are synthesized: a marimba-like bar with its fundamental and overtones at 4 and about 10 times the fundamental, each dying away faster than the last. The drum is a short thump whose pitch falls from about 140 Hz to 52 Hz. The stones' height on the plan picks a note from two octaves of the chosen scale, starting at A3.

The presets are worth a tour. The studio is mixed, with curtains and acoustic tiles on two walls, so its echoes come from one side. The bathroom rings in every direction. The concert hall is 40 metres long, so the first reflections reach the head tens of milliseconds after each note. In the stone church the notes pile up into a chord. The water tank is a 9-metre concrete cube with water for a floor, about as hard as a room gets.

Step 3 · BuildingBuild a room

Here is the instrument again with every control. Pick a room to start from, a scale, a tempo, how many bounces count, and what the floor and ceiling are made of; tap the walls for the rest. Changing the preset keeps your stones, head and drum where they are, as fractions of the room, so you can hear the same layout in a bathroom and in a church. Whatever you build here is what the share link carries.

Step 4 · SharingA room in a link

The share link holds the whole state in the query string, with no server: the preset, the room's size to a tenth of a metre, the six materials as positions in the material table, the head and drum as percentages of the room, the scale, tempo and echo setting, then two numbers per stone. They're written in base 36 and joined with dots, which keeps a room with five stones to about 70 characters. The address bar is updated as you play, so copying it from there works as well as the button. Opening a link builds the room before the first note, and if the link is damaged you get the studio instead.

EpilogueRooms as instruments

A few lessons from this part:

  • One model, two uses. The same image sources give the rhythm (their lengths) and the sound (their filters).
  • Slow the clock, keep the physics. Rhythm needs a slowed pulse, but the echoes you hear stay at the real speed of sound.
  • Materials are dynamics. A wall's absorption becomes the accent of its echo.
  • Crossfade everything that moves. A new impulse response fades in on a second convolver, so dragging never clicks.

Things I'd try next: a third dimension for the sequencer, so the floor and ceiling join in; stones that are also sources, so a ringing stone sends its own pulse and the room starts to answer itself; moving stones, which would bring in Doppler shift; and rooms that aren't rectangles, where mirror images stop being exact and need ray tracing.

That's the end of Acoustic Spaces. It started with a clap in an empty hall, and the image-source method that turns echoes into geometry. Then Sabine's stopwatch and the noise tail it justifies, materials that colour each octave differently, the room's own notes below the Schroeder frequency, two ears and a head, and Lucier's room singing itself. This part puts all of it into one toy where the room writes the rhythm and plays it back to you.
