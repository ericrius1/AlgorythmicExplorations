Acoustic Spaces · Part 2

The Room Instrument

A room that writes its own music. A drum's reflections off the walls trigger the rhythm, the wall materials set the accents, and you hear every note from where the head stands.

In Part 1, a room was a passive filter: you clapped, and the walls, Sabine decay tail, and spherical head colored what came back. Put those exact same calculations in charge of timing, and the architecture itself turns into a sequencer. Tap the floor plan above to get it running. Once every bar, the orange drum fires a circular pulse outward; as that wavefront and its wall reflections sweep across the colored stones, each stone rings out, pitched higher the closer it sits to the top wall. Drag any stone, the drum, or the listener's head to reshape the pattern in real time. Tap empty floor to drop in more stones (up to seven), fling one out past a wall to remove it, and tap any wall to cycle through glazed tile, brick, wood panels, window glass, heavy curtains, and acoustic tiles. Whenever you land on a groove you want to keep, click copy share link to pack the entire room into a URL.

Step 1 · RhythmThe room is the sequencer

Underneath, every path from the drum to a stone comes straight out of the Part 1 image-source solver: the direct line across the floor, one first-order bounce off each wall, and (when up to second is enabled) eight double-bounce paths off pairs of walls. To turn spatial distances into a beat you can nod along to, the sequencer runs on its own slowed-down speed of sound scaled so the room's diagonal always spans exactly 12 sixteenth notes, regardless of how many meters wide the space actually is. Each path length divides into steps, rounds to the nearest sixteenth, and wraps modulo 16 so a 20-step bounce lands neatly on step 4 of the next bar. Its velocity comes from the wall reflection gain at 1 kHz (with geometric spreading stripped back out) times a mild distance taper, dropping any hit quieter than 0.18: 

for (const im of ims) {
  const steps = im.dist / mPerStep();
  // reflection loss at 1 kHz (spreading removed), plus a gentle fall with distance
  const refl = im.gain[3] * im.dist;
  const vel = refl * Math.min(1, 1.3 / (1 + steps / 8));
  if (vel < 0.18) continue;
  const step = Math.round(steps) % STEPS;
  // …keep the loudest hit on each step
}

To see a single voice in isolation, the figure below drops one stone into an 8 × 5 meter tiled room with first only reflections active. That leaves at most five hits per bar: the direct strike plus one echo off each of the four walls. The step strip underneath draws the bar the geometry produces. Drag the stone near a wall and watch that wall's echo slide right up beside the direct hit, then flip walls from tile to curtains.

Glazed tile reflects 99% of the energy at 1 kHz, so every bounce arrives almost as loud as the direct pulse. Heavy curtains absorb 72% at the same band, cutting the pressure roughly in half on a single hit and turning the four reflections into soft ghost notes behind the main strike. Switching echoes to up to second brings in eight more paths off two walls apiece, filling out the bar when the walls are hard while barely showing up once curtains are hung. Hard rooms naturally generate dense, even-accented patterns; soft rooms pare them back until the direct pulse carries the beat.

Scaling the sequencer clock to the room diagonal solves an obvious headache with physical sound speed: at 343 meters per second, a pulse crosses a living room in roughly 20 milliseconds, collapsing every bounce into an unreadable flam. Tying step length to the diagonal guarantees that a tiny bathroom and a vast church with matching proportions trigger the exact same rhythm, letting the reverberation tail and wall materials supply the contrast between them.

Step 2 · SoundTwo speeds of sound

While the expanding rings on screen creep along at the musical tempo, the sound coming out of your speakers obeys real physics at 343 meters per second. Every stone owns its own dedicated convolver loaded with a binaural impulse response from that stone's coordinates to the listener's head, computed the same way as in Part 1: early image sources up to the mixing time, each delayed and shadowed separately for the left and right ears, followed by an independent six-band decaying noise tail per ear. Place a stone on your left and it plays from the left; drag it toward the back of a church and it washes out into a distant, seconds-long ring.

Any change in layout invalidates those filters. Dragging a stone only queues a rebuild for that single voice, while moving the head, changing the room size, or tapping a wall marks the whole set dirty. The animation loop rebuilds at most one impulse response every 60 milliseconds so pointer movement stays fluid, and every fresh response crossfades in over 80 milliseconds on a paired convolver while the previous one fades out, avoiding clicks mid-bar. The notes themselves are synthesized directly into short buffers: a marimba-like bar combining a fundamental with overtones at 4× and roughly 10× that decay progressively faster, paired with a drum thump whose pitch drops from about 142 Hz down to 52 Hz. Each stone's vertical position selects a pitch from two octaves of the active scale starting on A3.

Each preset highlights a distinct acoustic setup. The studio pairs wood panels on two walls with heavy curtains and acoustic tiles on the others, making reflections arrive mostly from one side. The bathroom wraps four glazed-tile walls around a small footprint that rings sharply in every direction. The concert hall stretches 40 meters long, so first reflections trail tens of milliseconds behind each strike. In the stone church, successive notes smear together into slow-moving chords, and the water tank puts you inside a 9-meter concrete cube with water across the floor, about as reflective as an enclosed volume gets.

Step 4 · SharingA room in a link

Sharing needs no database or backend because the entire instrument state packs straight into the URL's query string: the preset index, room length, width, and height in tenths of a meter, indices for all six surface materials, the head and drum coordinates as percentages of the room, the scale, tempo, and echo depth, followed by two coordinates for each stone. Converting those integers to base 36 and joining them with dots keeps a five-stone room around 70 characters long. The page keeps the browser address bar synced as you edit, so copying the URL directly works just as well as pressing copy share link. Opening a shared link restores the whole layout before the first beat drops, falling back safely to the studio preset if anything in the string is garbled.

EpilogueRooms as instruments

The biggest surprise in building this was how naturally one small geometry engine carried both halves of the instrument. The exact same image-source paths that determine the binaural echoes in your headphones also lay down the sixteenth-note groove once you slow their clock to fit a bar; surface absorption coefficients double as musical dynamics, turning tile into bright accents and curtains into ghost notes; and crossfading paired convolvers lets you drag walls and stones around live without hearing the audio engine tear.

There are plenty of ways to push the toy further. Bringing the floor and ceiling into the sequencer would turn the flat grid into a 3D rhythm box; letting each ringing stone fire a secondary pulse of its own would set up chains where the room keeps answering itself; putting stones in motion would bring in real Doppler pitch bends; and swapping the shoebox mirror solver for 2D ray tracing would open up crooked walls, hallways, and courtyards.
